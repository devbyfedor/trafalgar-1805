### Boulogne and the invasion army

- **The camps.** Napoleon ordered six camps on 14 June 1803; Desbrière calls that order the origin of the Grande Armée (Desbrière, *Projets* III 184). By 1805 the army was the armée des côtes de l'Océan, set out in Napoleon's note of 15 May 1805 and the war minister's letter of 30 May: Lannes's advance guard (Oudinot's grenadiers), Davout's corps (the camp of Bruges), Soult's (the camp of Saint-Omer, at Boulogne), Ney's (the camp of Montreuil), corps at Utrecht and Brest, a reserve and the Guard (*Projets* IV 459–461). English writers called it the Army of England. High.
- **The ports.** Seven: Étaples, Boulogne, Wimereux, Ambleteuse, Calais, Dunkirk and Ostend; Wimereux and Ambleteuse were dug in 1803–04 (James III 217; Corr. IX No. 7832). Ney's corps lay at Étaples and Montreuil, Soult's two camps either side of Boulogne, Lannes's grenadiers at Wimereux, Davout's corps at Ambleteuse and the reserve at Calais (James III 325–326). High.
- **Boulogne.** The right camp stood on the cliff plateau north of the Liane by the Tour d'Ordre, where Napoleon had his hut; the left camp on the plateau south of the river; headquarters inland at Pont-de-Briques (Corr. IX 77, 488; XI No. 9137). High; medium for the left camp's exact site (Outreau rests on Wikipedia only).
- **Huts, not tents.** From the autumn of 1803 the troops lived in wooden huts (*baraques*) built for the winter, in rows by regiment: one division of Soult's corps had 748 of them in May–June 1804 (*Projets* III 206–207, 432–433). The huts excavated at Étaples had tiled roofs and glazed windows (Fondation Napoléon, newsletter 410). Tents were campaign kit, sent from Metz to Strasbourg in August 1805 (Corr. XI No. 9137). High. The scene draws rows of small huts along camp streets.
- **The army.** The embarkation return of spring 1805 counted 147,662 men (142,601 combatants) and 15,764 horses: Montreuil 27,050, Saint-Omer (Soult, at Boulogne) 45,907, Bruges 33,012, the reserve 41,693. Its 2,054 craft had room for only 127,500 men and 9,975 horses (*Projets* IV 433). Napoleon's own round figure was 150,000 men and 10,000 horses (Corr. XI No. 9209). High. The modern figures of about 60,000 at Boulogne and over 175,000 in the camps have no source and are not used (`research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R12–R13).
- **The flotilla.** The count of 20 July 1805 found 2,293 craft in the seven ports (1,339 armed, 954 transports); at Boulogne alone 578 prames and gun craft and 526 transports, 1,104 in all (James III 217, 325; Clowes V 62). Medium: other counts are right for their own dates (1,301 built in 1803–04, 2,054 in the spring of 1805; `research/scene-02/h2-boulogne-cadiz.md`, H2-05).
- **The craft.** Prames: three-masted and ship-rigged, 110 French feet, twelve 24-pounders. Chaloupes canonnières: brigs of 76–80 ft. Bateaux canonniers and péniches: luggers of 60 ft. Fishing boats and merchant craft served as transports (*Projets* III 90 and the plate facing it; James III 216–217, who has some gun-vessels as schooners and the péniches "chiefly as schuyts"). High for the designs, medium as built. The flotilla is drawn as rigged gun craft, not barges.
- **Basin and road.** Boulogne's harbour was the Liane estuary, nearly dry at low water, given quays, a dug basin and a dam to keep the craft afloat, with a line of heavy gun-vessels moored across the road (James III 217). In July 1804, 45 brigs and 43 luggers lay at anchor in the road; in October 1804 about 150 craft lay in a double line outside the pier (III 227, 232). After he left, Napoleon meant to keep gun-vessels "in the basin and road" (III 332). High for 1804; medium for August 1805.
- **What the crossing needed.** Command of the Strait (*détroit*, Pas-de-Calais) for a short time, and the span changed often: eight hours of night (November 1803), six hours (2 July 1804; April and June 1805), four or five days (16 July 1805), three days (20 and 26 July, 13 August), twenty-four hours (13 and 22 August) and, looking back, fifteen days (13 September) (Corr. IX–XI; `research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R10). High. Beat 1's "command of the Channel" stands. The six-hours line is No. 7832 (2 July 1804); the Tower of London sentence is genuine but from a letter to Brune of 27 July 1804 (No. 7874), and popular versions splice the two. High.
- **Napoleon at Boulogne.** From 3 August 1805 (his datelines; James III 326; Corbett 1910, p. 240). He had the army embarked twice in his presence. High.

### The turn east and Ulm

- **The decision, 22–26 August.** Word that Villeneuve had left Ferrol reached Boulogne on 22 August (Desbrière 1907, p. 110). On 23 August Napoleon told Talleyrand "Mon parti est pris", and had Marmont told that if the fleet were held at Cádiz he would put the crossing off "à une autre année" (Corr. XI Nos. 9117, 9120; No. 9120 is misprinted "23 mai"). Cavalry orders followed on the 24th, "Mon mouvement est commencé" on the 25th, and the general order of march on the 26th, when the Grande Armée was named; it was announced on the 29th (Nos. 9128, 9130, 9137; Alombert and Colin I 216–218). High.
- **Not because of Cádiz.** Napoleon knew Villeneuve was in Cádiz by 31 August at the latest, from a postscript dated 13 fructidor (Corr. XI 157). Corbett's 1 September (p. 278) reads the same postscript; Desbrière's 1–2 September (pp. 115–116) is the arrival of the full dispatches. All of it came after the orders of 23–26 August, and he had weighed the Cádiz case on 22–23 August. James (III 332) has the news arriving about four days after the 22nd, and the Daru story of the Emperor turning east in fury at it (James III, App. 28) does not fit the dates (`research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R9). High. Beat 3 may put Cádiz before the turn east, but must not make it the cause.
- **Put off, not abandoned.** He left nineteen battalions at Ambleteuse, Boulogne and Étaples, concentrated the flotilla at Boulogne and armed its seamen as infantry (No. 9137); on 13 September he planned to keep about 500 craft and 80,000 men there as a standing threat (No. 9209). High. "Gives up on England" (beat 3) is acceptable; "puts off" is nearer his words.
- **The army leaves.** Oudinot's grenadiers marched on 26 August. The first divisions of Davout's, Soult's and Ney's corps left on 28 August (10 fructidor), the second on 30 and the third on 31 August, the rest from 1 September; by Berthier's order nobody marched on the 29th, though Napoleon wrote on the 28th that the second divisions would go "demain" (No. 9137; Alombert and Colin I 337; No. 9155). Napoleon left Boulogne on 2 September and was at Montreuil that evening (No. 9177; Desbrière 1907, p. 116); James (III 332) has 4 September. High. Date line: 28 August 1805.
- **To the Rhine.** About 400 miles in 24 days' march by three roads (Maude 1912, p. 123). Soult's corps, from the Boulogne camps, took the centre road through Metz and Bitche (Alombert and Colin I 236, 414). The army crossed between 25 and 30 September: Lannes and Murat at Kehl on the 25th, Davout at Mannheim on 26–29, Soult at Rheinhausen by Speyer on 26–30, Ney between Pforz and Durlach on the 27th (Alombert and Colin II 39–42). Napoleon's 1st Bulletin rounds this to 25–26 September. High.
- **The Danube.** Crossed at Donauwörth on 7 October. Napoleon directed the crossing and slept in the town while his headquarters stayed at Nördlingen, which is why his papers of the 7th bear that name (Schuermans 1908, p. 180; Maude 1912, pp. 199–200). High for the date; medium for the hour (afternoon by Schuermans, morning by Maude).
- **The dashed line.** A composite, labelled as the army. Boulogne–Metz–Bitche–Speyer–Heilbronn–Nördlingen–Donauwörth was Soult's IV corps; from Donauwörth Soult turned south-east by Augsburg and Memmingen, and Ney's VI corps took Elchingen and Ulm (`research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R6). High. Dates beside it: Boulogne 28 August, the Rhine 25–30 September, the Danube 7 October, Ulm 20 October.
- **Ulm.** Mack's capitulation was signed on 17 October (at noon by Alombert and Colin; the Austrian eyewitness has Berthier come at 2 p.m.), the garrison to become prisoners unless relieved by 25 October. A convention of 19 October brought the march-out forward. On 20 October, from 2 p.m. (it had been announced for 3), the garrison marched out by the Frauentor and laid down its arms before Napoleon, who watched from a rise at the foot of the Michelsberg until about 7 p.m. (Alombert and Colin III/1 225–228; 7th and 8th Bulletins, Corr. XI 339–342). High. The scene uses 20 October; "lays down its arms" is exact, "surrenders" defensible. The 19 October of some modern summaries has no period support.
- **How many.** About 25,000: 25,365 prisoners (Alombert and Colin III/1 229; Maude 1912, p. 250), "vingt-trois mille" by the eyewitness. Napoleon's figures grew from 15,000 and 20,000 on the 17th to 27,000 with 18 generals in the 7th Bulletin (the source of the modern 27,000) and 30,000 in the 8th. Taken with them: 40 colours, 63 guns, 2 howitzers and 13,600 muskets (page image; the OCR's 43,600 is a misreading). Medium.

### Nelson off Cádiz

- **The passage.** Nelson left Merton at 10.30 p.m. on 13 September and sailed from St Helens at 8 a.m. on the 15th (Nicolas VII 33–36). Ajax and Thunderer joined off the Lizard on the morning of the 18th (Nelson to Marsden, VII 45; James and Beatty say off Plymouth). On 26 September he sent the Euryalus ahead to ask for no salute and no colours. High.
- **Joining.** He joined Collingwood at about 6 p.m. on Saturday 28 September, could not communicate until morning, and took command on the 29th, his forty-seventh birthday (Victory's log, Nicolas VII 53 n.; his letters, VII 60, 62–63; Duff, VII 70 n.; Codrington 1873, I 51). Nicolas heads both 28 and 29 September "Sunday", a slip. High. Date line: 28 September 1805.
- **Strength on arrival.** 29 of the line, counting Victory, Ajax and Thunderer: 23 in the main body and 6 inshore under Rear-Admiral Louis (Victory's log and signal log; Nelson to Acton, 30 September, VII 54; Collingwood's 26, named ship by ship in Desbrière 1907, pp. 143–145, plus Nelson's three). James (III 379) and Beatty (p. 3) give 27, with 5 inshore. High for 29 under his command; medium for all 29 in company (`research/scene-02/a-h2b.md`, H2-16).
- **Louis's detachment.** Ordered on 2 October to Gibraltar and Tetuan for water and stores, he sailed that evening with Queen 98, Canopus 80, Spencer 74, Tigre 74 and Zealous 74, the frigate Endymion and five merchantmen (Nelson to Marsden, VII 63; Francis Austen, Hubback 1906, p. 148). The log's "October 3rd, P.M." is the nautical day; Desbrière's 3 October reads it literally. Euryalus's news turned Louis back on the morning of the 3rd; he rejoined on the 4th (Beatty) or the 5th (Austen) and was sent off again, while Zealous and Endymion went on to Gibraltar. Medium-high; the return date is disputed.
- **The count through October.** 23 by 5 October (Nelson's own count); 24 on the 7th (Defiance); 26 on the 8th (Leviathan back from Gibraltar, and Royal Sovereign); 27 on the 10th (Belleisle); 28 on the forenoon of the 13th (Agamemnon); 27 that evening, when Calder went home in the Prince of Wales; 28 when Africa joined (13 or 14 October); 27 from the 17th, when Donegal went to Gibraltar for casks, to the battle (Nicolas VII 75–121; the diary, Beatty 1825, p. 97). James and Clowes give a peak of 29, which needs Africa to have joined before Calder sailed; no source shows that (`research/scene-02/a-h2b.md`, H2-19a). Medium-high; high for the 27.
- **At Trafalgar.** 27 of the line: 3 of 100 guns, 4 of 98, 1 of 80, 16 of 74 and 3 of 64; the frigates Euryalus, Naiad, Phoebe and Sirius; the schooner Pickle (joined 1 October) and the cutter Entreprenante (joined 4 October) (Nicolas VII 64, 74, 130). Louis's five and Donegal were away. In all, 34 different ships of the line served under Nelson off Cádiz. High.
- **Louis on 18–21 October.** He left Gibraltar eastward on the 18th with the Malta convoy, five of the line and three frigates (Austen, Hubback pp. 152–153), and was some 200 miles east of the Strait at dawn on the 20th (Corbett 1910, p. 335). Medium. He is outside the Gulf frame.
- **The station.** Nelson kept the body of the fleet "from sixteen to eighteen leagues West of Cadiz" (to Blackwood and Barham, 4–5 October; 15–20 leagues in his diary, 14 October), never north of the port's parallel, ready to crowd sail for Cape Spartel if the enemy came out (Nicolas VII 73–75, 111, 122). His reasons: not to be caught close in by a westerly gale and swept into the Strait, and to hide his strength and tempt the enemy out (to Barham, 5 October; Corbett 1910, pp. 326–327). High.
- **Out of sight.** From a watchtower about 45 m up (probably the Torre Tavira) a masthead shows to about 29 nm in clear air and a hull to about 18–19 nm, so the main body at 45–60 nm was invisible and the inner ships of the line at 21–30 nm were at the limit (computed; `research/scene-02/h2-boulogne-cadiz.md`, H2-24). Beurnonville wrote from Madrid on 20 October that Nelson's force was not in sight and kept only frigates on watch, which he put down to the weather (Desbrière 1907, annexes 70–71). Medium.
- **Blackwood's squadron.** The Euryalus and Hydra were off Cádiz from 29 September; Blackwood had "five Frigates, a Brig, and a Schooner" by 11 October (Nicolas VII 53, 111). On 19 October: Euryalus (Blackwood), Sirius, Phoebe and Naiad, the brig Weazle, sent that day to Gibraltar and Tetuan with the news, and the schooner Pickle, sent to Cape Spartel (NRS II 146). By day they kept 2–8 nm west or south-west of San Sebastián, at times much closer; at night a frigate might anchor off the port and the Pickle worked close inshore (Nicolas VII 76, 96). High.
- **The chain.** From 4 October Mars, Defence and Colossus (Duff's squadron, with Ajax at first) stood about four leagues east of the fleet, one pushed east toward Cádiz "as near as possible in the latitude", using distant signals and guns "by day or night" (Nicolas VII 70–73; `research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R1). From 14–15 October Defence and Agamemnon lay 7–10 leagues west of Cádiz and Mars and Colossus 5 leagues east of the fleet (VII 122). On 19 October the cordon was Colossus, Mars, Defence and Agamemnon (Beatty 1825, p. 7; Codrington 1873, I 57). Frigates at the Cádiz end, ships of the line beyond: beat 4's "chain of frigates" is wrong. High. How the signal ran is under "The signal chain, 19 to 21 October".

### Orders, Rosily and the sortie

- **The orders.** On 14 September Napoleon ordered Villeneuve to sail at the first chance with at least two and a half months' food, collect the Spanish squadron at Cartagena, land his troops at Naples, fight any enemy found in inferior force, and refit at Toulon (Corr. XI No. 9210). The orders reached Cádiz on 27 September by Decrès's report (Desbrière 1907, annexes 34) or on the 28th (Desbrière p. 136; James; Clowes). The troops embarked from 30 September. High; the day of arrival is disputed by one.
- **Rosily.** On 15 September Napoleon told Decrès that Villeneuve's "excessive pusillanimité" would keep him in port and that Rosily should replace him (No. 9220); Rosily's instructions are of 17 September (No. 9230; Desbrière's narrative says the 18th). Decrès's letter of 20 September superseding Villeneuve never reached him (Desbrière p. 142). High.
- **Rosily's journey.** Eleven days from Bayonne to Madrid, arriving in the night of 10–11 October (Beurnonville; Desbrière p. 128 has the 10th; Corbett's 12 October is the date of Rosily's own letter). With the posts broken down and robbers on the road beyond Córdoba he left Madrid only on the 14th, and reached Cádiz on 23–25 October: expected on the 23rd, his first letter from Cádiz dated the 25th (Desbrière 1907, annexes 68–73, 117–118; Fraser 1906, p. 379). Medium-high.
- **What Villeneuve knew.** On 15 October private letters said Rosily had passed Bayonne. On 18 October he heard "par le bruit public" that Rosily was in Madrid to take command, and wrote that he would sail next day if the wind allowed (Desbrière pp. 141–142; Corbett 1910, pp. 331–332). Fraser (p. 63) puts the Madrid news within hours of the Bayonne letters, about 15–16 October. Medium.
- **The council of war, 8 October.** Gravina had asked for it on the 6th, and Villeneuve called it on board the Bucentaure; fourteen officers signed, the Spanish brigadier Hore among them, whom Desbrière's narrative leaves out. It resolved to wait for weather that drove the British off, or for them to divide (Desbrière 1907, annexes 97–100). The argument that the allied crews were unpractised was Escaño's, Gravina's chief of staff, not Prigny's as Corbett (p. 324) has it. High.
- **Into the roadstead.** The fleet entered Cádiz on 20 August (Escaño's relation; his later letter says the 21st) and lay first in the inner harbour; the Argonauta anchored between the Puntal and Matagorda forts (Desbrière 1907, annexes 8). From 9–10 October it moved down to the roadstead at the harbour mouth (Fraser 1906, p. 53; James III 385; Nelson's diary, 14 October: "Enemy at the harbour's mouth"), out of range of the city's guns and so moored with springs (Escaño, annexes 98), with two anchors, NW and SE (so the Montañés, annexes 372). Not every ship moved: the Fougueux lay between Puntal and Matagorda until she warped out on the 19th, and the innermost ships stayed at anchor that day (annexes 213, 216, 273). High.
- **The decision to sail.** Hard westerly gales blew from the 10th to the 17th; at midnight on the 17th the wind went east (James III 385). Coast signals reported four of the line gone from Gibraltar with a convoy, one there dismasted and another going in, so Nelson seemed six short (Desbrière p. 141). On the 18th Villeneuve ordered Magon out that night with four French and three Spanish of the line and the Hermione to catch the inshore frigates (his letter, annexes 101; Desbrière's narrative says three ships), then decided to sail next day. The signal to unmoor went up at 5.15 p.m., and the cables were hove short by 10.30 p.m. (Desbrière p. 160). High.
- **19 October.** The signal to weigh went up at first light (5–6 a.m. by the French; 7 by James), but the breeze died and only seven of the line got out: Algésiras, Achille, Argonaute, Neptune, Héros, Duguay-Trouin and the Spanish Bahama, with the frigates Rhin and Hermione, the Thémis following in the evening (Philibert's journal, Desbrière 1907, annexes 229–230; the Montañés, annexes 373). British observers counted 9 sail at 4 p.m. and 12 at 6 p.m., frigates included, and that is where James's and Nicolas's "twelve ships" comes from (NRS II 146; `research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-30c). High.
- **The night of 19–20 October.** Magon stayed under sail off the port. After midnight the Bahama and Achille anchored north-west of the Cádiz light, near Rota; at daylight the Euryalus saw nine under sail and four at anchor (Desbrière p. 162; NRS II 147). Nelson was told of "near forty Sail" outside, an overcount (diary, Nicolas VII 136–137). High.
- **20 October.** The signal to weigh came at first light (6 a.m. by the Montañés and the Observateur, 6.30 by Desbrière). The whole fleet was out of the bay by about 8–8.30 a.m., the Rayo last at about 10, and the Euryalus counted 34 out at 8.50–9.10 (Desbrière 1907, annexes 158, 213, 216, 373; NRS II 147–148). Desbrière's time of about 11 (p. 163) and Corbett's "nearly noon" (p. 337) describe the fleet formed and standing off. High. The scene avoids "at noon".
- **At sea.** 33 of the line, 5 frigates and 2 brigs (Pickle's log at 3 p.m.; the Thémis). In rain and a fresh wind from SSW to SW they stood WNW, then wore onto the starboard tack in mid-afternoon: between 1 and 3 (Desbrière p. 164), at 3.30 (the Sirius) or about 4 (Corbett; Prigny), in three columns (Prigny, Fougueux, Desbrière) or five (James; the Spanish official report, per Nicolas). About 6 p.m. the Achille saw 18 British sail to the WSW. About 8 p.m. Villeneuve ordered the line of battle on the starboard tack, Gravina leading with a light, and a night course "S.-O. 1/4 S.", that is SW by S by compass, about S by W true (Desbrière p. 164). High for the sequence; the hour and the columns are disputed (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-31e, H2-31g).
- **The combined fleet.** 18 French (4 of 80 guns, 14 of 74) and 15 Spanish: the Santísima Trinidad (130 guns in Nicolas VII 130, 136 in the scene 1 notes, 140 in Desbrière p. 134), Santa Ana and Príncipe de Asturias (112), Rayo (100), two of 80, eight of 74 and San Leandro (64). With them the French frigates Cornélie, Hermione, Hortense, Rhin and Thémis and the brigs Argus and Furet (Collingwood's dispatch; James III 386). High.
- **Left in port.** The bay had held 35 of the line: Villeneuve's 29 from Ferrol and 6 Spanish fitted out at Cádiz (Clowes V 121; Desbrière pp. 134–135). The Terrible 74 was disarmed, and the San Fulgencio 64 docked to survey her bottom; their crews manned the newly commissioned ships (Gravina to Decrès, 16 September, Desbrière 1907, annexes 84). The corvette Observateur stayed behind by order (Desbrière p. 174). British counts from the offing ran from 34 to 36. High.

### Positions on the chart

- **Method.** Logged positions are worked by plane sailing from San Sebastián light (36°31.7′ N 6°19.0′ W), compass bearings corrected for 21° W variation. Every point was checked against the chart's own land (Natural Earth 10 m), its 15″ ETOPO tile and modern depths (EMODnet, from the Spanish hydrographic model of the bay; open water today, not 1805 soundings). In the bay they were also checked against Faden's *Plan of the Harbour of Cadiz* (London, 22 September 1797), from Tofiño's survey of 1789, georeferenced for this section on eight forts and towers (Santa Catalina at El Puerto, Puntal, Matagorda, San Sebastián, Torregorda, Puertas de Tierra, Fort San Luis, Rota) to within about 0.1 nm in the bay (0.18 nm rms). Medium.
- **The bay in 1797.** The plan sounds the entrance between Las Puercas (36°32.9′ N 6°18.2′ W) and the shoals of El Diamante (about 36°33.6′–34.6′ N 6°17.4′–18.0′ W) at 6–7 (fathoms or Spanish brazas, about 10–13 m), where French ships anchored after the battle "entre les Porques et le Diamant, par les 7 brasses d'eau", and the outer bay north-east of it at 5¾–7. It draws the Spanish fleet of 1797 moored in a north–south line along about 6°15.8′ W from 36°33.5′ N to 36°31.8′ N, and more ships through the Puntal–Matagorda narrows: a guide to the water, not to the moorings of 1805. The outer bay is shallower today (4–9 m), and the narrows are bridged. Medium.
- **The anchorage, 10–19 October.** `research/scene-02/a-h2c.md` (H2-36c–e) gives the description: out of range of the city's guns, moored with springs, a main group in the outer bay north-east of the entrance shoals, and a few ships deeper in until the 19th. The main group is centred on 36°33.8′ N 6°16.4′ W and strung WNW–ESE over about 1.2 nm, from 36°34.0′ N 6°17.1′ W to 36°33.6′ N 6°15.7′ W, perhaps in two or three lines (not recorded). Its glyphs stay inside 36°33.4′–34.1′ N, 6°15.5′–17.2′ W: 5¾–7 on the plan, 4–9 m today, at least 0.6 nm from every shore and 1.0 nm from the chart's coast. The 1.5–2 nm of that ruling would reach El Diamante at the WNW end. Medium for the description, low for the coordinates.
- **Outer and inner ships.** The outermost ships, the seven that got out on the 19th, lie at the WNW end toward the passes, about 36°33.7′ N 6°17.2′ W. A few ships (the Fougueux among them; the number is not recorded) lie in the narrows at 36°30.9′ N 6°15.3′ W, 0.2 nm NNE of the ruling's point, which falls on Natural Earth's coastline; here the water is 6–7 m today, and the 1797 plan draws Spanish ships. Low.
- **Headings at anchor.** About 294° true: the line of the two anchors (NW and SE by compass), and the way a moored ship lay to the westerlies of 10–17 October and to the flood on the 18th–19th. Medium.
- **Not here.** The first suggested centre, 36°33′ N 6°18′ W, lies beside Las Puercas in about 4 m of water, too shallow for a ship of the line (`research/scene-02/v-h2c.md`, H2-36d). High.
- **The harbour mouth.** The passes between Las Puercas and El Diamante: 36°33.5′ N 6°17.4′ W, in 9–10 m today, 0.9 nm north-east of Las Puercas and 0.5 nm south of El Diamante (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-38b). Medium.
- **The sortie.** At 4 p.m. on 19 October the Algésiras lay about 36°33.8′ N 6°22.0′ W, about 3½ nm west of the passes (Philibert's cross-bearings, `research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-38b). At 6 p.m. Magon's division was about 36°29.4′ N 6°23.1′ W, 4 nm south-west of San Sebastián (W by S by compass), standing north (Euryalus; Desbrière's 2 leagues WSW of the light agrees within 2 nm). At 9 a.m. on the 20th the fleet was outside about 36°34′ N 6°25′ W (the Montañés's bearing of the San Sebastián tower). Medium; low for the last.
- **The watch on 19 October.** The inshore frigates kept close off the port; the chain ran about due west (true) from Cádiz and sagged south at its inner end (table below; it agrees with "The signal chain, 19 to 21 October"). Medium for the frigates, low for single ships of the line.
- **The main fleet.** Its October station was 15–20 leagues west of Cádiz on about the port's parallel, centred near 36°32′ N 7°22′–25′ W (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-40c). At 9.30 a.m. on 19 October Victory lay about 36°34′ N 7°24′ W, between the diary's "Cadiz bearing E.S.E. by compass, distance sixteen leagues" (36°33′ N 7°18′ W) and the noon log, 36°36′ N 7°30′ W: due west (true) of Cádiz. The "W.S.W." of James and of Nicolas's narrative follows the "E.N.E." misprint (`research/scene-02/a-h2b.md`, H2-20b). The fleet lay to, then chased SE by compass, about 114° true. Medium.
- **Dawn, 21 October: the British.** As in "The setting", some 20 nm west of Cape Trafalgar: about 36°08′ N 6°28′ W (±6′; `research/dawn-setting.md`, section 1.2). 27 of the line "sans ordre" in two groups strung north to south (Scipion), some ships 3–4 nm from the Victory at 4 a.m. (Mars, Ajax), heading N by E by compass, about 350° true, until they bore up at 6.40–6.50 to ENE (about 047° true), then E by N (058° true) from 8 a.m. (Victory's log). Africa, parted in the night, had Cape Trafalgar SE 8–9 leagues at 6 a.m.: about 36°21′ N 6°30′ W. Medium; low for Africa.
- **Dawn, 21 October: the combined fleet.** Its centre about 36°09′ N 6°14′ W, 9–12 nm east of Victory. 33 of the line in a ragged line about 5 nm long running north–south true (NNE–SSW by compass), in two irregular divisions with Gravina's squadron to windward of the van, heading about south (180–195° true) on the starboard tack under easy sail. The Euryalus lay between the fleets, 5–6 nm west of them (`research/dawn-setting.md`, section 7). Medium.
- **Where this differs from scene 1.** Nothing here contradicts "The setting" or "The fleets". It adds the British spread, Africa's place and the British heading at first light (the lab draws them on 068° true, nearer the course steered after 8 a.m.). Villeneuve's own reckoning (Cape Trafalgar ESE about four leagues; Fraser 1906, pp. 110–111) puts his centre about 2½ nm north-west of the scene 1 position, and French officers put the coast 18–24 nm off (`research/dawn-setting.md`, F-13). Desbrière's midnight estimates cannot be joined to the dawn positions to better than 5–15 nm (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-32i), so the night tracks are drawn loosely and end on the dawn positions. Medium.
- **Gibraltar and Tetuan.** Louis's ships lay in Gibraltar Bay off the town (36°08′ N 5°22′ W, 15 m) and in Tetuan Bay off the Martil (35°38′ N 5°15′ W, 17 m); the town points in the notes are on land (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-39). Medium.
- **Boulogne.** Natural Earth runs the coast at about 1°34.5′ E on the harbour's latitude, west of the Liane, so on the chart the basin is land. The visible flotilla lies in the road outside the pier, as the lines of 1804 did: about 50°43.5′–44.7′ N, 1°33.3′–33.9′ E, a line parallel to the shore 0.6 nm inside the chart's water. The camps (the Tour d'Ordre plateau, 50°43.9′ N 1°35.8′ E; Outreau, 50°42.2′ N 1°35.7′ E), Wimereux, Ambleteuse, Étaples and Pont-de-Briques all fall on the chart's land. Medium for the places, low for the flotilla's layout.
