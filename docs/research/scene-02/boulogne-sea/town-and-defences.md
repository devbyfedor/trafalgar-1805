# Boulogne from the roads, August 1805: the town and the shore defences

Research note for scene 2, beat 1 ("Boulogne from the sea"): what of the town of Boulogne-sur-Mer and of the defences of its roads could be seen from a boat anchored in the roads on an August evening in 1805, at what size, colour and place, and how sure we are.

Status: complete. Section 4 lists what is uncertain. The camps on the heights and the flotilla are in their own notes in this folder and appear here only where they touch the town and the defences.

Confidence follows the scale in `docs/history-notes.md`: **High**, several independent primary sources agree, or a computation confirmed a second way; **Medium**, one good source or a consistent inference; **Low**, an estimate, a measurement from a picture, or sources in conflict. Each fact also says what it rests on: **period** (documents, memoirs, plans and images of the time), **later scholarly** (Desbrière 1900–02, James, Bertrand 1828–29, Nicolay 1907 and the like), **modern** (recent works and web pages, used as leads), **DEM** (heights read from the IGN RGE ALTI terrain model through the Géoplateforme altimetry service, modern ground) or **inference** (ours).

Units: the French foot (*pied*) of 1805 is 0.325 m and the *toise* 1.949 m. Bearings are true, from a sample camera in the roads (see 1.1).

## For the painter

1. **The skyline has one tower.** The town climbs the slope from the river to the walled upper town (*haute ville*) on a plateau at 50–60 m. Its only tall vertical is the belfry: a square stone tower with an octagonal top stage, about 37 m high on ground at about 52 m, so its top stands near 90 m above the sea. In 1803–05 the arms of the Chappe telegraph stood on its top: a mast with a crossbar and two dark swinging arms. Seen from the roads the belfry stands against the green slope of Mont Lambert (187 m, about 3 km behind), not against the sky. High for the belfry; medium for the telegraph still being there in August 1805 (period engraving of 1803–04, two period views of 1804, Bertrand 1829).
2. **What must not be painted.** No dome and no basilica (begun 1827, dome finished 1854 or 1863). No tall column on the northern plateau: the Colonne de la Grande Armée was begun on 9 November 1804 and was only 19–20 m high when the Empire fell, so in August 1805 there was at most a building site. No lighthouse tower on the cliff: the Roman Tour d'Odre fell in 1644. No stone lighthouse at the harbour, no long breakwaters, no casino, no town on the west bank (Capécure was built after 1808), no bastioned earth forts on the hills (built from September 1805) and none of the 1879 forts. High (period and later scholarly sources agree; section 1.6).
3. **The old cathedral is gone or going.** It was sold on 21 July 1798 and pulled down stone by stone. No source found says how much stood in August 1805, but the views of 1803–04 show no church mass beside the belfry. Paint nothing there, or at most a low ruin hidden behind the ramparts. Medium for "no tall ruin"; low for the exact state.
4. **The upper town's ramparts** form a walled rectangle of about 400 × 325 m, its walls rising 13–16 m above the ground outside, of dark grey-brown stone, crowned by a double row of trees. The enclosure stands corner-on to the sea, so from the roads one sees its western corner with a face running off to each side. The château, an octagon of round towers, sits at the far (eastern) corner and is barely visible from the sea. From 2–3 km offshore the wall top lies about 1.1–1.4° above the horizon. Medium (Bertrand 1829, one source; DEM for the heights).
5. **The lower town** of houses two and three storeys high spreads from the quays up the valley and the slopes. Before about 1800 it was dark like the ramparts. Plastering and whitewashing were making it pale, so in 1805 it was a mix of cream-white and grey-brown walls. Roofs were red-brown tile with some grey slate. The fishermen's quarter (*la Beurrière*) climbs in steps up the slope under the northern cliff. Chimney smoke rises. Medium for the change of colour (Bertrand 1829); low for the roofs (read from Hue's painting of 1804–06 and regional usage).
6. **The harbour is a forest of bare masts** filling the Liane valley between the town and the dunes. Two long timber jetties on piles run out across the beach. At the end of the western one stands a pierhead battery of two tiers, called Fort Napoléon (ten 36-pounders and mortars), with a tall wooden harbour light near it. A three-storey barracks block about 76 m long, used as the flotilla's store, stands at the head of the harbour. High for the masts and the timber jetties (four period views); medium for the details.
7. **The tide changes everything.** The spring range is about 7.9 m and the neap range about 4.4 m. At low water the beach in front of the jetty heads is about 750 m wide and the two stone forts stand on bare rock. At high water they rise straight out of the sea, and the wooden fort stands in the water on its piles. High (Desbrière's tide scale and plan of 1768).
8. **Fort de l'Heurt**, the southern twin, stands on a reef off Le Portel, 2.5–3 km south-south-west of a camera off the harbour. It is a D-shaped masonry fort about 48 × 32 m, with a round front to the sea and a straight back. Its gun platform is 41 French feet (about 13.3 m) above the rock, behind a low parapet. It carried twelve 36-pounders and four long-range howitzers, with mortars in the centre. It is of large hewn blocks of grey-buff stone, with casemated quarters in the back. High for date, size and armament (period reports in Desbrière); medium for the colour.
9. **Fort de la Crèche**, the northern twin, is the same design on a lower rock in front of the Pointe de la Crèche, about 2.3 km north of the harbour mouth. Its platform is a little lower and was more often awash. It carried 16 guns and howitzers. Nothing of it survives. Medium (same reports; its exact rock is not located).
10. **The wooden fort** (*fort en bois*, also called fort Rouge or fort de l'Expédition) is a timber gun platform on piles, set on the low-water line at the end of a 700 m fascine dike, seaward of the harbour mouth on the north side of the channel. That puts it 0.5–1.5 km from a camera in the roads, the nearest work of all. It carried eight 24-pounders and two howitzers and had a garrison post for 50 men. A period British view draws it as a low reddish block bristling with guns. High for the position and armament; low for its look.
11. **Batteries line the cliff tops** from the Crèche to Cap d'Alprech: low earthworks with parapets of earth-filled wicker gabions, embrasures and dark gun muzzles, with mortars on timber platforms and tricolour flags. Their magazines and guard-houses are of planks. The heaviest were at the Tour d'Odre: twelve 24-pounders and six huge mortars, with a second, lower battery below. From the sea they read as brown-red blocks along the cliff edge. Medium (period orders and reports; Nicolay for the Tour d'Odre armament).
12. **The cliffs.** North of the harbour, from the Tour d'Odre to the Crèche, the cliffs are 45–55 m high, broken, brown and grey, with fallen rock and the debris of the Roman tower on the beach below. An engineer of the time measured 55 m at the Tour d'Odre. South of the harbour, dunes run up to the Châtillon hill (about 40 m), then the Outreau plateau (55–60 m) and lower cliffs of 20–35 m at Le Portel. High for the heights (DEM and the period figure); medium for the colours (period views).
13. **Signal posts.** Besides the telegraph on the belfry, a naval signal mast stood on the Tour d'Odre cliff between Napoleon's and Admiral Bruix's pavilions. Flagstaffs on the Fort de la Crèche and on Mont de Couppes, south of the town, repeated the admiral's signals. Napoleon's pavilion on the cliff top was a long wooden hut, painted grey, ending in a rotunda that faced the harbour. Medium.
14. **Framing.** From a boat 1–2 km west of the harbour mouth, the town fills bearings of about 95–110°. Mont Lambert's crest and the belfry top both stand about 1.5–2° above the horizon. The Tour d'Odre cliff, just left of the harbour mouth, stands 1.3–2.2°. The twin forts frame the roads at bearings of about 25–45° (la Crèche) and 190–210° (l'Heurt), outside a 60° view centred on the town. Inference from the coordinates and the DEM.

## 1. The town from the roads

### 1.1 Setting, heights and a sample camera

The town sits where the little river Liane breaks through the coastal hills. North of the mouth a cliffed plateau runs to the Pointe de la Crèche. South of it lie dunes and the Châtillon hill, then the Outreau plateau and the cliffs of Le Portel and Cap d'Alprech. The upper town stands on a spur on the east side of the valley, about 1.5 km inland. Behind everything rises Mont Lambert.

Heights of the modern ground from the IGN terrain model (DEM; the ground has changed little away from the harbour, the cliff edges have retreated somewhat since 1805):

- Belfry foot 52 m; the highest point of the upper town, by the basilica, 59 m; the plateau slopes up eastward to 60–65 m just outside the walls. High (DEM).
- The lower town lies at 5–20 m: Saint-Nicolas church stands at 13 m. A section from the harbour to the upper town crosses the valley at 10–12 m and climbs to 52–60 m within 500 m. High (DEM).
- The north cliffs: at the Tour d'Odre site the plateau edge is at 45–55 m, rising inland to 60–80 m. A little further north, near Terlincthun, the edge drops to about 30 m in a valley, then rises again to 45–69 m at the Pointe de la Crèche. High (DEM). The officer of engineers Henry, who wrote on Boulogne at the time, gave 55 m for the cliff under the Tour d'Odre (quoted in Nicolay 1907, p. 45). Medium (period, at second hand).
- The south side: the Châtillon ridge about 43 m; the Outreau plateau rising from cliffs of about 33 m at the shore to about 60 m; Le Portel's cliffs 20–30 m, rising inland to 55–60 m; Cap d'Alprech about 50 m. High (DEM).
- Mont Lambert 187 m, 3.5 km east-south-east of the harbour; the column's site 92 m, on the plateau about 2.5 km north-east of the upper town. High (DEM).

Positions: the belfry 50.7250 N 1.6132 E; the château 50.7256 N 1.6169 E; the basilica, on the old cathedral's site, 50.7262 N 1.6151 E; Saint-Nicolas 50.7233 N 1.6073 E; the Tour d'Odre site 50.7317 N 1.5972 E; Fort de l'Heurt 50.7081 N 1.5597 E; the column 50.7411 N 1.6183 E; Mont Lambert 50.7175 N 1.6514 E (Wikipedia geodata, modern). The 1805 harbour mouth (the jetty heads) is taken here as about 50.730 N 1.594 E, the wooden fort as about 50.732 N 1.585 E and Fort de la Crèche as about 50.749 N 1.593 E. Those three are our inference from the descriptions in section 2, uncertain by 200–300 m. Low.

Distances and bearings from two sample cameras (our computation):

- Camera A, about 2 km off the harbour mouth (50.7300 N 1.5650 E): wooden fort 1.4 km at 81°; harbour mouth 2.0 km at 90°; Tour d'Odre 2.3 km at 85°; Saint-Nicolas 3.1 km at 104°; belfry 3.4 km at 99°; château 3.7 km at 98°; column site 4.0 km at 72°; Mont Lambert 6.2 km at 103°; Fort de la Crèche 2.9 km at 43°; Fort de l'Heurt 2.5 km at 189°; Le Portel 2.6 km at 165°.
- Camera B, about 1.1 km off (50.7300 N 1.5780 E): wooden fort 0.5 km at 66°; harbour mouth 1.1 km at 90°; Tour d'Odre 1.4 km at 82°; belfry 2.5 km at 103°; Mont Lambert 5.4 km at 105°; Fort de la Crèche 2.4 km at 27°; Fort de l'Heurt 2.8 km at 208°.

Angular heights follow from these: the belfry top (about 89 m) stands 1.5° above the horizon from camera A and 2.0° from camera B. Mont Lambert's top stands 1.7° and 2.0°. The cliff at the Tour d'Odre (about 50 m) stands 1.3° and 2.1°. So from anywhere in the roads the belfry is seen against the hillside behind it, and the skyline is the long, soft line of Mont Lambert and the plateaus, not the town. Earth curvature is negligible here (under 3 m at 6 km). Inference, from the DEM and the positions above.

### 1.2 The upper town: ramparts, gates and château

- **Walls.** The walls were built for Count Philippe Hurepel between 1227 and 1231 (modern: French Wikipedia, *Ville fortifiée de Boulogne-sur-Mer*; the year 1231 and the builder stand in the inscription over the château gate, which Bertrand transcribes, 1829, p. 97). Bertrand describes the enclosure as a parallelogram whose long sides, running east–west, are 400 m each and whose short sides are 325 m. Its stone ramparts rise 40–50 French feet (13–16 m) above the ground outside, are flanked by half-moons and are crowned by a double row of trees forming a promenade. Medium (Bertrand 1829, pp. 95–96; later scholarly, by a local historian writing in the 1820s).
- **Gates.** There were four, one on each face: the Porte des Dunes, still in use; the Porte Neuve or de Calais, rebuilt in 1632; the Porte Gayole; and the Porte des Degrés, walled up since the sixteenth-century wars of the League (Bertrand 1829, pp. 101–102). French Wikipedia also lists four gates. Medium for their 1805 state.
  - **Orientation.** Bertrand's compass names are a quarter-turn loose. The modern coordinates put the Porte des Degrés south-west of the belfry (50.7238 N 1.6123 E), the Porte Gayole on the south side (50.7236 N 1.6151 E) and the château at the eastern corner (Wikipedia geodata). So the enclosure's faces look north-west (the Porte des Dunes, Bertrand's "north"), north-east (the Porte Neuve), south-east (the Porte Gayole) and south-west (the Porte des Degrés). The enclosure stands corner-on to the sea: from the roads one sees its western corner, with the north-west face running off to the left and the south-west face to the right, above the roofs of the lower town. Our inference from the coordinates. Medium.
- **Corner towers.** A tower once stood at each corner. They were razed in 1687, except the château at the eastern corner (Bertrand 1829, p. 96). Medium.
- **The château.** It was built in 1231 at the same time as the walls, an octagon with round towers. Six of its eight towers still showed in 1828; two had been built into newer walls. It stood in a dry ditch and served in 1803–05 as a powder magazine and arsenal (Bertrand 1829, pp. 96–100; Bertrand 1828, p. 337). Position 50.7256 N 1.6169 E, at the far, landward corner: from the sea it hides behind the town and shows at most as a few towers at the right-hand end of the ramparts. Medium. The 1801 British plan by Luffman shows the walled square with "The Castle" at its eastern corner (section 3).
- **What happened in 1805.** Napoleon did nothing for the town's walls: not one gun was added to them, and the defences were put on the heights about a quarter of a league out (Bertrand 1829, p. 11). Medium.

### 1.3 The belfry and its telegraph

- **The tower.** The belfry stands in the upper town behind the town hall. Its lower part is a square medieval keep. Its upper stage is octagonal. The French Wikipedia article, citing the Mérimée record and the town's leaflet, dates the octagon to the early eighteenth century, built by the engineer Martinet after a slate spire burned in 1712, and gives the height as 37 m. Bertrand (1829, pp. 102–103) thought the octagon older, before 1544. He says the tower had once carried a spire and in his day ended in a small turret, on which a telegraph had stood. A watchman kept guard in it and rang the great bell for fires. High for the form (square base, octagonal top, small turret or lantern, no spire in 1805); medium for the 37 m (modern, one figure). Position 50.7250 N 1.6132 E, ground 52 m (DEM).
- **The Chappe telegraph on it.** The order to build a telegraph branch from Lille to Boulogne was given on 28 August 1803, and the branch worked throughout the Empire (modern: the Chappe telegraph history site, `telegraphe-chappe.com`). Three independent pieces put its terminal on the belfry:
  - Nicolay (1907, pp. 33–34) reproduces an engraving of an XII (1803–04) titled "Le tachygraphe de Chappe sur le beffroi de Boulogne". It is a view from inland towards the roads. The belfry rises above the upper town with the telegraph's crossbar and arms on its top, labelled "signaux au beffroi". Other labels mark a signal mast by the baraque on the cliff, the camp, the channel and Châtillon. Period image, at second hand.
  - Bertrand (1829, pp. 103–104) says the telegraph on the belfry's turret, which talked to Paris and Strasbourg in minutes, drew lightning onto the tower several times. It was then moved to the south-west angle of the rampart, and later taken to Calais. He gives no date for either move.
  - The two British views from the sea of 1804 (section 3) both show it. Lewis's key lists "The Telegraph" in the town, where he draws a slender steeple with a crossbar on top. Pocock's view shows the big tower of the upper town with an apparatus on its top.

  So a Chappe telegraph stood on the belfry in 1803–04. High. That it was still there in August 1805, rather than already on the rampart, is likely but not proven. Medium. A Chappe telegraph of this period is a vertical mast carrying a long crossbar (the *régulateur*) with a short arm (*indicateur*) pivoted at each end, painted dark and worked by ropes from the room below; its dimensions were not checked for this note.

### 1.4 Churches in 1805, and the cathedral that was gone

- **The old cathedral of Notre-Dame**, in the upper town next to the bishop's palace, was the town's great church. In 1798 it measured about 47 toises 3 feet long (92.6 m) and 7 toises 3 feet (14.6 m) high to the entablature, in the sale's own description, and it had a tower and two big turrets in sixteenth-century pictures (Bertrand 1828, p. 97; Haigneré 1857, p. 261). It was sold as national property at Arras on 3 thermidor an VI (21 July 1798) to six buyers from outside the district, for 510,500 francs. They pulled it down: the tombs were broken open, the marble columns and statues thrown down, the walls brought crashing to the ground (Haigneré 1857, pp. 260–262, quoting a witness who saw it as a child). The Mérimée record has 10 July 1798 for the sale and "progressive destruction" (modern, via search). By 1820, when the abbé Haffreingue bought the site, it was a field of rubble with a few pieces of wall and broken column shafts. By 1828 only the site and one piece of wall remained (Haigneré 1857, p. 272; Bertrand 1829, p. 16).
  - **August 1805: not documented.** No source found says how far the demolition had gone by 1805. The two British views of 1804 and the engraving of 1803–04 show the belfry as the only tall building of the upper town, with no church tower or roof mass beside it. Our reading is that by 1805 the cathedral was a ruin no higher than the houses, if it stood at all. Low to medium. Paint no cathedral.
- **Saint-Nicolas**, the lower town's church, on the market square (place Dalton). It was rebuilt and enlarged in 1231. Its tall medieval tower was pulled down in 1688 and replaced by the steeple of Bertrand's day, and the ruined nave was rebuilt in 1775. During the Terror it served as a barracks and a store, which is why it was neither sold nor destroyed. It was returned to worship and repaired (Bertrand 1829, pp. 138–139). In 1805 it stood with a modest steeple, at 13 m above the sea and 2–3 km from the roads. High for its standing; medium for the steeple's form. In the 1804 views two small spires show in the lower town, one of which is probably Saint-Nicolas.
- **The upper town's parish** after 1802 was the chapel of the Annonciades convent, a church begun in 1772 (Haigneré 1857, p. 262; Bertrand 1829, pp. 104–105). It is small and hidden among the houses. Medium. Other convent churches had been sold and mostly demolished; the Capuchins' church at the south end of the lower town still stood almost whole in 1828 (Bertrand 1829, p. 16). Low for any spire visible from the sea.

### 1.5 The lower town and the harbour

- **Houses and colour.** Bertrand (1829, p. 113) describes the lower town's streets as wide, paved and lined with houses of two and three storeys. Less than thirty years before he wrote, the lower town had looked gloomy, "couleur sombre comme l'extérieur des remparts" (sombre in colour like the outside of the ramparts; his exact words are the evidence for the colour). The local stone was the cause. The new habit of plastering and whitewashing the walls was making it look like a new town. So around 1800–05 the town was turning from dark grey-brown stone to whitewash. Medium. Pocock's view from the sea (1804–05) paints the town as a pale cream mass, and Lewis's shows several white houses among darker ones (section 3). Medium.
- **The fishermen's quarter.** Seen from the Outreau side, Bertrand describes la Beurrière rising like an amphitheatre to the top of the slope, under the north cliff. Another part of the lower town forms a second amphitheatre up to the foot of the ramparts, with the belfry dominating the upper town (Bertrand 1829, pp. 54–55, a description of about 1828). Medium.
- **Roofs.** No written source was found. Hue's painting of the harbour (1804–06) shows red-brown tile roofs on most houses and a few grey roofs on larger buildings. Bertrand notes bricks and tiles made in the district (1829, p. 62). Low.
- **The barracks (*les Casernes*).** Built after 1770 on land the sea had left at the head of the port, to designs by Giraud-Sannier. The block is 234 by 33 French feet (76 × 10.7 m), of local stone, with two floors over the ground floor and huge cellars. In 1803–05 it served as the flotilla's general store and arsenal (Bertrand 1829, pp. 139–140; Bertrand 1828, legend no. 16 and p. 336). It is the biggest single block on the quays. Medium.
- **Quays.** In the spring of 1803 there were only short quays, all on the east bank. An order of 14 March 1803 called for a kilometre of timber quays, and work began on 1 May. Bertrand's figures, as Desbrière quotes them, run as follows. An open-work timber trestle (*estacade à claire-voie*) 221 m long, begun in May 1801, ran from the east jetty to the packet-boat quay. A solid timber quay then joined it to the old Citadel quay, making 443 m. The Victoire quay joined the customs quay to the barracks quay, another 429 m. From the Pidou (east) jetty to the end of the barracks quay the east side measured 1,090 m. The west quays later reached 1,301 m, and 2,015 m with the semicircular basin (Desbrière III, pp. 149–151). Medium.
- **The basin.** A semicircular basin was dug on the west bank, in the muddy flats called the Brebiettes, with a 78 m passage through the west quay. It was meant at first for 100 large gun-boats. Digging began in May 1803, the main excavation from 24 November 1803 with 2,000 soldiers. The basin was finished well after the planned date (Desbrière III, pp. 149–152). In 1805 the west bank opposite the town was dunes, mudflats, the basin, boatyards and the big artillery stores. The houses of Capécure and the "new town" there came only after 1808 (Bertrand 1829, pp. 12–14). High.
- **The service bridge, dam and sluice.** A timber service bridge crossed the Liane above the town. A dam with a flushing sluice (*écluse de chasse*), long planned to scour the channel and keep craft afloat, was building in 1804–05. Its timber frame was finished, or nearly, by December 1804, when work stopped for the winter. Desbrière prints the date of that letter as 22 frimaire an XIII "(13 décembre 1805)"; frimaire XIII is December 1804 (Desbrière III, pp. 150–151; IV, pp. 372–374; Bertrand 1828, legend no. 15). The powder magazines stood beyond the service bridge (Nicolay, p. 82). Medium.
- **The jetties and the beach.** The harbour mouth was framed by two timber jetties built on piles, running out across the beach. Ozanne's view of 1776, Luffman's plan of 1801 and Hue's painting of 1804–06 all show them (section 3). Hue paints them as open-work trestles on piles. Medium for 1805. A plan of 1768 by the engineer Cadier gives 386 toises (752 m) from the west jetty's head to the low-water line, and the bar lay just off the jetties (Desbrière III, p. 142). A fascine dike had been run out about 700 m from the Pidou jetty to the spring low-water line, nearly parallel to the west jetty, to straighten the channel (Desbrière III, p. 148). So at low water a beach some 700–750 m wide lay in front of the jetty heads, with the channel winding across it. High for the 1768–1803 geometry; medium that it was the same in 1805.
- **Tides.** The port's tide scale, as Desbrière gives it, has spring tides rising from 1.04 m to 8.90 m above chart datum (a range of 7.86 m) and neap tides from 2.64 m to 7.06 m (4.42 m). Equinoctial high water reaches 9.30 m; the quay tops are at 10.56 m (Desbrière III, p. 142, note). High.
- **The pierhead battery and the light.** These are described under the defences (2.5). The old lantern and reverberating lamp at the jetty's end were pulled down in July 1803. A new lantern was built of wood, much higher and set back so as not to hinder the guns (Desbrière III, pp. 154–155, the order of 3 July 1803). Lewis's key of October 1804 has "The Light House" beside the battery on the pier head. Medium.

### 1.6 What did not stand in August 1805

- **The basilica of Notre-Dame and its dome.** The abbé Haffreingue bought the site in 1820. The first stone was laid on 1 May 1827, the dome was finished in 1854 (the town's leaflet) or 1863 (the regional inventory), and the western towers were crowned in the 1870s (modern: the town's heritage leaflet on the basilica and crypt; the Mérimée and regional inventory records via search). High that nothing of it existed in 1805; the dome's completion date is disputed but irrelevant here.
- **The Colonne de la Grande Armée.** The army decided on it in September 1804: Soult's order of the day of 21 September 1804 called for a column 50 m high with a bronze statue, between the Tour d'Odre headquarters and the camp of the first division (Nicolay, pp. 309–310). Bertrand dates the decision to 1 vendémiaire XIII, and says it was to be 150 feet high (1828, pp. 383–384, 391). Marshal Soult laid the first stone on 18 brumaire an XIII, 9 November 1804 (Bertrand 1828, p. 390, with the stone's inscription). Nicolay's translator prints "6 November", which is a misconversion. The foundations went 6 m deep (Nicolay, p. 312). Work was brisk at first and slowed when the army left in 1805. The column was only 19 m high in 1814 (Nicolay, p. 313), or 20 m at the fall of the Empire (French Wikipedia). It was finished in 1841, 53.6–54 m tall, on ground at 92 m (DEM).
  - **In August 1805** there was at most the foundations and the beginning of the base, with scaffolding, cranes and stone stacks, on the plateau about 4 km north-east of a camera in the roads, close to 90 m above the sea. No source gives its height at that date. High that there was no tall column; low for anything visible.
- **The Tour d'Odre.** The Roman lighthouse (Caligula's, in tradition) stood on the cliff edge north of the harbour. It fell in 1644 when the cliff under it collapsed (Nicolay, ch. II, pp. 35–45; French Wikipedia). In 1803–05 nothing of the tower stood on the cliff. Its fallen debris and the rocks of the cliff strewed the beach below; Henry, an officer of engineers, saw them there (Nicolay, p. 45). On the cliff top were remains of the fortifications the English built around it in 1544: stumps of red-brick walls and a polygonal earth bulwark. Napoleon's pavilion stood on a mound within them, in one of the old bastions (Bertrand 1828, legend no. 34 and pp. 344–345; Nicolay, pp. 44–45; Luffman's plan of 1801 marks a "Fort d'Ordre" there). High for the absence of the tower; medium for the remains.
- **Lighthouses.** No tall masonry lighthouse stood at Boulogne in 1805. The only light was the wooden harbour lantern by the pierhead (1.5). Medium. The present lighthouse is a modern work at the end of the Digue Carnot.
- **The modern harbour.** The long breakwaters (the Digue Carnot), the deep-water port, the jetties extended to the low-water line (still only a project in 1828: Bertrand 1828, legend no. 39) and the station and quays of Capécure are all later. High.
- **The sea-bathing establishment and the casino**, built from 1824, are later (Bertrand 1828, legend no. 37). High.
- **The fortified ring on the heights.** Bertrand says Napoleon, before he left, ordered sixteen earthworks, mostly bastion-shaped and palisaded at the back, on the heights around the town, built by the flotilla's crews. A second line of six forts with scarp and counterscarp followed on Mont de Couppes, at the Petit-Moulin, by the Renard farm, on Mont Lambert, half-way along the Wimille road and between the Crèche cliff and Terlincthun (Bertrand 1828, pp. 406–408). Napoleon wrote from Ludwigsburg on 3 October 1805 that he was glad to hear Boulogne's fortifications were finished. He asked for intermediate lunettes and for works on the promontory of l'Heurt and on the Crèche (Corr. XI, No. 9325). The Fort de Terlincthun dates from 1806–08 (English Wikipedia). So in August 1805 the heights had camps and coast batteries, not this ring of forts. High for the dates. One caution: Bertrand mentions the First Consul firing a gun "at the fort of Mont-de-Couple" in November 1803 (1828, p. 346), so some battery stood on that hill earlier.
- **The 1879 forts** on the Pointe de la Crèche, Cap d'Alprech, Mont de Couppes and the Tour d'Odre, of the Séré de Rivières system, are later (English Wikipedia, *Fort de la Crèche*). High.

### 1.7 Materials and colours

- **Stone.** The local building stones are the grey and brown Jurassic limestones and sandstones of the Bas-Boulonnais, with the darker "marbles" of Marquise. Bertrand's remark (1.5) that the old town was sombre in colour like the outside of the ramparts is the nearest period evidence. Hue's painting gives the ramparts and the old houses a warm grey-brown. Medium.
- **Whitewash.** From about 1800 more and more of the lower town's houses were plastered and whitewashed (Bertrand 1829, p. 113). In 1805, paint a mixed town, with pale cream-white fronts among grey-brown ones. Pocock's view, made from the sea, shows the whole town pale. Medium.
- **Roofs.** Red-brown tile with some grey slate on larger buildings. Low (no text found; Hue's painting).
- **Timber** for the jetties, quays, the wooden fort and the huts; black or grey with tar and weather. Medium (no colour stated; inference from period practice).
- **The evening light.** With the sun low in the west behind the camera, the west-facing town, ramparts and cliffs are lit full on. The whitewashed fronts will glow warm, the dark stone will read brown, and the hills behind will catch the light on their seaward slopes. Inference.

## 2. The shore defences of the roads

### 2.1 The scheme

The roads of Boulogne ran from the Crèche in the north to Cap d'Alprech in the south (Napoleon to Berthier, 28 March 1804, Corr. IX, No. 7650). Dode, the engineer in charge, set out the plan in a report of 22 November 1803 (Desbrière III, pp. 153–154). The anchorage could only be safe for a large flotilla if strong works held both ends. Nature had marked the places: the rock of l'Heurt for the left (south) wing and the rock of la Crèche for the right (north) wing, 2,400 toises (4.7 km) apart. That was too far for the two to cover the centre, so a third, middle fort was needed. The two wing forts were designed for 13 guns in a casemated battery plus 6 guns and 4 mortars on an upper platform; the project was settled at the end of germinal an XI (about 20 April 1803). The centre was first to have Forfait's floating fort, which could not be got out of the port. It was given instead a timber fort on piles at the low-water line, the fort en bois (2.4). Behind and between them the cliff tops and beaches carried batteries (2.6). Period (Dode's report and Napoleon's letters, printed by Desbrière, a later scholar). High.

The straight-line distance between the modern Fort de l'Heurt and the rocks off the Pointe de la Crèche is nearer 5 km than 4.7 km by our reckoning, a difference that may come from where the Crèche fort's rock lay (2.3). Low.

### 2.2 Fort de l'Heurt (the south wing)

- **Where.** On a rock reef on the foreshore off Le Portel, at 50°42′29″ N 1°33′35″ E (Wikipedia). The DEM puts the reef about 0.7 km out from the top of the beach and 0.8 km from the low cliff of Le Portel, on ground that dries at low water. From a camera off the harbour mouth it lies 2.5–3 km away to the south-south-west. High.
- **When.** Work began on 4 prairial an XI, 24 May 1803 (Bertrand 1828, p. 319; Wikipedia). Two months went on borings, cutting the rock bed and building ramps down the cliff. The first stones were laid on 29 June 1803 (Desbrière III, p. 154). The rest of the story is one of storms:
  - The masonry reached 7–8 feet by mid-September 1803. Gales then tore away whole courses, and from October only the outer shell was built (Desbrière III, p. 160). The gale of 10 November 1803 swept away a month's work in three tides.
  - Work stopped in December 1803 and resumed on 23 March 1804 (III, pp. 453–454). The storm of 30 May 1804 nearly demolished both forts (IV, p. 381).
  - In June 1804 l'Heurt reached the level of the solstice spring tides (III, p. 436; IV, p. 382). On 15 July 1804 the cores were finished and the first 36-pounders mounted. On 16 July guns and howitzers stood in battery on temporary platforms. From 17 July l'Heurt had a garrison of 30 men and gunners for six pieces (IV, pp. 382–383).
  - In August 1804, 40–45 large dressed stones were laid at each fort daily (IV, p. 383). By September the core had reached the level of the first battery, and before winter only the parapets were added (IV, p. 384).
  - On 11 November 1804 Napoleon noted that the two forts were "made" and were not to have casemates, and cut their budget (Corr. X, No. 8177, p. 51). The upper, casemated battery of the first design was dropped.
  - High (period reports and letters).
- **State in 1805.** Soult's report of 8 March 1805 (Desbrière IV, p. 384) gives the state after the winter.
  - Both forts stood at the level of the first battery, "41 pieds au-dessus du rocher" (41 French feet, about 13.3 m, above the rock).
  - Each had 16 pieces. L'Heurt had twelve 36-pounders and four long-range howitzers.
  - The casemates in the gorge (the rear) were finished and being fitted with bunks, doors and windows. The cordon (the string course under the parapet) was in place, and the parapet had been raised 3 feet.
  - 18 feet of paving was laid around the platform's edge. The parapet's coping and the rest of the paving were still to do.
  - The engineers asked to build a guard-house over the casemate to keep the rain out.

  Napoleon had ordered three long-range mortars for each fort in March 1804 (Corr. IX, No. 7651). Wikipedia gives the armament as twelve 36-pounders and four 6-inch howitzers behind a parapet, with three 12-inch mortars in the centre of the platform. High for the guns and the height; medium for the mortars.
- **Size and shape.** A note attached to Dode's report of 22 November 1803 gives "the fort" a great diameter of 48 m, a small diameter of 32 m and a circumference of 150.75 m (Desbrière III, p. 160). It was built as a shell of large dressed blocks, with a rubble core and internal buttresses (*noyau et contreforts*; III, p. 160; IV, p. 384). Wikipedia describes the gun deck as a half-circle "with a development of 150 m". It gives the facing blocks as 1.90 × 1.30 × 0.40 m from the quarries of Ainghem and Wimille (Desbrière prints "Aingban"), with a rubble core in lime or cement mortar. Entry was by a door reached by a ladder, then a spiral stair in a tower up to the platform.
  - Our reading of the figures is a D or horseshoe plan: a half-round front of 32 m diameter facing the sea, straight sides, and a straight gorge 32 m long, 48 m from gorge to nose. That shape gives a perimeter of about 146 m, close to the 150.75 m stated. Medium for the size; low for the exact plan and orientation. The ruin can still be seen at Le Portel and would settle it.
- **From the sea.** A massive pale stone drum rises from a dark reef. In August 1805 the stone was new, grey-buff with weed and wet dark at the foot. It stands about 13 m above its rock, with a projecting string course and a low parapet over which the muzzles of the 36-pounders show. A flagstaff carries the tricolour, and timber ramps or ladders lead down to the rock. At high water the reef is covered and some 7–8 m of wall show above a spring tide. At low water the whole height and the reef show. The heights above water are our inference: the DEM puts the reef near mean sea level, which we take as about 4.4 m above the port's chart datum, against the tide scale in 1.5. Low. The colour is inferred from the stone. Medium for the rest.
- **Later.** It is a ruin today, preserved by a German concrete slab of the Second World War and damaged by a storm in 1998 (Wikipedia).

### 2.3 Fort de la Crèche (the north wing)

- **Where.** On a rock in front of the Pointe de la Crèche, between Boulogne and Wimereux, about 2.3 km north of the harbour mouth and 2.4–2.9 km from a camera off the harbour at bearings of 25–45°. Its rock was lower and flatter than l'Heurt's: neap low water left only half of it uncovered, and the bed had to be cut 8–10 inches deeper (Desbrière III, p. 154). High for the general place. Its exact rock is not fixed here. The coordinates of the 1879 battery on the cliff top (50.7489 N 1.6014 E) are not the Napoleonic fort's. We take the fort as about 50.749 N 1.593 E. Low.
- **Same design, a little lower.** It was built at the same time and to the same design as l'Heurt (Desbrière; English Wikipedia calls it a copy). In June 1804 it stood two or three feet lower than l'Heurt above its rock and was often covered by the sea (Desbrière III, p. 436; IV, p. 382). It fired its first shot on 16 July 1804, at a British frigate that came in with two brigs (IV, p. 382). In March 1805 it carried six 36-pounders, six 24-pounders and four long-range howitzers (IV, p. 384). A battery on the land behind protected it (Nicolay, p. 80). High.
- **Later.** The sea wore it down, and its ruins, a danger to shipping, were removed (Nicolay, p. 80). Only foundations are said to be visible today (English Wikipedia). The fort now called Fort de la Crèche is the 1879 battery on the cliff top.

### 2.4 The wooden fort (fort en bois, fort Rouge, fort de l'Expédition)

- **What and where.** On 21 July 1803 an order charged the naval engineers with building a timber platform on the low-water line, to the right of the east jetty, able to carry five heavy guns and two mortars (Desbrière III, p. 155). It stood at the seaward end of the fascine dike that ran about 700 m from the east (Pidou) jetty to the spring low-water line (III, pp. 148, 155). Bertrand puts it at the end of the channel, close to the low-water mark (1828, p. 331); Nicolay says it was close to the harbour entrance and built of timber because the sand would not bear masonry (pp. 80–81). Napoleon noted in September 1803 that it stood right at the low-water line and that it uncovered at low tide (Corr. VIII, No. 7074; Desbrière III, p. 158).
  - "Right" here means right as seen from the harbour looking out, that is on the north side of the channel. Lewis's view from the sea of October 1804 draws his "Pile Battery" on the beach north of the harbour mouth (section 3).
  - From the roads it was the nearest work of all, 0.5–1.5 km from the camera. High for the place in general; low for exact coordinates (about 50.732 N 1.585 E, our inference).
- **Names.** It was called successively *fort en charpente* (timber fort), *fort Rouge* (red fort) and *fort de l'Expédition* (Desbrière III, p. 155), and the Fort-en-Bois in Boulogne (Bertrand 1828, legend no. 40). High.
- **When.** The first piles were driven at the start of fructidor an XI, late August 1803. Nicolay gives 1 fructidor, 19 August (Bertrand 1828, p. 331; Nicolay, p. 80). The British cut down the pile-driving engines one night (Bertrand dates it 8 September 1803). Real work went on only from 17 September 1803 to 5 February 1804, with about 50 men when the weather allowed (Desbrière III, p. 159). It was taken up again in May 1804 and finished at the end of August 1804, armed and provisioned before the winter, through which it stood (III, p. 453; IV, p. 381). High.
- **Armament and garrison.** In March 1805 it was "in the best possible state and entirely finished": eight 24-pounders and two 6-inch long-range howitzers, with biscuit, water, brandy and cheese for 50 men for six days. A raised step behind the parapet and a palisade of piles around the fort had been asked for and put off (Soult's report of 8 March 1805, in Desbrière IV, p. 381). Napoleon had allowed it two Gomer mortars in March 1804 (Corr. IX, No. 7651). High.
- **Look.** No technical description was found. The evidence is three pictures and a name:
  - Lewis (October 1804) draws the "Pile Battery" as a squat, reddish-brown block on the beach with a row of guns along its top.
  - Hue's painting (1804–06) shows, at the seaward end of the jetty works, a long low battery with small buildings and a flagstaff, standing in the sea.
  - Nicolay (p. 81) prints a drawing of uncertain source of a weatherboarded building with a hipped roof raised on a grid of piles, with braced timber stairs.
  - The name "fort Rouge" suggests it was painted red, but no source found says so.
  - Paint a timber platform on a forest of piles, with a parapet of heavy timber, guns, a timber guard-house and a flagstaff, dark-tarred or reddish. Low.
- **Later.** The British required its demolition in 1814, and it was pulled down (Bertrand 1828, legend no. 40; Nicolay, p. 81). A street in the fishermen's quarter keeps the name.

### 2.5 The pierhead battery (the musoir, "Fort Napoléon")

- An order of 3 July 1803, made at Dunkirk, had the head (*musoir*) of the jetty enlarged in stone or timber for a battery of ten 36-pounders on coast carriages: three on each side and four in the middle, with two long-range mortars and a furnace for heating shot. Four guns were to stay ready to fire while the work went on (Desbrière III, pp. 154–155). The same order raised the old lantern and its lamp at the jetty end, replacing it with a much taller wooden lantern set back from the guns. A second order of the same day put two 36-pounders at the end of the Pidou (east) jetty (III, p. 155). High.
- The west pierhead was to be named Fort Napoléon. It was unfinished in the spring of 1804, needed 20 more days on 3 August 1804, and was finished at the end of August 1804 (Desbrière III, p. 453; IV, p. 381). Napoleon wrote of mortars to go "between the first battery and the high battery" of the musoir, so it had two tiers (Corr. IX, No. 7650). He had forbidden any vault over "the fort of the musoir" and threatened to have one pulled down at the engineer's cost, so its guns stood in the open (Corr. IX, No. 7431, 26 December 1803, p. 174). High for the dates, the tiers and the open platform.
- From the sea, Lewis's view of October 1804 shows the pier head as a rounded whitish masonry work with two rows of guns, which his key calls "a battery carrying [a] line of guns on the pier head", with "The Light House" rising beside it. Pocock's view shows a long white low battery with a flag at the harbour entrance. Medium for the look.

### 2.6 The batteries along the cliffs and beaches

- **What was ordered.** Napoleon's orders of 3 July 1803 (Desbrière III, p. 155) did several things:
  - They replaced the short-range 12-inch mortars of the coast batteries round Boulogne with long-range ones.
  - They moved the 24-pounders of the battery at the Moulin Hubert to the platform above it.
  - They added ten new two-gun batteries of 24-pounders or heavier between Boulogne and Calais. These were built in earth, with magazines and guard-houses of planks.

  In August–September 1803 he wanted two-gun platforms on the beaches: a third of them on the low-water line, a third at 50 toises above it and a third at 80 toises. He wanted mortar beds that the tide covered, with the mortars brought out at low water, and light 24-pounders and 8-inch howitzers kept in teams to run down onto the sand when the British came near (Corr. VIII, Nos. 7061 and 7074; Desbrière III, pp. 157–159). By March 1804 the roads from the Crèche to Alprech had 30 mortars, which he thought too few (Corr. IX, No. 7650). Period. High.
- **Names in the reports.** The monthly report for prairial XII (May–June 1804) shows guns being changed at the batteries of the Petite-Garenne, Ningle, la Crèche, the Tour d'Ordre and the "low battery of the Tour d'Ordre", Le Portel, the "Haut-Blaner" (so printed) and the Chasseurs' battery. That month the coast batteries fired 62 bombs, 15 shells and 220 round shot at British ships (Desbrière III, p. 435). The "battery of lower Alprech" was finished and armed on 17 July 1804 (IV, p. 383). Ney, writing to Soult on 12 March 1804 about a possible bombardment, names the Grenadiers' battery as one end of the stretch an enemy squadron might anchor along, with the fort de l'Heurt at the other (III, pp. 551–552). Nicolay (pp. 79–80, 82) adds several more:
  - three plunging-fire batteries on Cap d'Alprech;
  - a battery half-way down the slope north of the harbour;
  - the great battery at the top of the Tour d'Odre cliff, with twelve 24-pounders and six huge mortars, nicknamed the "monsters";
  - a battery by the Moulin-Wibert, which is the Moulin Hubert of 1803;
  - the "battery of the Republic" on a headland, since washed away, opposite the later Legion of Honour monument;
  - a well-armed rampart north of the Tour d'Odre near the Honvault farm.

  Medium. Nicolay's figures for the Tour d'Odre battery are not sourced.
- **"Batterie de l'Impératrice."** No battery of that name was found in Desbrière III–IV, Napoleon's Correspondence (vols VIII–XI), Bertrand or Nicolay, nor in a web search. Do not use the name without a source.
- **What a battery looked like.** An etching of 1804 of the Tour d'Odre mortar battery (reproduced in Nicolay, p. 80) shows a row of very large mortars on timber platforms behind a long parapet of gabions (earth-filled wicker baskets), with heaps of shells. Lewis's view of 1804 draws the batteries as low reddish-brown earthworks with a line of dark guns along the top and tricolour flags. They stand on the cliff tops, at mid-slope and at the cliff foot on both sides of the harbour. In Pocock's view the battery on the northern cliff top is pale, with a flag. Bertrand says the English called the coast between l'Heurt and the Crèche "the iron coast" for its guns (1828, pp. 333 and x). James speaks of masked as well as open batteries all along the cliff edge, with seven or eight "forts erected on the sands at low water" and mortar beds over which the tide flowed (III, p. 229). Medium.

### 2.7 Signal posts

- **The belfry telegraph.** The Chappe telegraph on the belfry linked Boulogne to Paris by Lille (1.3). High for 1803–04; medium for August 1805.
- **The Tour d'Odre.**
  - At the end of August 1803 Napoleon asked to be lodged outside the town, in one of two country houses he had noticed near the "tour des signaux", which he thought was on the Calais road (Corr. VIII, No. 7061, 31 August 1803, pp. 508–509). Three days later he turned down Sganzin's plan for quarters at the "croix des signaux" as too big and costly and asked instead for a wooden "tent" the size of a ship's great cabin, with four small cabins and a roof projecting six feet all round (No. 7069, 3 September 1803, pp. 513–514). That lodging became the baraque on the Tour d'Odre. The names suggest a signal tower or a signal mast with a cross-yard on that cliff in 1803. Medium.
  - Between Napoleon's and Admiral Bruix's pavilions stood the naval "Signal" semaphore. Next to Napoleon's observation post was a "signalling terrace" from which orders went to the flotilla (Nicolay, pp. 22, 29).
  - An order of the day of 27 germinal XII (17 April 1804) had flagstaffs on the Fort de la Crèche and on Mont de Couppes repeat the admiral's signals from the Tour d'Odre (Nicolay, p. 29). The engraving of 1803–04 marks a signal mast by the baraque and a flagstaff on the Châtillon heights (Nicolay, p. 33).
  - In 1809 a "semaphore of the Tour d'Ordre" served as a survey mark (Bertrand 1828, p. 426).

  Medium. Paint a tall mast with a yard and flag halyards on the cliff top near the pavilions, and flagstaffs on the forts and the southern heights.
- **"Telegraph Hill."** Lewis's key of October 1804 lists "The Telegraph on Telegraph Hill" as well as the one in the town, and draws a small mast with arms on a distant hilltop to the right of the town. Pocock paints something like a windmill on the top of the high hill behind the town. Which hill this was, and whether it was a Chappe relay, was not found. Low.
- **Coast guard posts.** The coast guard's guard-houses and signal posts ran along the whole coast; Napoleon ordered them put in the best state in October 1803 (Corr. IX, p. 51, a letter of 12 October 1803). The plank guard-houses of the batteries are in the order of 3 July 1803 (2.6). Low for any detail.

### 2.8 Napoleon's pavilion and the headquarters huts on the Tour d'Odre

- The pavilion stood on a small mound on the cliff edge, inside the old English fortifications of 1544 around the site of the Roman lighthouse, looking over the camps, the town, the harbour, the roads and the English coast. It was of wood painted grey, 100 feet long by 22½ feet wide (about 32.5 × 7.3 m). The end facing the harbour was a rotunda 30 feet (9.7 m) across, the council room (Bertrand 1828, pp. 344–345). Near it stood the huts of the ministers, the commander-in-chief and the admiral (same). Bruix's was built on the same lines but smaller. Soult's was thatched down to the ground and lit from above; Decrès's was like Soult's (Nicolay, pp. 21–22). Medium.
- From a camera in the roads the pavilion is 1.4–2.3 km away on the cliff top, about 50 m up, just left (north) of the harbour mouth. It would read as a long low grey building with a round end, beside a signal mast, among the camp's huts. Inference. The camps themselves are in the companion note on the camps.

## 4. Doubts and disagreements

- **The old cathedral in August 1805.** It was sold on 21 July 1798 (Haigneré, from the sale) or 10 July 1798 (the Mérimée record, via search). It was razed by 1820. No source states its state in 1805. "Nothing tall standing" rests on our reading of three views of 1803–04. Low to medium.
- **The belfry.** Bertrand (1829) thought the octagonal stage older than 1544. The Mérimée record and French Wikipedia say it was built after a fire of 1712 by the engineer Martinet. The 37 m height rests on Wikipedia alone. The telegraph is certain for 1803–04, but when it moved to the rampart is unknown.
- **"Telegraph Hill"** in Lewis's key is unidentified. Pocock's matching object looks like a windmill.
- **The column.** The first stone is dated 9 November 1804 by Bertrand, the inscription's 18 brumaire XIII and Wikipedia. Nicolay's translation prints 6 November, and some web pages 9 October. The planned height was 50 m (Soult's order) or 150 feet (Bertrand). Its height in 1814 is 19 m (Nicolay) or 20 m (Wikipedia). Its state in August 1805 is not documented.
- **The forts' dimensions.** Desbrière's note gives 48 m, 32 m and 150.75 m for "the fort", without saying which. The two were built to one design, so it should hold for both. Wikipedia's 175 m² for the gun deck cannot be squared with a 48 × 32 m fort and is not used. The platform height of 41 French feet is 13.3 m; Wikipedia says 13.50 m. The D-shaped plan is our inference from the figures.
- **The forts' start.** Work began on 24 May 1803 (Bertrand, Wikipedia) and the first stones were laid on 29 June 1803 (Desbrière, from Dode). Both fit: site work came first. Wikipedia's "operational on 16 July 1804" matches Dode's report of that day.
- **The distance between the forts.** It is 2,400 toises (4.7 km) in Dode's report and about 5 km on the modern map to the rocks off the Pointe de la Crèche. The Crèche fort's rock is not located.
- **The wooden fort.** The piles were begun on 1 fructidor XI, 19 August 1803 (Nicolay), or "at the start of fructidor" (Bertrand), after the order of 21 July 1803 (Desbrière). Its look is uncertain: Lewis, Hue and Nicolay's figure all differ, and nothing says "Rouge" meant its paint. Its position is inferred.
- **The 1805 jetty heads and low-water line** are placed by inference from the 1768 plan and the 700 m dike (Desbrière). The cliff edges have retreated since 1805, so the DEM edge heights are modern.
- **Nicolay (1907)** is a popular history based on documents but seldom referenced. His armament of the Tour d'Odre battery, the "battery of the Republic" and the Moulin-Wibert battery are not checked against period papers. The Moulin-Wibert does match the "moulin Hubert" of the 1803 order.
- **The ring of forts on the heights.** Bertrand's legend says "built in 1805". His text says ordered at Napoleon's departure, and Napoleon's letter of 3 October 1805 says finished by late September. So they postdate August 1805. A battery or signal post did stand on Mont de Couppes in 1803–04.
- **Colours.** How far whitewashing had gone by 1805 is unknown. The roofs are undocumented in text, and the colours of the forts' stone and of the wooden fort are inferred.
- **Battery names.** "Haut-Blaner" is as printed in Desbrière and may be a misprint. No "batterie de l'Impératrice" was found.
- **The views' geometry.** Pocock's and Lewis's views compress distance, and Lewis's is naive. They show what existed, not where exactly.

### How the note was made

The full texts of the period and later books were downloaded from archive.org into a scratch folder outside the repository and searched there. Page images were checked for the key figures (Desbrière III, p. 160; IV, p. 384; III, p. 151; Bertrand's page offsets). The views were viewed and cropped in the same scratch folder. Nothing downloaded is in the repository.
