# Check of `town-and-defences.md`: Boulogne's town and shore defences seen from the roads, August 1805

An independent, adversarial check of `town-and-defences.md` (same folder), claim by claim. I did not write that note and do not edit it. Every verdict says what I opened and where. Verdicts: **confirmed** (the source says it, at that place, and nothing better contradicts it), **corrected** (substantially right, but a page, figure, date, attribution or confidence needs changing), **unsupported** (the cited source does not say it, or I could not find it there or anywhere), **refuted** (the evidence says otherwise). Confidence uses the scale at the top of `docs/history-notes.md`. Kinds of evidence as in the note: **period**, **later scholarly**, **modern**, **DEM**, **inference**.

Status: complete (13 November 2026, session 26).

Summary of the outcome:
- The note is careful. Its quotations, dates and figures are mostly where it says. Its distances and bearings are right.
- Its main errors are in what the eye sees from the boat. The belfry's top does break the skyline. The château is on the wrong side of the belfry. The belfry may still have carried a spire in August 1805.
- It also puts mortars on the stone forts that the 1805 state does not list.
- It misreads the Crèche fort's height, misdates Pocock's sketch, and repeats Bertrand's late dates for the wooden fort.

## What the painter must change or know

1. **The belfry's top breaks the skyline.**
   - From a boat 0.7–2.5 km off the harbour mouth, the top 4–8 m of the belfry, and the telegraph on it, stand against the sky, not against Mont Lambert.
   - The ground straight behind the belfry is Mont Lambert's north-west shoulder: 140–166 m high, 5–6 km from the boat, lower than the summit. The summit itself is 2–6° to the right of the belfry.
   - The lower two-thirds of the tower stand against the hill. From 3 km off, the top just touches the skyline.
   - Medium. This rests on a bare-earth terrain model. Trees on the crest would lift the skyline a little.
2. **What crowned the belfry in August 1805 is not settled.**
   - The town's own leaflet says a spire stood on the octagon "until 1805". The engraving of 1803–04 and Lewis's view of October 1804 draw a slender pointed top with the telegraph on its point. Pocock's view (sketched in July 1804) draws a blunt top with the arms on it. Bertrand's small turret is the state of 1828.
   - The owner should choose: a slim spire carrying the telegraph (two views of three), or a blunt octagon with a small turret and the telegraph.
   - Either way, the square lower stage has four small corner turrets.
   - Low. The tower without a spire is 35–37 m.
3. **The château is left of the belfry, not at the right-hand end.**
   - Seen from the roads, the château stands behind the middle of the north-west rampart, about 2° left of the belfry.
   - The rampart's west corner, nearest the sea, is 1–2° right of the belfry. The north-west face runs off to the left over 6–9° of the view. The south-west face runs off to the right over only about 3°.
   - The wall top is about 0.8° above the horizon from 2 km off, and 1.1° from 1 km off.
   - Medium.
4. **No mortars on Fort de l'Heurt or Fort de la Crèche.**
   - The state of March 1805 lists twelve 36-pounders and four howitzers at l'Heurt, and six 36-pounders, six 24-pounders and four howitzers at la Crèche. It lists no mortars.
   - The parapets were only 3 feet high and their coping unfinished. A guard-house over the rear casemates had only been asked for.
   - Both forts had plank ramps on piers running to the shore.
   - Medium.
5. **La Crèche stood on a lower rock; it was not lower on its rock.**
   - Both platforms are 41 French feet (13.3 m) above their rock. La Crèche's rock was lower and more often covered, so its platform stood about a metre lower than l'Heurt's.
   - It lay close under the cliff, within reach of the cliff batteries.
   - Medium.
6. **At low water the harbour is a bed of sand and mud.**
   - The jetty heads stood high on the beach. Their foot was 6 m above chart datum, with the low-water line about 750 m further out. Only the Liane ran between them, 20–40 m wide.
   - The craft along the quays and in the basin sat on the mud. The basin was dug as a drying basin.
   - Even at spring high water the jetty heads had only about 3 m of water at their foot.
   - High.
7. **The lower town was mostly dark stone in 1805, with some new pale fronts.**
   - Bertrand's brightening by plaster and lime wash began after about 1800 and grew with the peace.
   - His houses "à deux et trois étages" have three or four levels (a ground floor plus two or three floors), not two or three.
   - Low to medium.
8. **No earth forts on the hills, not even begun.** Napoleon first asked for the plan of the redoubts round Boulogne on 29 August 1805. High.
9. **The period views show 1803–04, not 1805.** Pocock's covering letter is dated 31 July 1804, not October. By August 1805 the wooden fort, the pierhead battery and the two stone forts had stood finished for a year. High.
10. **Smaller fixes.**
    - The column site is 1.8 km north-north-east of the belfry, not 2.5 km north-east.
    - The Tour d'Odre cliff stands about 1.1° above the horizon from 2 km off and 1.8° from 1 km off.
    - The wooden fort's piling began in early August 1803.
    - The fascine dike is not dated.
    - Paint no "Batterie de l'Impératrice": no source names it.

## Verdicts

### The "For the painter" list

1. **The skyline has one tower.** Corrected, in two places.
   - Square base, octagonal upper stage, watchman, telegraph on top in 1803–04: confirmed. Bertrand 1829, pp. 102–104, I read them (archive.org leaves 113–115). The engraving of an XII is in Nicolay, p. 33; I looked at the page image.
   - **The top of the tower in August 1805 is open.** The town's own leaflet (*Focus: le beffroi et l'hôtel de ville*, Ville d'art et d'histoire, 2016, p. 3) says the octagonal stage was built in 1734, culminates at 35 m, and that "until 1805" a spire crowned it. The regional inventory has the fire of 15 August 1712 and the architect Étienne Martinet finishing the octagon in 1734 (its record IA00059444 in the national POP database, read in session 42; first seen as a search summary). Two of the three period pictures show a pointed top. The engraving of an XII draws a tall, tapering top with the telegraph mast on it. Lewis (October 1804) draws "The Telegraph" as a slender spire with a crossbar and arms on its point. Pocock's view (sketched in July 1804) shows a stout, blunt-topped tower with the crossed arms on top and no spire. Bertrand's "small turret" is his own day, 1828–29: "Autrefois surmonté d'un clocher, il est aujourd'hui terminé par une petite tourelle". So the spire may still have stood in August 1805, or may have been taken down that year. No source gives the month. Low. The note's "no spire in 1805, High" is not supported.
   - **Height.** 37 m is the French Wikipedia infobox. The town leaflet gives 35 m for the top of the octagon. Both are the present tower, without a spire. Medium for 35–37 m without a spire.
   - **Against the hill, not the sky: refuted for the top.** I traced the line of sight past the belfry over the IGN terrain model (see Recomputations). The ground behind the belfry along its bearing is not Mont Lambert's summit but its north-west shoulder, 140–166 m high, 5.3–5.7 km from the camera. From a boat 0.7–2 km off the harbour mouth, the top 4–8 m of a 37 m tower stand above that skyline. The telegraph on top would show against the sky. From about 3 km off, the top just touches the skyline. The body of the tower below about 20–25 m stands against the hill. Mont Lambert's summit lies 2–6° to the right of the belfry. Medium (bare-earth terrain model; trees or hedges on the crest, not in the model, could raise that skyline by a few tenths of a degree).
   - "Mont Lambert (187 m, about 3 km behind)": confirmed. The terrain model gives 188–190 m at the summit, 2.8 km east-south-east of the belfry.
2. **What must not be painted.** Confirmed, with one sharpening.
   - Basilica: Haigneré 1857 has the old site bought on 18 August 1820 and the new church begun in 1827 (ch. XXVIII). French Wikipedia (the walled town) gives 1827–1866 for the basilica. High that nothing stood in 1805.
   - Column: confirmed. Soult's order of 21 September 1804 for a 50 m column, Nicolay p. 309. First stone 18 brumaire XIII, 9 November 1804, with the inscription, Bertrand 1828 p. 390. Only 19 m high in 1814, Nicolay p. 313. Foundations dug 6 m deep, Nicolay p. 312.
   - Tour d'Odre: confirmed. It fell in three pieces, on 19 January, later in January and on 30 July 1644 (Nicolay p. 47, quoting the cathedral's chaplain; the note cites pp. 35–45). Henry's 55 m and the debris on the beach: Nicolay p. 45.
   - Ring of earth forts: confirmed and sharpened. Napoleon asked Berthier for "the plan of the field redoubts to be made around Boulogne" only on 29 August 1805 (Corr. XI, No. 9159, pp. 159–160). On 3 October 1805 he was glad they were finished (No. 9325, p. 283). Bertrand 1828, pp. 406–408, has the sixteen earthworks and the six forts. So on 9–11 August 1805 none of them existed, not even as a building site. High.
   - Capécure, breakwaters, lighthouse, casino: confirmed (Bertrand 1829, pp. 12–14; Bertrand 1828, legend nos. 37 and 39). The 1879 forts: not checked beyond the note's own source.
3. **The old cathedral is gone or going.** Confirmed as stated. The sale on 3 thermidor VI (21 July 1798) for 510,500 francs to six outsiders, and the account of the demolition, are in Haigneré, pp. 260–262. The child witness is T. Hédouin, whom Haigneré quotes, not Haigneré himself. In 1820 the site was "a confused heap of rubble" with a few pieces of wall and broken column shafts (Haigneré, ch. XXVIII). Bertrand 1829, p. 16, has only the site and a piece of wall left. No source dates the end of the demolition. A web search found nothing better. "Paint nothing there" stays. Low to medium.
4. **The upper town's ramparts.** Mostly confirmed. One error in the view.
   - 400 × 325 m, walls 40–50 French feet above the ground outside, half-moons, a double row of trees: Bertrand 1829, pp. 95–96, confirmed. OpenStreetMap's wall lines give a rectangle of about 430 × 333 m, so the figures hold within 7 %. The trees are attested in 1829. Nothing found says they stood in 1805. Medium for 1805.
   - Corner-on to the sea, the west corner nearest, faces looking NW, NE, SE and SW: confirmed by my own fit of the OpenStreetMap walls and gates (see Recomputations). Bertrand's compass names are an eighth of a turn out, not a quarter. His four corner towers stood at the north, west, south and east corners, which already shows the diamond orientation (p. 96).
   - **The château is not at the right-hand end.** From both cameras it lies 1.6–2.5° to the *left* of the belfry, behind the north-west face. The west corner, nearest the sea, lies 1.3–2° to the right of the belfry. The north-west face runs off to the left over 6–9° of view. The south-west face runs off to the right over about 3°.
   - Wall-top angle: corrected. The terrain model puts the rampart top at about 47–53 m. That is 0.8–0.9° above the horizon from camera A (2 km off) and 1.1–1.2° from camera B (1.1 km off), not 1.1–1.4°.
   - Colour "dark grey-brown": Bertrand compares the old lower town's sombre colour with the outside of the ramparts (p. 113). Medium.
5. **The lower town.** Corrected.
   - Bertrand's houses "à deux et trois étages" are houses with two or three floors *above* the ground floor, so three or four levels, not two or three (p. 113).
   - Colour: the quotation is exact, p. 113. Bertrand ties the brightening to the years after about 1800, and in the same breath names the army's stay, the peace and the fishing as the causes of later growth. In August 1805 the town was early in that change. Paint mostly grey-brown stone with scattered pale, freshly plastered fronts, not an even mix. Low to medium. *Badigeon* is a lime wash, often tinted, so cream or buff as well as white.
   - Roofs: no text found by me either. Low.
6. **The harbour.** Confirmed in substance; a few details need care.
   - Forest of masts, two timber jetties on piles: confirmed for 1804 by Hue and by the letter with Pocock's view.
   - The pierhead battery on the **west** jetty, ten 36-pounders (three each side, four in the middle) and two long-range mortars, on coast carriages: the order of 3 July 1803, Desbrière III, pp. 154–155, confirmed. Two tiers ("between the first battery and the high battery"): Corr. IX, No. 7650, p. 308, confirmed. "Fort Napoléon" was the name the west pierhead was to bear (Desbrière III, p. 453).
   - Taller wooden lantern, set back from the guns: confirmed (same order, art. 3).
   - Barracks: 234 × 33 French feet, two floors over the ground floor, built after 1770 to Giraud-Sannier's designs on land left by the sea at the head of the port, general store of the flotilla: confirmed (Bertrand 1829, pp. 139–140; Bertrand 1828, legend no. 16).
7. **The tide.** Confirmed, with one addition the painter needs.
   - Spring range 7.86 m (1.04 to 8.90 m above chart datum) and neap range 4.42 m (2.64 to 7.06 m): Desbrière III, p. 142, note. These are the port's scale of about 1900, not an 1805 measurement. High.
   - 386 toises (752 m) from the west jetty to the low-water line (Cadier's plan of 1768), and a foreshore 700–800 m wide at the head of the bay but only 450 m at La Crèche: Desbrière III, pp. 141–142. The 700 m fascine dike: p. 148. Desbrière does not date the dike; he says it had been built "long since" ("depuis longtemps"), so "the dike of 1803" is unsupported.
   - Addition: the foot of the west pierhead stood at +6 m above chart datum (III, p. 143). At low water the pierheads stood high and dry on the sand. Even at spring high water they had only about 3 m of water at their foot. Inside the harbour, all but the river channel dried. That channel was under 40 m wide, in many places under 20 m (III, p. 147). Grounding was "almost permanent" for the flotilla (III, p. 148).
8. **Fort de l'Heurt.** Corrected for the mortars.
   - Platform 41 French feet (13.3 m) above the rock, twelve 36-pounders and four long-range howitzers: Soult's report of 8 March 1805, Desbrière IV, p. 384. I checked the figures on the page image.
   - 48 × 32 m and 150.75 m: the note attached to Dode's notice of 30 brumaire XII (22 November 1803), III, p. 160. Confirmed. It says "the fort" without naming it.
   - **Mortars: unsupported for 1805.** Napoleon ordered three long-range mortars for each fort on 28 March 1804 (Corr. IX, Nos. 7650–7651), but the state of March 1805 lists 16 pieces and no mortars. Do not paint them.
   - March 1805 also had the parapet raised only 3 feet, the cordon (string course) in place, 18 feet of paving round the edge of the platform, and the coping and the rest of the paving still to do. The engineers asked for "a building over the casemate to serve as a guard-house". Whether it was built by August is unknown.
   - Its distance and bearing from the cameras: confirmed by my own computation (2.5 km at 189° from A, 2.8 km at 208° from B).
   - Colour: inference, as the note says. Medium is too strong for the colour. Low.
9. **Fort de la Crèche.** Corrected.
   - Its armament in March 1805 was six 36-pounders, six 24-pounders and four long-range howitzers (IV, p. 384). The note's sixteen pieces are right.
   - In June 1804 the fort was "generally two or three feet lower, although equally high above the rock, which is lower and often covered by the sea" (IV, p. 382). So the *rock* was lower and more often awash, and the platform stood 41 feet above it, as at l'Heurt.
   - Dode says the Crèche fort, unlike l'Heurt, could be backed by the cliff batteries, so it lay near the cliff foot (III, p. 160). The foreshore there is about 450 m wide (III, p. 141).
   - "Nothing of it survives" contradicts the note's own 2.3 ("only foundations are said to be visible"). Nicolay, p. 80, says its ruins were removed as a danger to shipping.
10. **The wooden fort.** Confirmed for the place and the guns; one detail added.
   - The order of 21 July 1803 ("on the low-water line, to the right of the east jetty"), the end of the fascine dike, and the three names: Desbrière III, p. 155. Eight 24-pounders and two 6-inch howitzers, supplies for 50 men for six days: Desbrière IV, p. 381.
   - Napoleon's note of August 1803 says it "uncovers" at low water (III, p. 158). So at low water it stands on dry or nearly dry sand, at high water in some 7–8 m of water.
   - "Garrison post for 50 men" over-reads the source, which gives rations for 50 men. Lewis's "low reddish block": I saw it in the crop of his drawing. Low for the look, as the note says.
11. **Batteries.** Confirmed with one correction of citation. Earth batteries with plank magazines and guard-houses (Desbrière III, p. 155, art. 4–5). The mortar battery behind gabions (the etching in Nicolay, p. 80). Twelve 24-pounders and six huge mortars at the Tour d'Odre (Nicolay p. 79, unsourced). The "low battery of the Tour d'Ordre" (III, p. 435). The "iron coast" (Bertrand 1828, legend p. x). Bertrand's legend speaks of 24- and 36-pounders along the whole cliff. Medium.
12. **The cliffs.** Corrected for the angles, confirmed for the heights. The terrain model gives 47 m at the Tour d'Odre site itself; Henry's 55 m is in Nicolay, p. 45. The cliff stands about 1.1° above the horizon from camera A and 1.8° from camera B, not 1.3° and 2.1°. Colours: Lewis paints the north cliffs dark brown and the south ones pale. Medium.
13. **Signal posts and the pavilion.** Confirmed. The "Signal" semaphore between Napoleon's and Bruix's pavilions: Nicolay, p. 22. The signalling terrace and the order of 27 germinal XII (17 April 1804) for flagstaffs on the Fort de la Crèche and the Mont de Couple: Nicolay, p. 29. The pavilion of grey-painted wood, 100 × 22½ feet, with a rotunda of 30 feet at the end facing the harbour: Bertrand 1828, pp. 344–345. Medium.
14. **Framing.** Confirmed for the bearings: my own computation reproduces every bearing and distance in 1.1 to within 0.1 km and 1°. Corrected for the cliff and wall angles (items 4 and 12) and for "the belfry against the hill" (item 1).

### 1.2 The upper town

- **Walls 1227–1231 for Philippe Hurepel.** Confirmed: French Wikipedia (*Ville fortifiée*), citing the tourist office. The Mérimée record IA00059456, which I read through the POP platform, has 1231 and "following almost the Roman enclosure". The inscription over the château gate with MCCXXXI: Bertrand 1829, p. 97, confirmed.
- **Size, height, half-moons, trees.** Confirmed, pp. 95–96. The trees for 1805: not documented (painter's item 4).
- **Gates.** Confirmed, pp. 101–102. Bertrand names the Porte Neuve also "Flamengue". The Porte des Degrés was blocked in the League's siege by the duc d'Aumale and "has not been opened since".
- **Orientation.** Confirmed with a wording fix: Bertrand's names are an eighth of a turn out, not a quarter. My fit to the OpenStreetMap walls gives corners near north, east, south and west. The long faces (about 430 m) run south-west to north-east. The Porte des Dunes is on the north-west face, the Porte Neuve on the north-east face, the Porte Gayole on the south-east face and the Porte des Degrés on the south-west face.
- **Corner towers razed in 1687.** Confirmed, p. 96. The Mérimée record has Louis XIV's demolition of an outer line "from 1689", a different work.
- **The château.**
  - Octagonal, built 1231, ditch: confirmed, pp. 96–97.
  - "Six of eight towers still showed": the sentence can also be read as six standing, of which two are hidden in newer walls. Low either way. It does not matter from the sea.
  - Powder magazine: Bertrand 1829, p. 99, says "in 1803 and 1804". Bertrand 1828, p. 337, says the château was "crammed with powder" during the camp. Confirmed.
  - "Arsenal" is the 1828 use (an artillery store, armoury and barracks, p. 98), not specifically 1803–05.
  - **Its place in the view: refuted** (painter's item 4). From the roads it lies left of the belfry, behind the north-west face.
- **Luffman's plan.** It shows "The Castle" at the bottom right of a square drawn square to the map, so south-east on the plan. His upper town is schematic. Confirmed as a source for "a corner castle", not for orientation.
- **Napoleon did nothing for the town walls.** Confirmed, p. 11: "on ne mit seulement pas un canon de plus sur ses remparts", the works being carried a quarter of a league out.

### 1.3 The belfry and its telegraph

- **Form and Wikipedia's account.** Confirmed as a report of what French Wikipedia says (octagon "early 18th century", Martinet, slate spire burned in 1712, 37 m).
- **The town's leaflet disagrees.** It has the octagon in 1734, 35 m, and a spire on it "until 1805". I read it. The regional inventory (record IA00059444) has the fire on 15 August 1712 and Martinet's octagon finished in 1734.
- **Bertrand.** Confirmed: the octagon "existed before the siege of 1544" (p. 103), the "clocher" of former days, the small turret of his own day, the watchman and the great bell (pp. 103–104).
- **Corner turrets.** Bertrand 1828, p. 97, describing a picture of the 1544 siege, says the belfry showed "its four turrets as one sees them today". So in 1805 the square stage carried four small corner turrets. The note does not mention them.
- **Confidence for the form: corrected from High to Medium** for the square base, corner turrets and octagon, and Low for what crowned it in August 1805 (painter's item 1).
- **Telegraph.**
  - The order of 28 August 1803 and the branch working through the Empire: confirmed on the Chappe history page.
  - Nicolay's engraving and its labels: confirmed, p. 33, from the page image. The belfry is drawn as a tall, slender tower whose top tapers to a point under the telegraph mast.
  - Lightning, the move to the south-west angle of the rampart, then to Calais: Bertrand 1829, p. 104, confirmed.
  - One informal web page claims that "Mr Chappe" put a telegraph on the belfry "in 1805" and that lightning destroyed the roof timbers in 1807. It cites nothing. If it reflects a local tradition, the 1805 work may be a rebuilding of the top, which would fit the leaflet's "until 1805". Not usable as evidence.
  - Medium for "still there in August 1805": confirmed.
- **The Chappe machine.** A mast with a regulator and two indicators, worked from below: confirmed as general knowledge. I took no dimensions either.

### 1.4 Churches

- **The old cathedral.**
  - 47 toises 3 feet long, 7 toises 3 feet to the entablature: Haigneré, p. 261, note 1, quoting the sale. Confirmed. The sale also gives a "reduced width" of about 12 toises 3 feet (24 m; the first digit is unclear in the scan), which the note leaves out.
  - The sale on 3 thermidor VI (21 July 1798), 510,500 francs, six outsiders: confirmed.
  - The demolition scene: confirmed, but the child witness is Hédouin, quoted by Haigneré (pp. 261–262).
  - The sixteenth-century tower and two big turrets: Bertrand 1828, p. 97, confirmed.
  - The site in 1820 and 1828: confirmed (Haigneré ch. XXVIII; Bertrand 1829, p. 16).
  - The Mérimée date of 10 July 1798: seen only as a search summary, as the note says.
- **"August 1805: not documented."** Confirmed. I found nothing either.
- **Saint-Nicolas.** Confirmed, pp. 138–139 (leaf 149), with one fix. Bertrand says it had been a chapel that Philippe Hurepel had *enlarged* in 1231, not "rebuilt and enlarged". The tall tower was pulled down in 1688 and replaced by "the present steeple". The ruined nave was rebuilt in 1775, to Giraud-Sannier's design (his biography, Bertrand 1829). It served as a barracks and a store in the Terror.
- **The Annonciades.** Confirmed: its first stone was laid on 27 July 1772 (Bertrand 1829, p. 105). The parish of the upper town after the Concordat: Haigneré, p. 262.
- **The Capuchins' church almost whole in 1828.** Confirmed, Bertrand 1829, p. 16.
- **Two small spires in the lower town in the 1804 views.** Confirmed in both the Pocock and the Lewis crops.

### 1.5 The lower town and the harbour

- **Houses and colour.** Confirmed with the *étages* correction (painter's item 5). The quotation is exact (p. 113).
- **La Beurrière and the second amphitheatre.** Confirmed, Bertrand 1829, p. 54. Bertrand describes the view from the Outreau slope. In it the belfry "dominates" the upper town.
- **Roofs.** Low, as the note says. In Hue's painting I see red-brown roofs on the houses below the slope in the foreground. Bertrand's bricks and tiles (p. 62) are made at Samer, 15 km up the valley.
- **Barracks.** Confirmed (painter's item 6). Bertrand adds cellars 770 feet long and room for 1,500–1,800 men.
- **Quays.** Confirmed, Desbrière III, pp. 149–151. The figures are Bertrand's (1828, p. 292), quoted in Desbrière's footnote. Bertrand gives the trestle of May 1801 as 221 m long, 5.80 m wide at the base and 7.29 m high above the tide-scale zero, on 67 frames.
- **The basin.** Confirmed. It was a *bassin d'échouage* (a drying basin) from the first: Desbrière III, p. 149. The note leaves this out. The Brebiettes: Bertrand 1829, p. 13. The new town of Capécure is "not yet twenty years old" in 1829 (p. 13), so after about 1809.
- **The service bridge, the dam and the sluice.**
  - The service bridge was finished by July 1803 (III, p. 151).
  - The dam was "barely begun" when work stopped in late 1804 (IV, p. 373).

- **Where.** Confirmed: the Wikipedia coordinates and the terrain model (ground about −1 m NGF at the fort, that is on the foreshore). Distances confirmed by my computation.
- **When.**

  - The two months of borings, cutting the bed and ramps down the cliff, and the first stones on 29 June 1803: Desbrière III, p. 154, confirmed.
  - 7–8 feet by mid-September, the shell only from October, the gale of 10 November 1803 sweeping away a month's work "in three tides": III, p. 160, confirmed.
  - Add: the same note gives "the fort" as 10 feet high on 26 vendémiaire XII (19 October 1803) and 15 feet 6 inches on 9 brumaire (1 November) (III, p. 161).

  - 15 July 1804, cores finished and the first 36-pounders mounted; 16 July, guns and howitzers in battery on provisional platforms; a garrison of 30 men and gunners for six pieces from the evening of 16 July: IV, pp. 382–383, confirmed.
  - 40–45 large dressed stones a day at each fort in August 1804; the first battery's level reached by September; then only the parapets: IV, pp. 383–384, confirmed.
  - Corr. X, No. 8177 (11 November 1804), p. 51: the forts "being made and not to have casemates", and the budget cut from 960,000 to 500,000 francs. Confirmed.
  - **Corrected wording:** "the upper, casemated battery was dropped" mixes two things. The first design had a *lower* casemated gun battery with an open upper platform above it. As built, the guns stood in the open at the level of the first battery, and only the gorge (the rear) had casemates, as quarters (IV, p. 384).
- **State in 1805.** Confirmed in every figure from the page image of IV, p. 384: 41 feet, sixteen pieces, twelve 36-pounders and four long-range howitzers, casemates finished, cordon set, parapet raised 3 feet, 18 feet of paving, coping to do, the guard-house over the casemate asked for. Add: the stair steps were to be set "within a month".
- **Mortars.** Corrected to unsupported for 1805 (painter's item 8).
  - French Wikipedia does say three 12-inch mortars in the centre, but without a source.
  - The order of 28 March 1804 (Corr. IX, No. 7651) is for three "mortiers à semelle" for each fort, to stand elsewhere until the forts were ready.
  - In June 1804 two 12-inch mortars "cast on a bed" (*coulés sur semelle*) went to the Crèche battery and the Tour d'Odre battery on the cliff, not to the forts (III, p. 435).
- **Size and shape.**
  - 48 m, 32 m and 150.75 m for "the fort": III, p. 160, confirmed.
  - Shell of dressed blocks, rubble core and buttresses: III, p. 160; IV, p. 384, confirmed.
  - The French Wikipedia details (blocks 1.90 × 1.30 × 0.40 m from Ainghem and Wimille, a door reached by a ladder, a spiral stair in a tower, a half-circle "of 150 m development", 175 m²): confirmed as Wikipedia's.
  - The D shape: my own sum for a half-round front of 32 m plus two straight sides of 32 m plus a 32 m gorge is 146 m, as the note says. An ellipse of 48 × 32 m would give only 127 m. So the D or horseshoe fits the stated circumference better. Medium, as an inference.
  - OpenStreetMap's outline of the present ruin is only about 37 × 14 m, so the ruin as mapped no longer shows the plan.
- **From the sea.** Inference, as marked.

  - Grey-buff stone: Low (painter's item 8). Flagstaff: likely, not documented.
  - Timber ramps: Dode's report of 11 August 1804 speaks of a left ramp at la Crèche that lost 25 planks, a right ramp whose pier was damaged, and a pier being built for the left ramp at l'Heurt (IV, pp. 383–384). So both forts had plank ramps on piers to the shore. Medium.
- **Later.** Confirmed as Wikipedia's: the German slab of the Second World War, the storm of November 1998.

### 2.4 The wooden fort

- **What and where.** Confirmed: the order of 21 July 1803 and "at the end of the fascine dike" (III, p. 155). The dike from the Pidou jetty: III, p. 148. Bertrand's "end of the channel, close to the low-water mark": 1828, p. 331. Nicolay's "close to the harbour entrance" and the sand: p. 80.
  - Napoleon's "fort Rouge ... tout à fait à la laisse de basse mer": Corr. VIII, No. 7074 (4 September 1803), confirmed.
  - "It uncovers at low water": the note annexed to the letter of 22 August 1803, in Desbrière III, p. 158. So August, not September.
- **"Right" means north.** Confirmed by the same convention in Dode (2.1). It agrees with Lewis's "Pile Battery" north of the mouth.
- **Names.** Confirmed: III, p. 155. Bertrand 1828, p. 331, calls it "Fort l'Expédition"; legend no. 40 has "Fort-en-Bois ou de l'Expédition, détruit en 1814".
- **When.** Corrected.
  - Bertrand puts the first piles at "the beginning of fructidor (end of August 1803)" and the British cutting of the pile-driving engines on 21 fructidor (8 September 1803), pp. 331–332. Nicolay gives 1 fructidor.
  - The documents in Desbrière contradict all three dates. The *Moniteur* of 14 August 1803 reports British ships firing, at 350 toises, on "the pile-drivers set up for building a fort" (III, p. 156, note 4). Napoleon's letter to Bruix of 22 August 1803 rebukes him for letting the British destroy them (III, pp. 156–157).
  - So the piling began by early August 1803, and the engines were cut before 19 August. Bertrand's dates look a month late (perhaps thermidor misread as fructidor).
  - The rest is confirmed: work from 17 September 1803 to 5 February 1804 with about 50 men (III, p. 159); resumed in May 1804 (III, p. 453); finished at the end of August 1804 and armed before winter (IV, p. 381).
- **Armament and supplies.** Confirmed, IV, p. 381. "Garrison post for 50 men" over-reads rations for 50 men. Two Gomer mortars allowed in March 1804: Corr. IX, No. 7651, confirmed.
- **Look.** Confirmed as stated, Low. Nicolay's figure, which I looked at, is a weatherboarded house with a hipped roof on a grid of piles, reached by two braced stairs. It shows no gun, embrasure or parapet. So it looks more like the fort's guard-house, or a later imagining, than the battery itself.

## Recomputations

**Method (mine, not the note's).**
- Positions are converted from WGS84 latitude, longitude and height to earth-centred coordinates and then to a local east-north-up frame at the camera. That gives exact straight-line geometry with the earth's curvature built in.
- Normal refraction is added as k·d²/2R with k = 0.13.
- Heights come from the IGN RGE ALTI service (NGF-IGN69, close to mean sea level), queried at my own points and along my own profiles.
- The eye is 3 m above NGF, roughly mid-tide in a boat. I also ran −1 m (low water) and +7 m (high water).
- The ramparts come from a minimum-area rectangle fitted to the OpenStreetMap city-wall lines and gate nodes (OSM API extract of 13 November 2026).
- Cameras are the note's A (50.7300 N 1.5650 E, about 2 km off the mouth) and B (50.7300 N 1.5780 E, about 1.1 km off), plus C (0.7 km off, 1.5841 E) and D (3 km off, 1.5514 E).

**Distances and bearings.** Every value in the note's 1.1 is reproduced within 0.1 km and 1°.
- From A: belfry 3.45 km at 99.2°; château 3.70 km at 97.6°; Tour d'Odre 2.28 km at 85.2°; wooden fort (assumed) 1.43 km at 81.0°; harbour mouth (assumed) 2.05 km at 90.0°; Crèche fort (assumed) 2.89 km at 43.1°; Fort de l'Heurt 2.47 km at 188.7°; column site 3.96 km at 71.8°; Mont Lambert 6.17 km at 103.1°.

- Platform of Fort de l'Heurt (about 12 m NGF): 0.20° and 0.18°.
- Column site ground: 1.27° and 1.62°.

**The belfry against the skyline.** I sampled the terrain every 50 m along the camera-to-belfry line, from the belfry out to 8 km beyond it, and took the highest angle.
- Camera C (0.7 km off): belfry top 2.31° against a skyline of 2.09°. At low water, 2.42° against 2.14°.
- Camera B (1.1 km off): 1.93° against 1.76°.
- Camera A (2 km off): 1.42° against 1.35°. At low water 1.49° against 1.40°; at high water 1.35° against 1.31°.
- Camera D (3 km off): 1.11° against 1.12°.
- The skyline points are on Mont Lambert's north-west shoulder: ground 140 m at 50.7218 N 1.6448 E (from A), 166 m at 50.7197 N 1.6507 E (from B).
- A point 20 m up the tower (72 m) is at 1.14° from A, against 1.35°. A point 25 m up (77 m) is at 1.66° from B, against 1.76°.
- So the lower two-thirds of the tower always stand against the hill. The top 4–8 m, and anything on top, stand against the sky from inside about 2.5 km.
- Caveat: a bare-earth model. Ten metres of trees on that shoulder would add about 0.1° to the skyline.

**The ramparts in plan.**
- Fitted rectangle 431 × 333 m. Corners at about N 50.72760 N 1.61438 E; E 50.72567 N 1.61800 E; S 50.72270 N 1.61407 E; W 50.72463 N 1.61046 E.
- The long faces run at 40°/220°. The gates sit on the faces: Dunes on NW, Neuve on NE, Gayole on SE, Degrés on SW.
- From A: N corner 94.4°, château 97.6° (the east corner 97.3°), belfry 99.2°, W corner 100.5°, S corner 103.2°.
- From B: N corner 95.9°, château 100.1°, belfry 102.6°, W corner 104.6°, S corner 107.7°.
- So the north-west face spans 6–9° to the left of the west corner, and the south-west face only about 3° to its right. The château stands behind the middle of the north-west face, left of the belfry.
- A terrain profile across the west corner rises from about 37 m to 49 m within 20 m: a 12 m wall, consistent with Bertrand's 40–50 feet.

**Other checks.**
- The D-shaped fort (half-round front of 32 m, straight sides, 32 m gorge, 48 m deep) has a perimeter of 146 m. An ellipse of 48 × 32 m has 127 m. The stated circumference is 150.75 m.
- 2,400 toises = 4.68 km. Fort de l'Heurt to the note's assumed Crèche fort = 5.1 km.
- Belfry to Mont Lambert: 2.8 km. The 1805 harbour mouth (assumed) to Mont Lambert: 4.2 km. Belfry to column site: 1.8 km at about 11°.
- Earth's curvature: 2.8 m drop at 6 km, 2.4 m with refraction.
- Sunset azimuth, 9–11 August 1805: about 294–296° true (geometric, declination about +15.3°). The camera, looking about 100°, has the sun 10–20° off straight behind it.

## Additional facts found

Facts the note does not have, useful to the painters. Each gives its source.

1. **The belfry had four corner turrets** on its square stage: Bertrand 1828, p. 97, says the 1544 picture shows them "as one sees them today". The town leaflet mentions a big corner turret and the bell chamber with two openings on each face. Medium.
2. **A spire on the octagon "until 1805"**: town leaflet *Focus: le beffroi et l'hôtel de ville* (2016), p. 3. The octagon "culminates at 35 m" and dates from 1734 (same). Low for the spire's state in August 1805.
3. **The harbour at low water.**
   - The foot of the west pierhead stood at +6 m above chart datum (Desbrière III, p. 143). A sand bank still stood at +5.5 m 150 m in front of the west pierhead (p. 148). Ships had to hug the east jetty (p. 144).
   - The Liane at low water was under 40 m wide, often under 20 m (p. 147). Three-fifths of the port lay above +4 m (p. 147). Grounding was "almost permanent" for the flotilla (p. 148).
   - The basin was a drying basin, *bassin d'échouage*, from the first plan (p. 149). High.
4. **Foreshore widths**: 700–800 m at the head of the bay, 450 m at la Crèche (Desbrière III, p. 141, from Desbrière's own hydrography of about 1900). Medium for 1805.
5. **The 1801 timber trestle** from the east jetty's head to the packet-boat quay: 221 m long, 5.80 m wide at the base, 7.29 m high above the tide-scale zero, 67 frames, with a towing walkway (Bertrand 1828, p. 292). High.
6. **A dam and a fascine causeway existed in August 1805.** The tide of 26 August 1805 breached "the fascine jetty that leads to Capécure" and cut a new channel "beyond the dam" (Sganzin, in Desbrière IV, p. 381). High.
7. **Night barriers**: a heavy chain across the harbour entrance at night (Constant, quoted by Nicolay, p. 82). Gun-boats used as powder hulks, with a chain across the Liane (Bertrand 1828, legend no. 5). Medium.
8. **Ammunition dumps**: "an enormous quantity of bombs and shot" piled at the foot of the Châtillon hill, and the château crammed with powder (Bertrand 1828, p. 337). Medium.
9. **The forts' ramps**: plank ramps on piers from the forts to the shore at both forts, damaged by the sea in August 1804 (Desbrière IV, pp. 383–384). Medium.
10. **Fort de l'Heurt's building pace**: 10 feet on 19 October 1803, 15½ feet on 1 November 1803, before the gale of 10 November (III, p. 161). The complete armament of the two forts was to be 22 36-pounders (IV, p. 384). High.
11. **The Tour d'Odre's low battery** received two 12-inch Gomer mortars in May–June 1804 and lost its four 6-inch howitzers. Le Portel's battery was rebuilt for four 36-pounders (III, p. 435). High.
12. **The redoubts round Boulogne** were first called for on 29 August 1805 (Corr. XI, No. 9159, pp. 159–160). The same letter orders all powder magazines moved into the upper town. That is after the scene's dates. High.
13. **The British frigate fired on by la Crèche on 15 July 1804** came in with two brigs (IV, p. 382). High.
14. **Decrès's hut** looked from afar "like an enormous extinguisher" (Nicolay, p. 22): a tall conical thatch. Medium.
15. **The barracks** held 1,500–1,800 men and had cellars 770 feet long (Bertrand 1829, p. 140). High.
16. **Saint-Nicolas's portal and nave** were by Giraud-Sannier (his biography, Bertrand 1829). Medium.
17. **On 25 August 1804** James counts 146 gun-vessels moored in line in the roads, 62 of them brigs and the rest mostly luggers (III, p. 229). That is for the flotilla note. High as James's count.
