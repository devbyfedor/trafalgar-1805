# Check of `flotilla.md`: the invasion flotilla at Boulogne, summer 1805

An independent, adversarial check of `flotilla.md` (same folder), claim by claim. I did not write that note and do not edit it. Every verdict says what I opened and where. Verdicts: **confirmed** (the source says it, at that place, and nothing better contradicts it), **corrected** (substantially right, but a page, figure, date, attribution or confidence needs changing), **unsupported** (the cited source does not say it, or I could not find it there or anywhere), **refuted** (the evidence says otherwise). Confidence uses the scale at the top of `docs/history-notes.md`. Kinds of evidence as in `flotilla.md`: **period** (1801–1825), **scholarly** (Desbrière, James, Clowes and the like), **modern**, **inference**.

Status: complete (13 November 2026, session 25).

Summary of the outcome: the note is careful and mostly right. Most quotations and figures are where it says they are. The main problems are the geometry of the camera against the line, a harbour that dried at low water more completely than the note says, prames and spacing presented more firmly than the 1805 evidence allows, the masthead pennant (its colours were fixed by law in 1794), and a handful of wrong page numbers.

## What the painter must change or know

1. **The camera at 700 m and the line in front of the town do not fit together.** In 1768–1805 the spring low-water line lay about 386 toises (750 m) seaward of the west jetty's head, and the wooden fort stood on it at the end of the 700 m fascine dike (Desbrière III pp. 142, 148; see 2.10). A boat anchored 700 m west of the pier heads therefore sits at about the spring low-water line. The town-and-defences note's coordinates put the wooden fort only some 250 m from such a camera. At low water on the spring tides of 9–11 August the boat would be in very shallow water. The moored line lay in the roads beyond the low-water line, in water deep enough for the craft at all states of the tide: in 1801 already 500 toises (975 m) off the entrance, and by 1805, according to Dumas, pushed further out. So it would lie beside the camera or seaward of it, not between the camera and the town. Two ways out: put the camera seaward of the line (roughly 2–2.5 km off the pier heads), or keep 700 m and accept that the craft in front of the town are not the line. Inshore of the line the documents put only some péniches (between the shore and the line's ends), bomb craft (just behind the line) and boats. This rests on inference from period and scholarly figures and on the town note's geometry. Low–Medium. The owner decides.
2. **No line is documented for 9–11 August 1805.** Confirmed (2.10). There was no craft in the roads on 3–6 August. After that the flotilla's reports have gaps and mention no line. The only August event on record is the sortie of 12 August, which left about noon. A moored line on 9–11 August is a reconstruction. Its documented model is the line of 10–21 July 1805: 27 chaloupes canonnières (brigs) and 27 bateaux canonniers (mostly luggers). **No prames are listed in any 1805 line.** Prames appear in the lines of 1804 and in the sortie of 12 August (five of them), so a prame in the moored line is a licence. Medium.
3. **At low water the harbour craft sat aground, even more completely than the note says.** Desbrière's reconstruction of the port concludes that, outside the narrow river channel, the flotilla's craft were aground almost all the time (III pp. 147–148). The semicircular basin was dug as a drying basin ("bassin d'échouage", III p. 149). The dam with a wooden flushing sluice was only decided on 21 April 1804 and held water upstream of itself (III p. 452). By the decrees of 26 and 30 August 1805 the craft not needed for sorties were to be kept "à flot au delà du barrage", afloat above the dam (Corr. XI Nos. 9137, 9164). So at low water the tiers of hulls along the quays and round the basin sat upright on mud and sand. The flat floors, and the prames' three keels, were built for this. The Liane ran between them as a stream about 20–40 m wide (III p. 147). No craft could pass the entrance around low water. High for drying; Medium for the details.
4. **The masthead pennant was mostly red.** The decree of 27 pluviôse an II (15 February 1794) that made the ensign three equal vertical bands, blue at the staff, also fixed the pennant: one fifth blue, one fifth white, three fifths red (Archives parlementaires LXXXV p. 78). On a chaloupe's pennant of about 11 m that is about 2 m of blue, 2 m of white and 7 m of red. This explains the red pennants in Lewis's 1804 view. Period. High for the proportions. Medium that the blue was next to the mast: the decree says "likewise", and the ensign has its blue at the staff.
5. **Spacing may be twice as wide as the note says.** The 45–55 m figure assumes a single row of about 150 craft. James has a double line in October 1804, and Saint-Haouen describes up to three rows. A row would then have about 80–110 m between craft. Napoleon's remark of September 1803, that two or three caïques could be placed in each interval between chaloupes canonnières, points to intervals of at least about 60 m. Low.
6. **The line also ran south of the harbour mouth.** Lewis's view of October 1804 shows the line on both sides of the harbour, and the roads were defined from la Crèche to Alprech. A line only north-east of the mouth is unsupported. Seen from a camera looking east, craft may also stand to the right of the harbour. Low.
7. **Heading at low water: probably bows to the north-north-east, to the left for a camera looking east.** Craft at anchor point their bows into the stream. Around local low water off this coast the stream should be the south-west-going one, so tide-rode craft would point roughly NNE. This is an inference from the general behaviour of the streams in the Strait and was not checked against a tidal-stream table. Low. Check it against the tide note.
8. **Chaloupes canonnières may show a few guns along the sides.** Besides the long 24-pounders at bow and stern and the howitzer, the orders of July and September 1803 gave chaloupes two to four 36-pounder carronades on the sides (Desbrière III pp. 88, 98). Willaumez speaks of guns "at its ends and in battery" (p. 123). Medium.
9. **Smaller corrections.** The bateau canonnier's side should be 0.8–1.2 m above the water including the washboard, as in the note's own table; its section 1 and 2.4 imply up to 1.5 m. Flags were of "toile", plain cloth, not specifically linen. The Lewis view shows October 1804, Keith's attack. Several page numbers are wrong: Corr. XI No. 9164 is on pp. 165–166, and the note's link to p. 155 opens another letter. Willaumez's entry Brigantin is on p. 107, and the Ville-de-Lille is on Desbrière III p. 222. The péniches lay inshore of the line's ends, not "at the ends and between it and the beach". Details below.

## Verdicts

### 2.1 Names, classes and counts

- **Class names and the "espèce" pairing.** Confirmed. Desbrière III p. 546 has the 2 December 1803 arrival of "canonnières ou bâtiments de 1re espèce, ainsi qu'on commence à les nommer" ([n577](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n577/mode/1up)). The period draught table facing p. 90 pairs prame, canonnière, bateau canonnier and péniche with the grande, 1re, 2e and 3e espèce exactly as the note says. I read it myself ([n104](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n104/mode/1up)). Period. High.
- **British names.** Confirmed. James III has "the prame, and the gun-vessel, or canonniere" on p. 216 and "prames, or corvettes" on p. 217. The first class are "rigged as brigs" (p. 217), and "a heavily-armed launch, or peniche (No. 267)" is on p. 233 ([p. 217](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/217/mode/1up)). The Naval Chronicle translation of Lacrosse's report uses "Gun-boats" for the line and "Pinnaces" for the British boats (XIV p. 339). Scholarly and period. High.
- **Forfait made inspector general on 24 May 1803.** Confirmed. The arrêté of 4 prairial an XI, printed in Desbrière III p. 82, gives him construction, repairs, purchases, classing and movements ([n93](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n93/mode/1up)). The caïques built to his plan are confirmed by Willaumez p. 123. The "modèle an XII" attribution comes from a modern product page and was not checked. Period. High for his role; Low for the attribution.
- **Spelling.** Confirmed. This is general French usage, and Desbrière writes "chaloupe canonnière" without a hyphen. High.
- **Return of 30 March 1804.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 558 ([n589](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n589/mode/1up)). Boulogne had 2 prames, 61 craft of the 1st kind, 158 of the 2nd, 118 péniches, 3 + 1 + 1 péniches for the First Consul, the admiral and the general-in-chief, 9 packet boats, caïques (number unreadable) plus one for the First Consul, and 2 bomb galliots. There were 474 transports: 45 for artillery, 190 for cavalry, 76 for battalions, 23 unclassed, 125 Newfoundland ships and 5 whalers. Period. High.
- **20 July 1805: 578 prames and gun craft, 526 transports.** Confirmed (James III p. 325). The type breakdown the note says is missing does exist for July 1805. James's appendix No. 27 (p. 496), taken from Dumas XII p. 304, gives Boulogne 13 prames, 11 bombardes, packet boats and avisos, 530 French gun-vessels (chaloupes and bateaux together), and 22 caïques, "corvettes de pêche" and péniches. James notes that the total of 578 does not quite agree with the items ([p. 496](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/496/mode/1up)). Scholarly from a period table. High for July 1805.
- **Decree of 30 August 1805: 6 prames, 72 chaloupes canonnières, 72 péniches.** Confirmed in substance. **Corrected** page: No. 9164 is on pp. **165–166** of Corr. XI, not 155–156 ([p. 165](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/165/mode/1up)). I checked the page image. The note's example link to p. 155 opens a different letter. Period. High.

### 2.2 Prame

- **Design: 110 × 25 × 8 pieds, ship rig ("gréement de corvette"), three keels, 12 × 24-pounders, 38 seamen and 120 soldiers.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 90 ([n101](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n101/mode/1up)). Desbrière gives the source as an official document of the Archives des Constructions navales, cited for the prices on the same page. James III pp. 216–217 agrees: 110 by 25 French feet, drawing 7–8 feet, rigged as a ship, 12 long 24-pounders, 38 sailors and upwards of 100 soldiers, and the first 20 ordered "each with stalls for 50 horses". I read the loaded draught on the table myself: 8½ pieds, within a quarter foot. Period and scholarly. High.
- **Origin: 5 July 1803, 50 boats on the model of Jornin's Foudroyant at Dunkirk, 12 × 24 and 60 horses, a leeboard each side.** Confirmed: Decrès to Forfait, Desbrière III pp. 87–89 ([n98](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n98/mode/1up)). Period. High.
- **"By 1804 there were 18 prames, 16 of them new (p. 90)."** **Corrected.** Page 90 says the actual armaments came down to 18 prames, 2 of them already existing, so 16 new, but it gives no date. "By 1804" is the note's addition. James's July 1805 table counts 17 prames: 13 at Boulogne, 3 at Ambleteuse and 1 at Calais. Medium.
- **Flat floors and three keels, so it could sit on the mud.** Confirmed, with a page added. Willaumez defines the prame (p. 476) as a large vessel built "à plates varangues", with a battery of heavy guns and two mortars on a platform in a hatchway before the mainmast. He calls such craft mobile fortresses for coast defence and refers to his plate A, fig. 14 ([n531](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n531/mode/1up)). The three keels are on Desbrière p. 90. Sitting on the mud is a sound inference: the harbour dried (2.10). Period. High.
- **The "Foudroyante" model (Musée national de la Marine 17 MG 18).** Confirmed as a type reference only. The English Wikipedia list of the Trianon models quotes the museum's record title: an artillery prame of 20 × 36-pounders, late 18th century. The list adds, unsourced, that it is a fictitious representative ship of Forfait's design ([Trianon model collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trianon_model_collection)). The museum's own catalogue refused automated access. Modern. Low for anything about the 1803 hull.
- **Guns: six a side (inference).** Unsupported as a fact, but fair as inference. Willaumez's "batterie" and James's "12 long 24-pounders" support a broadside battery. A dealer's description of Baugean's 1814 plate "Prame venant de mouiller" mentions about ten visible guns (see Additional facts). Medium-Low, as the note has it.
- **Slow; Ariadne "not above a third larger" than a prame.** Confirmed: James III p. 319 ([p. 319](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/319/mode/1up)). The same page has the Ville-de-Genève's mainmast cut away. Scholarly. Medium.
- **Names.** Mostly confirmed. **Corrected** in two places. (a) The Ville-de-Mayence also sailed with Ver Huell's division on 17 July 1805, with the Ville-d'Aix, Ville-d'Anvers and Ville-de-Genève and 32 first-class gun-vessels (James III p. 318). So in July 1805 it was with the Batavian flotilla, not at Boulogne. (b) The Ville-de-Lille at Dunkirk is on Desbrière III p. **222** (12 September 1803), not 223 ([n249](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n249/mode/1up)). Page 566 lists it with the Ville-de-Brest as the two prames at Boulogne, Ambleteuse and Wimereux on 29 June 1804. High.
- **Five prames came in at the end of March 1805; five went out on 12 August; six kept in the "bassin circulaire".** Confirmed: Desbrière IV p. 395 ([n52](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n52/mode/1up)); Dumas XII p. 76 ([n88](https://archive.org/details/prcisdesvnement10dumagoog/page/n88/mode/1up)), where I checked the date "12 août" on the page image; Corr. XI No. 9164, art. 4. Period. High for the decree; Medium for the sortie (French account).

### 2.3 Chaloupe canonnière

- **Design 76–80 × 17 × 5–6 pieds, brig rig; 3 × 24 and "a howitzer or mortar of 6–8 inches"; 22 seamen and 130 soldiers.** **Corrected** (wording). Desbrière III p. 90 says "I obusier de 6 à 8 pouces", a howitzer only. The mortar comes from James ("an 8-inch mortar", III p. 217). James also writes "an 8-inch howitzer" for Collet's gun-vessels of July 1805 (III p. 317). The draught table's "nouvelle construction" at 5½ pieds and "ancienne construction" at 8 pieds are confirmed: I read the scale myself. Linking the old type to the 1801 gun-brigs of 200–250 tons (James III p. 67) is a sound inference. Period. High for the design.
- **Rig: brig, with some brigantine or simplified rigs.** Confirmed: James III p. 217; Desbrière III p. 90 ("gréement de brick"); Desbrière III p. 88, where the First Consul wants the masting of chaloupes still to be built simplified, "de manière à éviter la complication d'une mâture de bâtiment carré". Willaumez p. 123 refers to pl. B fig. 10, "une canonnière de première espèce gréée en brigantin" ([n138](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n138/mode/1up)). **Corrected** page: the entry Brigantin is on p. **107**, not 106 ([n122](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n122/mode/1up)). Its content is as the note says: a smaller brig, after-yards shorter than the fore, flying topgallants at most, no royals, a mainsail that is always lowered, never a square main course. Hamelin's division of July 1805 had its six first-class gun-vessels "brig-rigged" (James III p. 324). The gaff mainsail in Lewis's view cannot be made out at the resolution available (1280 px). Period. Medium-High.
- **Guns and their places.** Confirmed, and more can be added. Desbrière III p. 88: the small chaloupes already built were to carry four 36-pounder carronades on the side and three 18- or 12-pounders fore and aft, and the 300 new ones three or four guns. The plan of 23 fructidor an XI (10 September 1803) arms every chaloupe with three 24-pounders and two 36-pounder carronades (III p. 98, [n113](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n113/mode/1up)). Willaumez p. 123: guns "tant à ses extrémités qu'en batterie", at the ends and along the side. Corr. No. 8925 (Desbrière IV p. 401): four chaloupes could fire only 8 guns. Placing the heavy guns at bow and stern is fair. A few side carronades should be allowed for. Period. Medium.
- **Oars and leeboards.** Confirmed: leeboards ordered for all chaloupes and bateaux, Desbrière III p. 88; Willaumez p. 123 on Swedish and Russian gun-sloops with 24 oars; Naval Chronicle XIV p. 482, "Gun-boats, which carry a number of oars". The note's account of French Wikipedia is accurate: 12 pairs, citing Roucaud 2019. Added: Napoleon's pencil note on the plan of 10 September 1803 gives a chaloupe canonnière 56 men for the oars, beside 36 for the guns and 4 officers (Desbrière III p. 99). That fits about 28 oars double-manned, like the bateau's 28 oars and 56 men. Collet's chaloupes "swept out" of Granville in July 1805 (James III p. 317). Period. Medium for the oars being used; Low for their number.
- **Garrisons in the roads: 50 men per chaloupe, 40 per bateau; six canonnières under 450 men.** Confirmed: Soult on 15 April 1804, Desbrière III p. 559 ([n590](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n590/mode/1up)). Napoleon, Corr. No. 8925 (Desbrière IV p. 401), counts six "canonnières" at no more than 450 men, but that six is four chaloupes plus the two corvettes canonnières. Period. High.

### 2.4 Bateau canonnier

- **Design 60 × 14 × 4½ pieds, lugger rig; one 24 and a howitzer or field gun; 6 seamen, 100 soldiers, 2 horses; loaded draught 3½ pieds.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 90 and the draught table, where I read the péniche and bateau line at 3½ pieds myself. Period. High.
- **A 24-pounder forward and a field piece aft; the plan of 10 September 1803; 40 boats with a bronze 12 and 100 with a light bronze 24.** Confirmed: James III p. 217; Desbrière III pp. 100–101 ([n115](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n115/mode/1up)). The field pieces were to stand at the stern on their own carriages to defend the boat. Forty boats carried a bronze 12-pounder field gun instead of the navy's 24, and 100 a light bronze 24 with its carriage aft. The note's "iron 24" is a reasonable gloss: the text says only that the 24 was supplied by the navy. Desbrière III p. 88 has the July 1803 order for a field piece "sur ses roues". Period. High.
- **Rig: lug, some schooners; two or three masts.** Confirmed and refined. James III p. 217: "some rigged as schooners, and some as luggers". James III p. 324: Hamelin's ten second-class boats of July 1805 were "two or three brigs, the remainder luggers". The number of masts is still undocumented. Medium.
- **Washboard of 18 inches (49 cm), removable, pierced for the oars, on the 100 Dunkirk boats and all new ones; leeboards.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 88 ([n99](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n99/mode/1up)). The text says the raised planking is to be crossed by the oars and easily taken down. Period. High.
- **Freeboard.** **Corrected** (internal inconsistency). Section 1 has "side under a metre plus an 18-inch washboard", and 2.4 "a little under a metre ... with the washboard above it". Together these allow up to about 1.5 m. The note's own table has 0.8–1.2 m including the washboard. From a loaded draught of 3½–4½ pieds and an undocumented depth, the hull side would be only about 0.3–0.7 m, plus the 0.49 m washboard. Use the table's 0.8–1.2 m. Inference. Low.
- **28 oars, 56 men.** Confirmed: Napoleon's pencil note, Desbrière III p. 101 ([n116](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n116/mode/1up)). French Wikipedia's 14 pairs (Roucaud 2019) is reported correctly. Period. High.

### 2.5 Péniche

- **Design 60 × 10 × 3½ pieds, lug; a 6-inch howitzer and a 4-pounder, or a Prussian howitzer, or an 8-inch mortar; 5 seamen and 66 soldiers.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 90. Period. High.
- **Willaumez p. 447.** Confirmed ([n494](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n494/mode/1up)). The Boulogne péniches were heavy and carvel-built ("à franc bord"). The first kind measured 60 pieds stem to sternpost, 53 pieds 8 pouces on the keel, 10 in beam and 5 deep, with 40 oars and 40 swivels or espingoles. The second kind measured 42 × 8½ × 3 with 28 oars. The coastguard boats were 50 × 10 × 5 with a 12-pounder and 12 espingoles, lug-rigged, with oars "à couples". "Double-banked" is a reasonable but not certain reading of "à couples". Period. High.
- **Shape and rig from the plate facing Desbrière III p. 90.** Confirmed ([n102](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n102/mode/1up)). The plate "Bateaux de 3e espèce", from the Archives de la Guerre, shows a three-masted péniche under lug sails with a jib on a bowsprit and the mizzen sheeted to an outrigger beyond the stern. A long pennant flies at the mainmast head. A two-masted péniche is half hidden by the fold. The details drawing has an open hull with mast steps for three masts and a round-bilged, fairly flat midship section. Its left end is cut off by the fold, so the count of about fifteen thwarts or tholes is approximate. My own measurement of the three-masted péniche gives a mainmast head 0.47–0.48 of the hull length above the waterline, as the note says. The same drawing shows the gunwale about 0.045 of the length above water, about 0.9 m for 19.5 m. That is more than the note's 0.5 m, but the drawing is not to scale. Period. Medium-High for the look; Low for the freeboard figure.
- **Oars: Napoleon's 64 men; Willaumez's 40 oars.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 101; Willaumez p. 447. French Wikipedia's 13 pairs is reported correctly. High that they were primarily rowing boats.
- **Role in the roads.** **Corrected** (wording and attribution). The ordre du jour of 16 April 1804 (Desbrière III p. 560, [n591](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n591/mode/1up)) puts picket péniches one cable ahead of the line, and "les autres péniches entre la terre et les extrémités de la ligne": the other péniches between the shore and the ends of the line, that is, inshore of its ends. They were not "at the ends of the line and between it and the beach". Bruix on 13 April 1804 had pickets at night "ahead of and on the wings of our line" (p. 559). The ordre du jour does not mention Prussian howitzers. The péniches "à obusiers prussiens" come from Desbrière IV p. 397 (June–July 1805) and Dumas XI p. 47 (August 1804), and the long-range howitzers of 12 August 1805 from Dumas XII p. 76. Period. High.
