# Boulogne from the sea: light, weather and the Royal Navy's watch (August 1805)

Research note for scene 2, beat 1 ("Boulogne from the sea"). Topic: the evening sun over the roads of Boulogne in August 1805, the moon, the weather, the sea and the tide, and the British ships that watched the port. The confidence scale (High, Medium, Low) is the one at the top of `docs/history-notes.md`. Each fact says what it rests on: **period** evidence (letters, reports and journals of the time), **later scholarly** works (James, Desbrière, Marshall), **modern** works and web pages (leads, or modern measurements), or **our own computation and inference**. The computations were run in a scratch folder outside the repository; the method is given in full so they can be repeated.

Status: complete (session 24 helper, light and watch). Not yet checked by an independent reader.

Conventions:
- Times are **local apparent time (LAT)** unless marked UT or LMT. In 1805 French public clocks still showed apparent (true) solar time; Paris moved to mean time only in 1816 or 1826 (Bianchi 2021 argues 1826 for public clocks), so a time in a French document of August 1805 is LAT. British naval logs also kept apparent time. At Boulogne, LMT = UT + 6.4 min; LAT = LMT + equation of time (−5.8 min on 3 August, −4.2 min on 15 August, −1.0 min on 28 August 1805).
- Bearings and azimuths are **true**, measured from north through east. "Left" and "right" are for a viewer anchored in the roads and **looking east** at the shore: left is north, right is south, behind is west.
- The sample viewpoint used for bearings is **50.727 N, 1.555 E**, in the roads about 2.3 km west of the harbour mouth. Any point 1.5–3 km out gives the same picture within a few degrees.
- Heights of the tide are on Desbrière's hydrographic zero for Boulogne (0.30 m below the equinoctial low water), the zero of the ponts-et-chaussées tide scale he prints.

## For the painter

1. **The sun is behind the camera, over the viewer's left shoulder.** It sets at azimuth 299.5° on 3 August, 293.7° on 15 August and 286.5° on 28 August 1805 (WNW, 17–30° north of west), so for a viewer looking east it stands behind and 17–30° to the left of straight behind. An hour before sunset it is about 8° up, an hour and a half before about 13° up. Sunset (upper limb): **19:37 LAT on 3 August, 19:17 on 15 August, 18:54 on 28 August**. High (two independent computations and the US Naval Observatory agree within a minute).
2. **Everything that faces the sea is lit square-on.** The cliffs north of the harbour face due west (about 273°), the shore from the harbour to Le Portel faces north-west (about 310°). In the last hour the low sun strikes these faces, the west walls and roof slopes of huts, the west faces of forts and town walls, and the sides of hulls and sails turned toward the viewer within about 10–35° of square: warm, frontal, almost shadowless as seen from the roads. Vertical faces turned to the sun are the brightest things in the view; flat ground, plateau tops and the sea get only grazing light. High for the geometry; Medium for the coast azimuths (modern map, 1805 cliffs essentially the same).
3. **Shadows fall away from the viewer and a little to the right**, toward 107–120° (ESE). They are long: a 3 m hut casts a shadow of about 35 m with the sun 5° up and about 85 m with it 2° up, so seen from above (during the camera's dive) the hut rows on the plateaux are striped with long dark bands pointing ESE; seen from the water the shadows are hidden behind the objects. The cliffs on the left (Tour d'Ordre plateau) are lit a little from the viewer's left, which models them; the Outreau shore on the right is lit almost exactly from behind the viewer, which flattens it. High (geometry).
4. **Golden light and dusk.** The best-founded warm light is 45–90 minutes before sunset (sun 5–12° up): about 18:05–18:50 LAT on 3 August, 17:45–18:30 on 15 August, 17:25–18:10 on 28 August. The last half-hour (sun below about 4°) turns orange to red; the sea-level shore loses the sun at sunset and the cliff tops and camps (60–70 m up) keep the last light for about 1½–2 minutes more. Civil dusk ends 34–39 minutes after sunset (20:16, 19:54, 19:28 LAT), nautical dusk 77–90 minutes after (21:07, 20:40, 20:11). High.
5. **The sky in front is the cool side.** Looking east, the viewer faces the anti-solar sky: clear blue to lavender above the heights, and after sunset a blue-grey band (the Earth's shadow) rising behind the town and camps with a pink band (the Belt of Venus) above it, centred about 107–120°, just right of straight ahead. There is no sun glitter on the water in front; the sea ahead mirrors the cool eastern sky and, near the shore, the warm-lit cliffs. The warmest, brightest sky and the glitter path are behind the camera. High for the directions (geometry); Medium for the appearance (general atmospheric optics).
6. **A full moon can rise over the town at sunset.** Full moon was on 10 August 1805 (11:57 UT). On 9, 10 and 11 August the moon rose in the ESE (azimuth 116°, 109°, 101°) 38 and 17 minutes before sunset and 2 minutes after it: straight ahead, low over the heights and the town, in the pink anti-twilight band. On 3 August a gibbous moon (64 %) stood 14° up in the south (behind the viewer's right shoulder); on 15 August there was no moon at sunset (it rose at 20:35 in the ENE); on 28 August a thin crescent (20 %) hung 7° up in the WSW behind the viewer. High (computed, checked against the US Naval Observatory).
7. **August 1805 was changeable.** Napoleon arrived on 3 August in bad weather with not one craft in the roads (Desbrière); on 20 August he wrote that the weather was very inconstant and it rained a great deal at Boulogne. The London weather journal for the month has rain on 1–3, 8–9 and 19–21 August and fair or fine days on 6–7, 10–14, 23–25, 27 and 29 August, with light westerly to south-westerly winds most days and spells of north-east wind. A fair evening with some broken cloud is well founded; **10–14 August** is the best-founded fine spell (and has the full moon). Medium.
8. **Smoke drifts away and to the left on the commonest wind.** In summer the wind in the Strait blows from the south-west about half the time and from the north-east about a fifth of the time (Météo-France statistics). On a south-west wind the camps' kitchen smoke streams **away from the viewer and to the left** (toward the north-east, along the line of the heights); on a north-east wind it comes **toward the viewer and to the right**, out over the roads; on a west wind it goes straight away and looks foreshortened. On a calm summer evening, after the afternoon sea breeze dies, smoke rises and spreads into a flat layer over the plateaux and the Liane valley. Medium for the frequencies; Low for any given evening; the calm-evening layer is inference.
9. **The sea in the roads.** In summer: a low south-westerly swell and short wind waves, usually well under 1 m; whitecaps only when the wind passes force 4. The roads were open from west round to north-east and became untenable in a westerly or northerly blow; the flood stream ran north-north-east along the coast at up to about 4 knots. Medium.
10. **The tide changes the whole foreground.** Springs rose about 7.9 m, neaps about 4.4 m; high water at full and change came at about 10½ h. On spring-tide evenings (**about 13–16 and 26–29 August 1805**) low water fell close to sunset: a strip of wet sand and rock **450–800 m wide** lay bare in front of the town and cliffs, the Liane harbour drained to mud with its craft aground on it, and only the craft in the roads floated. On evenings three to five days after the quarters (**about 5–7 and 20–22 August**) high water came near sunset and the sea stood at the foot of the cliffs and dunes. Medium (period tide data; the dates of a given state are good to about ±45 minutes).
11. **The British watched in plain sight, but behind the viewer.** In summer 1805 Rear-Admiral Billy Douglas (flag in the 50-gun Leopard) watched the flotilla, with Captain Edward Owen's frigate Immortalité (36) leading an inshore squadron of one or two frigates, a 20-gun ship, brig-sloops, gun-brigs and a cutter. They lay at anchor or cruised 3–5 miles off the port, mostly to the north-west, with a main body further out (about 10 miles north-west in 1804). From a boat in the roads looking east they are **behind the left shoulder** (bearing about 320–335°, 6–17 km), outside the frame. A British sail can enter the frame only at the far left, standing in off Wimereux or the Pointe de la Crèche, where they did come in to fight. The likeliest single sail close in: a two-masted gun-brig or brig-sloop; a three-masted frigate further out. Medium for their presence in August 1805; Low for any position on a given evening.
12. **England cannot be seen from the roads.** The cliffs of Dover and Folkestone are 46–48 km away and about 100–115 m below the sea horizon for an eye 3 m above the water; at most the highest downs could show as a hair-line 0.03–0.06° tall on a rare clear day. From the heights of Boulogne or Ambleteuse (50–55 m) the white cliffs and Dover Castle show on clear days as a thin pale line 0.1–0.2° tall, **behind the viewer's left shoulder** (bearing 320–345°, NW to NNW). High for the geometry; Medium for the period testimony.
13. **Caution on the lines of craft in the roads.** Desbrière found no craft at all in the roads from Napoleon's arrival on 3 August to at least 6 August 1805, and no report of a line afterwards; Napoleon's own letters claim that the flotilla attacked the British cruisers before 13 August and that a division fought off Cap Gris-Nez on 20 August. Lines of craft moored in the roads are documented for 1804 and for spells up to 21 July 1805. Low for "lines in the roads" in August 1805 (the flotilla research decides the layout; this note only flags the conflict).

## 1. The sun on August 1805 evenings

### 1.1 Method

- **Place.** Harbour mouth of the Liane, taken as 50.727 N, 1.588 E. The exact point hardly matters: moving it to 50.75 N 1.55 E or to 50.70 N 1.62 E changes the times by under 15 seconds and the azimuths by under 0.05°. Norie's 1805 tables put Boulogne at 50°43′30″ N, 1°36′55″ E (Norie 1805, latitudes table; period), which is the town; the jetties of 1805 were shorter than today's, but the figure is good enough here.
- **Method A: NOAA solar calculator algorithm** (after Meeus, *Astronomical Algorithms*, ch. 25): geometric mean longitude and anomaly of the sun, equation of centre, apparent longitude with nutation and aberration, mean obliquity with the nutation correction, declination, equation of time, then hour angle, altitude and azimuth. Computed for the Gregorian dates of 1805 themselves (Julian Day from Meeus ch. 7), with ΔT = 13.5 s for 1805. Sunset is the upper limb on the horizon with standard refraction, i.e. the centre at −0.833° true altitude; civil and nautical dusk are the centre at −6° and −12°. Apparent altitudes add refraction by Saemundsson's formula (10 °C, 1010 hPa).
- **Method B: Astronomy Engine 2.1.19** (D. Cross; VSOP87-based, with its own ΔT model): `SearchRiseSet` (upper limb, 34′ refraction plus the true semi-diameter), `SearchAltitude` for −6°, −12° and −18°, `Equator` and `Horizon` for positions. Independent of method A.
- **External check: US Naval Observatory**, Astronomical Applications API (rise, set and twilight for one day) and the altitude–azimuth table, for 50.727 N, 1.588 E, on the same three dates.
- **Result of the cross-check.** Methods A and B agree within 4 seconds of time and 0.01° for every value. The USNO rounds to the minute and agrees with both: sunset 19:36, 19:15 and 18:48 UT; end of civil twilight 20:15, 19:52 and 19:23 UT. USNO's tabulated altitudes and azimuths interpolate to our values within 0.05° (for example 15 August at 18:30 UT: USNO 6.0°, 285.1°; ours 6.0°, 285.1°).
- **The 1805 values differ a little from today's.** The equation of time on 3 August 1805 was −5.8 min (sun slow), about 0.3 min less than for the same date now, because the calendar and the perihelion have shifted slightly; this is why the computation was run for 1805 itself.

### 1.4 Colour of the light (general optics; inference)

- With the sun 8–13° up (60–90 minutes before sunset) the direct light is warm gold and still strong; shadows are long but the sky fill is bright.
- Below about 5° the direct light turns orange; below about 2° it is orange-red and weaker, and the lit faces glow against a sky that is darker than them.
- Summer haze over the Channel lowers the contrast of anything more than a few kilometres away and tints it toward the sky colour; the town at 4 km and the heights at 3–5 km are softened, the craft at 0.1–1 km are not.
- After sunset the eastern sky shows the Earth's shadow (a blue-grey band rising from the horizon) under the Belt of Venus (pink), for roughly 20–30 minutes; the land ahead loses its colour within a few minutes of sunset.

Confidence: Medium (general atmospheric optics, not specific to 1805).

## 2. Geometry: the coast, the viewer and the light

### 2.1 Which way the coast faces

Modern map positions (OpenStreetMap; modern): Fort de la Tour d'Odre site 50.7324 N 1.5995 E; Fort de la Crèche 50.7488 N 1.6009 E; Pointe de la Rochette (Wimereux) 50.7770 N 1.6055 E; Wimereux 50.7697 N 1.6119 E; Fort de l'Heurt (on its rock off Le Portel) 50.7081 N 1.5598 E; Alprech lighthouse 50.6985 N 1.5626 E; Équihen-Plage 50.6794 N 1.5717 E; the belfry of the upper town 50.7251 N 1.6132 E.

- **North of the harbour** (Tour d'Ordre to the Crèche and on to Wimereux) the coast runs almost due north (003–006°), so the cliffs face **west, about 273–276°**.
- **From the harbour south-west to Le Portel** (Outreau) the shore runs about 220°, so it faces **north-west, about 310°**.
- **South of Alprech** toward Équihen the coast runs about 163°, facing **WSW, about 253°**.
- Between the harbour and Wimereux the coast is a shallow bay: from the roads the shore wraps round the viewer from about 040° (Wimereux) through east to about 170° (Le Portel). The open sea lies only behind, to the north-west, west and south-west.

Confidence: Medium (modern map; the cliffs have retreated a little since 1805 and the harbour front has been rebuilt, but the orientation of the coast is the same).

### 2.2 The sun against the coast

In the hour before sunset the sun moves from azimuth 275–288° to 287–300°.

- **North cliffs (facing 273°)**: the sun is 0–27° off square; they are lit full-face to the last minute. Seen from the roads (from the WSW) they are seen a little obliquely, lit from the viewer's left-behind, so their folds model.
- **Outreau shore (facing 310°)**: the sun is 10–35° off square, lit almost frontally; seen from the roads the light comes from straight behind the viewer, so it reads flat.
- **The upper town** (on its hill about 60 m up, about 4 km from the sample viewpoint at bearing 093°): its west-facing walls, ramparts and the belfry's west face are lit; its east sides are in shadow, unseen.
- **Plateau tops and camps**: horizontal ground gets grazing light only (the sine of 2–10°, a tenth or less of full light), so the ground goes dim and the vertical faces (hut fronts, gable ends, walls) stand out bright. Shadows point to 105–120°.
- **Harbour mouth**: the Liane opens to the north-west, so the low sun shines up the estuary.

Bearings from the sample viewpoint (50.727 N, 1.555 E; inference from the map positions above): Wimereux 040° at 6.2 km; Fort de la Crèche 053° at 4.0 km; the Colonne site at Terlincthun 070° at 4.7 km; the Tour d'Ordre plateau 079° at 3.2 km; the harbour mouth about 087° at 2.5 km; the belfry 093° at 4.1 km; the Outreau plateau about 121° at 3.3 km; Fort de l'Heurt 171° at 2.1 km. So a 60–70° frame centred on east runs from about the Crèche on the left to Outreau on the right; Le Portel and Fort de l'Heurt are almost abeam on the right, outside it.

**Does "the low sun behind the camera, in the west" hold?** Yes, more exactly in the **west-north-west, behind and 17–30° to the left** of straight behind, 8° up an hour before sunset. High.

### 2.3 How high the shore stands in the view (inference)

From an eye 3 m above the water, a plateau edge 60–70 m high at 3 km stands only about 1.1–1.3° above the water line, and the upper town (60 m, plus walls and belfry) at 4 km about 1–1.5°. The plateau surface itself is never seen from the water. From 2.5–3 km out, a hut 3 m tall on level ground more than about 130–160 m behind a 60 m cliff edge is hidden by the edge (an object 5 m tall beyond about 220–260 m); huts show only near the edge or where the ground rises inland, while smoke shows above. This bears on the camps' layout (another note's topic); it is plain geometry. Confidence: High for the angles, Medium for the heights (modern figures: the Météo-France semaphore on the Tour d'Ordre plateau stands at 73 m; French Wikipedia gives about 60 m for the upper town and cliff heights of 6–33 m from Wimereux to the Crèche).

## 3. Weather, smoke and sea

### 3.1 Prevailing winds (modern climatology and period statements)

- **Modern, Météo-France (Le Cam and Baraer 2012).** In the Pas-de-Calais the relief on both shores channels the wind into two dominant directions, **south-west and north-east**. South-west winds blow all year, from 26 % of observations in winter to **48 % in summer (June–August)**, all speeds together; in summer, south-west winds above force 5 make up 27 % of the observations. North-east winds are less frequent, come mostly with anticyclones and can last several days, from 14 % (autumn) to **18 % in summer**. Coastal sea breezes set in from spring to early autumn and temporarily strengthen the wind. The strong summer winds at the Boulogne station (1981–2010) come from about 220°. (Offshore rose at about 50.9 N 1.3 E and the Boulogne station; modern.) https://sextant.ifremer.fr/documentation/dcsmm/documents/Evaluation_initiale/MMN/EE/MMN_EE_01_Climatologie_marine.pdf (pp. 3–4).
- **Modern, Météo-France station Boulogne semaphore** (62160001, on the Tour d'Ordre plateau, 73 m; normals 1991–2020): August mean wind 4.9 m/s (about force 3), mean maximum temperature 20.7 °C, mean minimum 15.3 °C, 63 mm of rain on 8.7 days, and gusts of 16 m/s or more on 6.9 days. https://donneespubliques.meteofrance.fr/FichesClim/FICHECLIM_62160001.pdf
- **Later scholarly, Desbrière (1902)**, on the roads of Boulogne ("rade de Saint-Jean"): east and south-east winds are rare, rarer in summer than in winter; **west and south-west winds blow two days in five** and generally make the roads untenable; north and north-west winds, more violent, are about as frequent (Desbrière III 141). https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n158/mode/1up
- **Later scholarly, James (1837; 1886 printing)**: in the Channel a breeze "in all likelihood, blows either up or down, and not across" (James III 328): the same south-west / north-east pattern. https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/n343/mode/1up

Confidence: Medium (modern statistics; the 1805 climate was cooler, the late Little Ice Age, but the wind regime is set by geography and the same pattern shows in the 1805 records below).

### 3.2 Smoke drift as seen from the roads (inference from the winds)

For a viewer looking east (the wind is named for where it comes from; smoke goes the opposite way):

| Wind | Smoke goes toward | As seen from the roads | How often in summer |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| South-west (225°) | north-east (045°) | **away and to the left**, along the line of the northern heights; plumes lean left | the commonest, about half of the time |
| West (270°) | east (090°) | straight away; plumes foreshortened, hard to read | common |
| North-west (315°) | south-east (135°) | away and to the right | less common |
| North-east (045°) | south-west (225°) | **toward the viewer and to the right**, out over the roads and the craft | about a fifth of the time, often in fine anticyclonic spells |
| Calm, or sea breeze dying at sunset | up, then spreading | columns rising and flattening into a horizontal layer over the plateaux and the Liane valley | fine summer evenings (inference) |

The afternoon sea breeze on this west-facing coast blows from the west, veering toward the north-west as the day goes on, and dies toward sunset (general sea-breeze behaviour; Le Cam and Baraer note the summer breezes). So on a fine evening the smoke of the evening meal may first lean inland (away, slightly right), then rise more vertically and lie in a layer as the air calms. Confidence: Medium for the directions, Low for any given evening.

### 3.3 The weather of August 1805: the record

**Period: Napoleon at Boulogne.**
- 3 August: he arrived to bad weather. Desbrière, from the flotilla's chief of staff Lafond: the weather had improved in the last days of July but had turned bad again, and on 3 August "il n'y avait pas un bâtiment en rade"; it stayed so until at least 6 August (Desbrière IV 398; later scholarly, from period correspondence). https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n55/mode/1up
- 20 August, to Cambacérès: the weather is very inconstant and it rains a great deal here ("le temps est très-inconstant. Il pleut ici beaucoup"; short quotation as evidence) (Corr. XI No. 9097, p. 104). https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/n113/mode/1up
- A key-word search of his Boulogne letters of 3 August–2 September 1805 (Corr. XI, OCR text) found no other remark on the weather.

**Period: the Royal Society's meteorological journal, London (Somerset House), August 1805**, readings at 7 a.m. and 2 p.m. (Phil. Trans. 96, 1806, separately paginated, pp. 16–17). https://archive.org/details/philtrans09860936/page/n15/mode/1up and https://archive.org/details/philtrans09860936/page/n16/mode/1up . The London rain of 2–3 and 19–20 August matches Desbrière's bad weather at Napoleon's arrival and Napoleon's "much rain" of 20 August. Temperatures at 2 p.m. ran 61–78 °F (16–26 °C), at 7 a.m. 53–63 °F (12–17 °C). The wind strengths are 1 or 2 on the journal's own small scale (no key is printed with the 1805 tables): light to moderate air throughout the month. Confidence: High for the London record, Medium as a guide to Boulogne.

**Period, nearby dates (James, from British logs and French accounts)**: 19 July 1804, a strong NNE wind raised a heavy sea in the roads and wrecked part of the flotilla (James III 227); 25 August 1804, a north-easterly wind (III 229); 17 July 1805, a light north-east wind, after "north-west gales" that had kept the Batavian craft in Dunkirk (III 318–319); 23 July 1805, at Fécamp, foggy weather and a fine south-west wind (III 323).

### 3.4 The sea in the roads

- **Modern (Le Cam and Baraer 2012):** waves come mainly from the south-west and north-east, the dominant winds; rough seas (2.5–4 m) are rare in the Pas-de-Calais (under 5 % of the year); the south-west swell in the Channel averages 1–1.5 m in height (whole year); the July map of mean wave height shows the eastern Channel near the Strait in its low classes (well under 1.5 m). Tidal streams against the wind make a short, steep sea. Medium.
- **Later scholarly (Desbrière III 141):** the roads ("rade de Saint-Jean"), 5–15 m deep, on marl and mud of good holding where anchors were often lost, were completely open; the Bassure de Baas bank offshore was too deep to break the swell; the **flood stream set north-north-east toward the English coast at up to about 4 knots**. Medium.
- **Period (Saint-Haouen, the flotilla's naval chief, report of 3 August 1805, printed by Desbrière IV 398–399):** the roads were beaten by every wind; craft caught there by a storm, or even by an ordinary gale from anywhere between west and north-east, were in danger; prames and chaloupes canonnières were put in the first line because they rode out a blow best; the bottom rose gently toward the land and held well. https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n55/mode/1up
- **Period (Napoleon's note of 13 September 1805, Corr. XI No. 9209):** the flotilla could not get out on a single tide nor ride out a gale in the roads of Boulogne (as summarised by Desbrière IV, ch. on the flotilla's end).
- **Period (letter "J. X.", off Boulogne, 31 July 1804, Naval Chronicle XIV 481–482):** the French craft moored outside "suffer more from the sea (when the wind shifts suddenly and blows on them) than from our Ships"; the writer had spent "many calm nights" at anchor off Boulogne. https://archive.org/details/navalchronicleco14londiala/page/n510/mode/1up

**For the scene:** an August evening of light wind gives wind waves of a few tens of centimetres on a long low swell from the west or south-west; craft at anchor pitch gently. Medium.

**How anchored craft lie (inference):** a vessel at anchor lies head to wind or to the stream. The streams run along the coast (NNE on the flood, SSW on the ebb), so with little wind the craft in the roads lie roughly parallel to the shore, broadside to a viewer looking east, bows to the left (NNE) on the ebb and to the right (SSW) on the flood; on a fresh south-west breeze, bows to the right and toward the viewer. Low.

## 4. The tide

### 4.1 Range and the harbour

- **Heights (later scholarly, Desbrière III 142, from the ponts-et-chaussées tide scale at Boulogne):** mean high water springs 8.90 m, mean low water springs 1.04 m (**spring range 7.86 m**); mean high water neaps 7.06 m, mean low water neaps 2.64 m (**neap range 4.42 m**); equinoctial high water about 9.3 m (the OCR of this figure is unclear); quay crowns 10.56 m. https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n159/mode/1up . High (the scale is of Desbrière's day, but the tide of a coast like this changes little in a century; not checked against modern SHOM figures here).
- **The harbour dried.** The Liane harbour was so shallow that even the flotilla's shallow craft were almost always aground (Desbrière III 141). At neap tides the canonnières could not cross the bar at all and the bateaux and péniches only for about two hours; at springs the canonnières had about three hours and the smaller craft four (Desbrière III 145, after the engineer Sauvage). In spring 1804 the engineer Sganzin reckoned that for six or seven days out of fifteen the flotilla in the upper harbour would lie aground and could make no movement (Desbrière III 145; period report). With winds from west through north to north-east, fresh, no craft could get out except by warping; with strong winds from WSW through south to ESE the port was closed to craft coming in (Desbrière III 144, from a period engineers' report). https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n161/mode/1up and https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n162/mode/1up . High.
- **The foreshore.** At low water the sands (*estran*) were 700–800 m wide at the head of the bay and still 450 m wide at the Crèche, which kept the line of moored craft far from the shore batteries (Desbrière III 141). Captain Owen to Lord Keith, off Boulogne, 7 October 1803: a long flat dries for about half a mile from the pier at low water, and there are seven fathoms about a mile off the pier head (Keith Papers III, NRS 1955, pp. 41–42; period letter via a modern edition and web page). https://www.historyofwar.org/sources/napoleonic/nrs1955/1_1_034.html . High for the 1803–05 state.
- **Forts on the sands.** In 1804 there were seven or eight forts built on the sands at low-water mark, and mortar beds over which the tide flowed, the mortars being brought to them when the sea left them dry (James III 229; later scholarly). At low water these stood on bare sand or rock; at high water in the sea. https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/n244/mode/1up

### 4.2 When high and low water fell in August 1805

- **Period:** Norie's *Epitome* (1805), Table XLI, gives the time of high water at full and change of the moon (the "establishment") at **Boulogne 10 h 30 m**, Ambleteuse 11 h 0 m, Calais 11 h 30 m (rise 18 ft), Dieppe 10 h 30 m, Dover about 11 h (Norie 1805, pp. 248–249). https://archive.org/details/norie-1805-a-new-and-complete-epitome-of-practical-navigation/page/n636/mode/1up . Medium (period tables of establishments were rounded; an error of 15–30 minutes is likely).
- **Method (our computation):** an M2 + S2 tide driven by the actual hour angles of the moon and sun at Boulogne in 1805 (Astronomy Engine), with M2 = 3.07 m and S2 = 0.86 m (from Desbrière's spring and neap ranges), the M2 phase set so that high water on the days of full and new moon falls 10½ hours after the moon's transit (Norie), and the age of the tide (springs lagging syzygy) taken as 1½ days ("a day or two", Norie's text). Varying the age between 1 and 2 days and the establishment between 10 h and 11 h moves the times by up to about 30–35 minutes. Shallow-water distortion of the tide curve is ignored. Confidence: Medium for the pattern, Low for any time closer than ±45 minutes.
- **Results (LAT; heights on Desbrière's zero):**

**What it meant for the craft:** in the harbour, at low water, the craft sat on the mud of the drained Liane, upright or heeled, packed in tiers (Owen in 1803 saw them three abreast); they could move only for two to four hours round high water, and at neaps the heavier ones not at all. In the roads they floated at all states of the tide, in 5–15 m of water, on two anchors. On a spring-tide evening the water's edge stood 450–800 m further out than at high water, so from the roads the foreground beyond the anchored craft would be a broad sheet of wet, reflecting sand with the forts on the sands standing dry; on a neap-plus-three-days evening the sea reached the foot of the cliffs and dunes and the harbour was full. High for the mechanism, Medium for the dates.

## 5. The Royal Navy's watch on Boulogne

### 5.1 Command

- **Lord Keith**, commander-in-chief in the Downs and North Sea from 1803: in July 1805 "Lord Keith, with Vice-Admiral Holloway, are stationed in the Downs", Rear-Admiral T. M. Russell cruised off the Texel, and **"Rear-Admiral Douglas is employed watching the Flotilla at Boulogne"** (Naval Chronicle XIV 68, the fleet list of the June–July 1805 number; period). The same list (checked on the page image) gives the 50-gun **Leopard** with Rear-Admiral Billy Douglas and Captain Richard Raggett, and Keith's flag in the St Albans (64), with Holloway's in the Utrecht (64). https://archive.org/details/navalchronicleco14londiala/page/n87/mode/1up . High for July 1805; Medium for August (no change is recorded in what was read).
- **Captain Edward W. C. R. Owen, frigate Immortalité (36)**, commanded the detached or inshore squadron off Boulogne in 1804 and 1805 and reported to Rear-Admiral Douglas in July 1805 (James III 320–323; Marshall, *Royal Naval Biography*, entry on Owen, which prints his letter of 18 July 1805 to Douglas). https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/n335/mode/1up ; https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Royal_Naval_Biography/Owen,_Edward_William_Campbell_Rich . High.
- In 1804 the squadron off Boulogne was under Rear-Admiral Thomas Louis in Leopard (James III 228). Louis went out to the Mediterranean early in 1805 and hoisted his flag in the Canopus in Nelson's fleet in the gulf of Palma at the end of March (James III 339); a modern web page that keeps Owen "under Louis in 1805" (morethannelson.com) is wrong for August 1805 or means the first weeks of the year. High. https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/n354/mode/1up
- Keith's standing order of 8 November 1803 (from HMS Monarch): captains were not to let themselves be drawn under the enemy's coast batteries unless there was a fair prospect of an advantage worth the risk (Keith Papers III, pp. 53–54; period). https://www.historyofwar.org/sources/napoleonic/nrs1955/1_1_040.html

### 5.2 How close they kept

- **August 1804 (James III 228–229; later scholarly from logs):** 15 to 20 vessels under Rear-Admiral Louis; the **main body usually lay at anchor in 15 fathoms about 10 miles north-west of the port**; a flying division of five or six vessels under Owen in Immortalité **"generally cruised just out of shell-range of the enemy's batteries, anchoring occasionally, as the state of the tide rendered necessary"**. On 25 August 1804, after a skirmish, Immortalité hauled off and lay-to **about three miles off the port**. https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/n243/mode/1up
- **31 July 1804 (period letter, Naval Chronicle XIV 481):** "The English squadron anchored about three miles from them" (from the French line moored in the roads); British ships often ran in within gunshot and drew fire from the forts. https://archive.org/details/navalchronicleco14londiala/page/n510/mode/1up
- **April 1805 (James, quoted by Desbrière IV 407):** a division of the frigate Leda, two sloops, a bomb and eight gun-brigs lay at anchor off Boulogne, two of the brigs under sail off Ambleteuse. https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n66/mode/1up
- **17–18 July 1805 (James III 320–321):** the French flotilla's chief tried to draw the British "at their anchorage" off Boulogne; Immortalité, the 32-gun frigate Hebe, the 20-gun ship Arab "and the remainder of the detached squadron" weighed **"from their station off the port"**, fought off Wimereux at half a mile north-west of the town, and **re-anchored "about five miles to the north-westward of Boulogne"**, sending a gun-brig or two to look out off Cap Gris-Nez. The French counted the British off their coast that day as 2 frigates, 3 corvettes, 8 brigs and a cutter (Lafond, in Desbrière IV 397). https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/n336/mode/1up
- **The camps saw them.** On 26 April 1805 Soult reported a British corvette seen passing "de la côte de Boulogne" with her foremast shot away (Desbrière IV 407, period report). Napoleon wrote on 13 August 1805 that the British ships in the Downs were harassed "every day" by his prames and flotillas (Corr. XI No. 9073, pp. 87–88). The title of the Naval Chronicle's plate of 1805 says what the British saw: Boulogne "with the encampments on the heights".

Confidence: High that British ships were constantly on station in sight of the camps in summer 1805; Medium for the distances in August 1805 (the figures are of 1804 and July 1805).

### 5.3 Actions nearest to August 1805

- **About 3–4 August 1805 (period):** Napoleon to Berthier, Camp de Boulogne, 4 August: a boat with a flag had been placed at half range of the batteries Sarrut, Augereau and Varé, and an English frigate had the insolence to come in and carry it off because the batteries fired only a few shots; he blamed the artillery generals (Corr. XI No. 9042, p. 59; the batteries were not located in this research). https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/n68/mode/1up
- **Before 13 August 1805 (period, Napoleon's claim):** he had the English cruising squadron attacked by the flotilla, which "chased it three and a half leagues out into the Channel, though it included ships of war"; he was very satisfied with the flotilla (Corr. XI No. 9069 to Cambacérès, p. 79, and No. 9074 to Prince Eugène, p. 88, both 13 August). No British account of this was found in James or the Naval Chronicle. https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/n88/mode/1up and https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/n97/mode/1up . Medium that a skirmish took place; Low for Napoleon's account of it.
- **20 August 1805 (period, Napoleon's claim):** a division of the flotilla doubling Cap Gris-Nez had an engagement with the English; the English cruising squadron was roughly handled and reportedly lost a topmast (Corr. XI No. 9097, p. 104). Medium that an engagement took place off Gris-Nez; Low for the damage.
- **18 July 1805:** the action off Wimereux, Ambleteuse and Gris-Nez with Ver Huell's Batavian flotilla (above); Immortalité had 4 killed and 12 wounded (James III 323; Marshall). High.
- **September 1805:** a modern web page says Immortalité was becalmed off Boulogne under heated shot and shells; not confirmed in period sources here. Low.
- **Not 1805:** Nelson's attacks on the Boulogne flotilla on 4 and 15–16 August belong to **1801**; some web summaries put them in 1805. High.

### 5.4 A British sail in the view?

- **Justified on an August 1805 evening:** yes. The inshore squadron was on station off Boulogne through July and in August (Napoleon's letters of 4, 13 and 20 August deal with it). Medium–High.
- **Where:** usually 3–5 nautical miles off the port, mostly to the north-west, with heavier ships further out. From the sample viewpoint: a ship 4 miles north-west of the harbour bears about **332° at 6 km**; "5 miles north-west" (July 1805) about **328° at 8 km**; the 1804 main body (10 miles north-west) about **321° at 17 km**; a frigate lying-to 3 miles off the port to the west is **straight behind** at about 3 km. All are behind the viewer's left shoulder, outside a frame looking east. A British vessel standing in close to the north, a mile or less off the Pointe de la Crèche, Wimereux or the Pointe de la Rochette (as off Wimereux on 18 July 1805), would bear about 020–045° at 3–6 km: at the far left edge of a wide frame, lit on its seaward side by the low sun. Low for any particular position.
- **What kind:** frigates (Immortalité, 36; Hebe, 32), a 20-gun ship (Arab), ship- and brig-sloops (16–18 guns), gun-brigs (12–14 guns, the commonest close watchers), bomb vessels and hired armed cutters (James III 227–230, 318–323; NC XIV 68). The likeliest single sail close in at evening is a two-masted **gun-brig or brig-sloop** (hull about 25–30 m); a three-masted **frigate** (hull about 40–45 m) further out. At 6 km a frigate is a silhouette about 0.4° long and 0.5° high. Medium for the types; the sizes are approximate (Low–Medium).
- **At anchor or under way:** both. In 1804 the main body anchored and the flying division cruised and anchored as the tide required; British officers wrote of "many calm nights" at anchor off Boulogne. On a quiet evening a British frigate or brig at anchor with sails furled, 3–5 miles out, is the best-founded picture. Medium.

## 6. Could England be seen?

**Computation (ours).** Horizon distance with standard refraction (k = 0.13): d = √(2Rh/(1−k)); the height hidden behind the sea horizon at distance D is (D − d)²·(1−k)/(2R). Target positions from modern maps, heights approximate (Low–Medium for the heights).

| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Roads, eye 3 m (horizon 6.6 km) | cliffs (100–105 m) **below the horizon**; only the top 25 m of the Castle hill would show, 0.03° tall | top 38 m shows, 0.04° tall | below the horizon |
| Roads, eye 6 m | cliff tops 0–12 m show, 0.02° | 0.06° | below |

- **From the roads:** in practice invisible. A line 0.03–0.06° tall, 46–52 km off in summer haze, would not be seen; a painter should leave the horizon empty. High.
- **From the heights:** on clear days, yes: a thin pale line of chalk cliffs and downs to the NW–NNW. Period: Napoleon to Cambacérès, Boulogne, 16 November 1803: from the heights of Ambleteuse he had seen the English coast as one sees the Calvary (Mont-Valérien) from the Tuileries, and one could make out the houses and the movement (Corr. IX No. 7279, p. 92; he probably used a glass). The British letter of 1804 gives 27 miles from Boulogne to England (NC XIV 482). https://archive.org/details/correspondancede09napouoft/page/n101/mode/1up . Medium.
- **Direction:** Folkestone about 322–325°, Dover 332–344°, Dungeness 295–297°: **behind the viewer's left shoulder** for a viewer looking east. High. (Dungeness lies almost exactly where the sun sets in mid-August, but it is low shingle and well below the horizon.)

## Doubts and disagreements

1. **Lines of craft in the roads in August 1805.** Desbrière (IV 398, from Lafond's correspondence): no craft in the roads from 3 to at least 6 August, gaps in the record afterwards, and no mention of a line in the reports; he judged the sortie of 17–21 July "apparently the last". Napoleon (Corr. XI Nos. 9069, 9074, 9097): the flotilla attacked the British cruisers (by 13 August) and a division fought off Gris-Nez (20 August). James (III 326) has the army embarked twice in Napoleon's presence. Both can be true (sorties for a day, no standing line), but a standing line of craft in the roads on a given August evening is not documented. For the flotilla research to settle.
2. **Weather at Boulogne itself.** The daily record used is London's (150 km away, 7 a.m. and 2 p.m. only). It agrees with the two Boulogne remarks (bad weather on 3–6 August, much rain on 20 August) but cannot give the evening at Boulogne. British logs off Boulogne (The National Archives, ADM 51/52) would, but were not available.
3. **Tide times.** Norie's 10 h 30 m establishment is a rounded period figure; the age of the tide (1½ days) is assumed; shallow-water effects are ignored. Times of high and low water are good to about ±45 minutes, the spring/neap pattern is sure.
4. **British positions in August 1805.** The distances (3 miles, 5 miles, 10 miles north-west) are from August 1804 and July 1805; nothing read gives the station for August 1805 itself. The Downs, with Keith, lay about 50 km north, out of sight.
5. **Napoleon's claims.** His letters of 13 and 20 August exaggerate in the usual way (James and Desbrière both note that French accounts inflated British forces); treat the "three and a half leagues" chase and the topmast as his version.
6. **James's "eight leagues" (III 227).** On 19 July 1804 James has Immortalité and Leda "at anchor about eight leagues to the westward of the town of Boulogne", which seems far for an inshore squadron; possibly a slip. Not used.
7. **Coast orientation and heights** come from modern maps and modern figures (OpenStreetMap; Météo-France; French Wikipedia). The cliffs have retreated a little and the harbour front was rebuilt after 1805, so the near-harbour shoreline differed; the orientation of the cliffs did not.
8. **Smoke and the sea breeze.** The drift directions follow from the wind; the frequencies are modern; the calm-evening layer and the sea-breeze behaviour are general meteorology, not observed for these camps.
9. **Douglas's flagship in August.** The Naval Chronicle's list (checked on the page image) puts Douglas in the Leopard in its June–July 1805 number; a change of flagship or of station before August was not looked for.
10. **Aside for the campaign notes (outside this topic):** to the spans of time Napoleon said he needed command of the Strait (`a-h2a.md`, R10), add **twelve hours**: "si nous sommes maîtres douze heures de la traversée, l'Angleterre a vécu" (to Decrès, Camp de Boulogne, 4 August 1805, Corr. XI No. 9043, pp. 59–60). https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/n68/mode/1up
