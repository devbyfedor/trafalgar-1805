# Check of `flotilla.md`: the invasion flotilla at Boulogne, summer 1805

An independent, adversarial check of `flotilla.md` (same folder), claim by claim. I did not write that note and do not edit it. Every verdict says what I opened and where. Verdicts: **confirmed** (the source says it, at that place, and nothing better contradicts it), **corrected** (substantially right, but a page, figure, date, attribution or confidence needs changing), **unsupported** (the cited source does not say it, or I could not find it there or anywhere), **refuted** (the evidence says otherwise). Confidence uses the scale at the top of `docs/history-notes.md`. Kinds of evidence as in `flotilla.md`: **period** (1801–1825), **scholarly** (Desbrière, James, Clowes and the like), **modern**, **inference**.

Status: complete (14 November 2026, session 28; sections 2.6-2.13, the recomputations, the additional facts and the sources added in session 28).

Summary of the outcome: the note is careful and mostly right. Most quotations and figures are where it says they are. The main problems are the geometry of the camera against the line, a harbour that dried at low water more completely than the note says, prames and spacing presented more firmly than the 1805 evidence allows, the masthead pennant (its colours were fixed by law in 1794), and a handful of wrong page numbers. Sections 2.6–2.13 do not change that judgement. They confirm the flags, the dark hulls, the brigs' furled sails at anchor and what the period images show. They add one point that matters for the picture: the line rode in water deep enough at every state of the tide, so a line drawn just outside the low-water line would be aground or barely afloat at 18:33 (item 10). The note's mast heights and its account of motion in a swell, which were estimates, are corrected (items 11 and 12).

## What the painter must change or know

1. **The camera at 700 m and the line in front of the town do not fit together.** In 1768–1805 the spring low-water line lay about 386 toises (750 m) seaward of the west jetty's head, and the wooden fort stood on it at the end of the 700 m fascine dike (Desbrière III pp. 142, 148; see 2.10). A boat anchored 700 m west of the pier heads therefore sits at about the spring low-water line. The town-and-defences note's coordinates put the wooden fort only some 250 m from such a camera. At low water on the spring tides of 9–11 August the boat would be in very shallow water. The moored line lay in the roads beyond the low-water line, in water deep enough for the craft at all states of the tide: in 1801 already 500 toises (975 m) off the entrance, and by 1805, according to Dumas, pushed further out. So it would lie beside the camera or seaward of it, not between the camera and the town. Two ways out: put the camera seaward of the line (roughly 2–2.5 km off the pier heads), or keep 700 m and accept that the craft in front of the town are not the line. Inshore of the line the documents put only some péniches (between the shore and the line's ends), bomb craft (just behind the line) and boats. This rests on inference from period and scholarly figures and on the town note's geometry. Low–Medium. The owner decides.
2. **No line is documented for 9–11 August 1805.** Confirmed (2.10). There was no craft in the roads on 3–6 August. After that the flotilla's reports have gaps and mention no line. The only August event on record is the sortie of 12 August, which left about noon. A moored line on 9–11 August is a reconstruction. Its documented model is the line of 10–21 July 1805: 27 chaloupes canonnières (brigs) and 27 bateaux canonniers (mostly luggers). **No prames are listed in any 1805 line.** Prames appear in the lines of 1804 and in the sortie of 12 August (five of them), so a prame in the moored line is a licence. Medium.
3. **At low water the harbour craft sat aground, even more completely than the note says.** Desbrière's reconstruction of the port concludes that, outside the narrow river channel, the flotilla's craft were aground almost all the time (III pp. 147–148). The semicircular basin was dug as a drying basin ("bassin d'échouage", III p. 149). The dam with a wooden flushing sluice was only decided on 21 April 1804 and held water upstream of itself (III p. 452). By the decrees of 26 and 30 August 1805 the craft not needed for sorties were to be kept "à flot au delà du barrage", afloat above the dam (Corr. XI Nos. 9137, 9164). So at low water the tiers of hulls along the quays and round the basin sat upright on mud and sand. The flat floors, and the prames' three keels, were built for this. The Liane ran between them as a stream about 20–40 m wide (III p. 147). No craft could pass the entrance around low water. High for drying; Medium for the details.
4. **The masthead pennant was mostly red.** The decree of 27 pluviôse an II (15 February 1794) that made the ensign three equal vertical bands, blue at the staff, also fixed the pennant: one fifth blue, one fifth white, three fifths red (Archives parlementaires LXXXV p. 78). On a chaloupe's pennant of about 11 m that is about 2 m of blue, 2 m of white and 7 m of red. This explains the red pennants in Lewis's 1804 view. Period. High for the proportions. Medium that the blue was next to the mast: the decree says "likewise", and the ensign has its blue at the staff.
5. **Spacing may be twice as wide as the note says.** The 45–55 m figure assumes a single row of about 150 craft. James has a double line in October 1804, and Saint-Haouen describes up to three rows. A row would then have about 80–110 m between craft. Napoleon's remark of September 1803, that two or three caïques could be placed in each interval between chaloupes canonnières, points to intervals of at least about 60 m. Low.
6. **The line also ran south of the harbour mouth.** Lewis's view of October 1804 shows the line on both sides of the harbour, and the roads were defined from la Crèche to Alprech. A line only north-east of the mouth is unsupported. Seen from a camera looking east, craft may also stand to the right of the harbour. Low.
7. **Heading at low water: probably bows to the north-north-east, to the left for a camera looking east.** Craft at anchor point their bows into the stream. Around local low water off this coast the stream should be the south-west-going one, so tide-rode craft would point roughly NNE. This is an inference from the general behaviour of the streams in the Strait and was not checked against a tidal-stream table. Low. Check it against the tide note.
8. **Chaloupes canonnières may show a few guns along the sides.** Besides the long 24-pounders at bow and stern and the howitzer, the orders of July and September 1803 gave chaloupes two to four 36-pounder carronades on the sides (Desbrière III pp. 88, 98). Willaumez speaks of guns "at its ends and in battery" (p. 123). Medium.
9. **Smaller corrections.** The bateau canonnier's side should be 0.8–1.2 m above the water including the washboard, as in the note's own table; its section 1 and 2.4 imply up to 1.5 m. Flags were of "toile", plain cloth, not specifically linen. The Lewis view shows October 1804, Keith's attack. Several page numbers are wrong: Corr. XI No. 9164 is on pp. 165–166, and the note's link to p. 155 opens another letter. Willaumez's entry Brigantin is on p. 107, and the Ville-de-Lille is on Desbrière III p. 222. The péniches lay inshore of the line's ends, not "at the ends and between it and the beach". Details below.
10. **The line rode afloat at low water; a line drawn just outside the low-water line would not.** The painting's eye is now 1.1 km off the jetty heads (`evening.md`), not 700 m, and item 1's conclusion holds there too. Cadier's plan of 1768 gives an even fall of 3.40 m over the 752 m from the west jetty to the lowest low-water line (Desbrière III p. 142), about 1 in 220. At the painting's moment, 18:33, the water stood at about +1.45 m on Desbrière's scale, and its edge lay about 500 m off the jetty heads (`evening.md`). At that slope a craft 24–84 m outside the 750 m low-water line, where `fleet.ts` now draws the two rows, has some 1.3–1.5 m of water under it. That floats a bateau canonnier (1.1 m loaded), but not a chaloupe canonnière (1.8 m) or a prame (2.8 m). At 18:33 a chaloupe needs to lie at least about 150 m outside that line, and a prame about 370 m, which is about where the eye floats. To ride at every state of the tide, as the line did, a chaloupe needs about 400 m outside it at the lowest springs. The documents put the line in such water: the trace of 1803 had 22 pieds (7 m) at low water (III p. 161), the bomb craft drawing 2.75 m kept just behind it (III p. 560; 2.6), and the wide strand kept the line far from the land (III p. 141). So the documented line would lie beside the eye or seaward of it, as item 1 says. If the line stays where it is drawn, record it as a licence. Low for the slope (one plan of 1768, extended); Medium-Low that the line floated clear at low water (2.10).
11. **The masts may stand taller than the note's lower figures.** By Steel's rule, which the note itself uses, a chaloupe canonnière rigged as a brig with topmasts reaches about 21 m, and about 25 m with topgallants. Only the "simplified" masting asked for in July 1803 (III p. 88), which is not described, brings it under 20 m. The prame's main truck comes to about 35 m by the rule, and the Foudroyante model agrees; the note's range is 28–36 m. The lug masts may also be taller than the note's 10–14 m: the lug-rigged caïque on the plate facing III p. 90 stands about 0.63 of its length, and Hue's luggers about one hull length. Low throughout (2.9).
12. **In a gentle swell the craft follow the swell.** Most of them roll quicker than a 6–10 s swell, so they rise and fall with it, about half a metre, and tilt by about the wave's slope. In a 0.5 m swell that is 0.6–1.6°, not "a degree or two". Brigs and prames may have a roll period of 5–10 s, and could roll two or three times as much if the swell nears it, though their flat floors damp the roll. Mastheads sweep 0.3–1 m. Péniches add small quick jerks. "Prames least" holds for heave and pitch, not securely for roll. A westerly swell's crests run roughly along a line moored parallel to the shore, so the craft of one row rise together. Inference. Low (2.12).
13. **Smaller points from 2.6–2.13.**
    - At 18:33, 52 minutes before sunset, every ensign and pennant flies. No riding lights are recorded. The guard boats rowed along the line only from sunset (2.11).
    - Twice the beam is Willaumez's minimum length for a pennant, not a typical one (2.8).
    - No hull numbers in view: every craft had one, but nothing says where or whether it was painted (2.8).
    - Two anchors did not stop the swing: the whole line turned together at each turn of the stream (2.12).
    - Bomb craft, if shown, were decked vessels drawing about 2.75 m, lying a short distance behind the line. Caïques were few in 1805: two in the June line (2.6).
    - Citations. The wooden fort at the end of the fascine dike is on Desbrière III p. 155 (and p. 158), not on pp. 142 or 148 (item 1). The roads' defended front ran from the rock of l'Heurt on the left to la Crèche on the right, 2,400 toises (4.7 km) apart (III p. 153); Alprech, in item 6, is the cape beyond l'Heurt. Item 3's citation of Corr. XI Nos. 9137 and 9164 stands: both have "à flot au delà du barrage" (2.10).

## Verdicts

### 2.1 Names, classes and counts

- **Class names and the "espèce" pairing.** Confirmed. Desbrière III p. 546 has the 2 December 1803 arrival of "canonnières ou bâtiments de 1re espèce, ainsi qu'on commence à les nommer" ([n577](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n577/mode/1up)). The period draught table facing p. 90 pairs prame, canonnière, bateau canonnier and péniche with the grande, 1re, 2e and 3e espèce exactly as the note says. I read it myself ([n104](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n104/mode/1up)). Period. High.
- **British names.** Confirmed. James III has "the prame, and the gun-vessel, or canonniere" on p. 216 and "prames, or corvettes" on p. 217. The first class are "rigged as brigs" (p. 217), and "a heavily-armed launch, or peniche (No. 267)" is on p. 233 ([p. 217](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/217/mode/1up)). The Naval Chronicle translation of Lacrosse's report uses "Gun-boats" for the line and "Pinnaces" for the British boats (XIV p. 339). Scholarly and period. High.
- **Forfait made inspector general on 24 May 1803.** Confirmed. The arrêté of 4 prairial an XI, printed in Desbrière III p. 82, gives him construction, repairs, purchases, classing and movements ([n93](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n93/mode/1up)). The caïques built to his plan are confirmed by Willaumez p. 123. The "modèle an XII" attribution comes from a modern product page and was not checked. Period. High for his role; Low for the attribution.
- **Spelling.** Confirmed. This is general French usage, and Desbrière writes "chaloupe canonnière" without a hyphen. High.
- **Return of 30 March 1804.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 558 ([n589](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n589/mode/1up)). Boulogne had 2 prames, 61 craft of the 1st kind, 158 of the 2nd, 118 péniches, 3 + 1 + 1 péniches for the First Consul, the admiral and the general-in-chief, 9 packet boats, caïques (number unreadable) plus one for the First Consul, and 2 bomb galliots. There were 474 transports: 45 for artillery, 190 for cavalry, 76 for battalions, 23 unclassed, 125 Newfoundland ships and 5 whalers. Period. High.
- **20 July 1805: 578 prames and gun craft, 526 transports.** Confirmed (James III p. 325). The type breakdown the note says is missing does exist for July 1805. James's appendix No. 27 (p. 496), taken from Dumas XII p. 304, gives Boulogne 13 prames, 11 bombardes, packet boats and avisos, 530 French gun-vessels (chaloupes and bateaux together), and 22 caïques, "corvettes de pêche" and péniches. James notes that the total of 578 does not quite agree with the items ([p. 496](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/496/mode/1up)). Scholarly from a period table. High for July 1805.
- **Decree of 30 August 1805: 6 prames, 72 chaloupes canonnières, 72 péniches.** Confirmed in substance. **Corrected** page: No. 9164 is on pp. **165–166** of Corr. XI, not 155–156 ([p. 165](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/165/mode/1up)). I checked the page image. The note's example link to p. 155 opens a different letter. Period. High.

### 2.2 Prame

- **Design: 110 × 25 × 8 pieds, ship rig ("gréement de corvette"), three keels, 12 × 24-pounders, 38 seamen and 120 soldiers.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 90 ([n101](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n101/mode/1up)). Desbrière gives the source as an official document of the Archives des Constructions navales, cited for the prices on the same page. James III pp. 216–217 agrees: 110 by 25 French feet, drawing 7–8 feet, rigged as a ship, 12 long 24-pounders, 38 sailors and upwards of 100 soldiers, and the first 20 ordered "each with stalls for 50 horses". I read the loaded draught on the table myself: 8½ pieds, within a quarter foot. Period and scholarly. High.
- **Origin: 5 July 1803, 50 boats on the model of Jornin's Foudroyant at Dunkirk, 12 × 24 and 60 horses, a leeboard each side.** Confirmed: Decrès to Forfait, Desbrière III pp. 87–89 ([n98](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n98/mode/1up)). Period. High.
- **"By 1804 there were 18 prames, 16 of them new (p. 90)."** **Corrected.** Page 90 says the actual armaments came down to 18 prames, 2 of them already existing, so 16 new, but it gives no date. "By 1804" is the note's addition. James's July 1805 table counts 17 prames: 13 at Boulogne, 3 at Ambleteuse and 1 at Calais. Medium.
- **Flat floors and three keels, so it could sit on the mud.** Confirmed, with a page added. Willaumez defines the prame (p. 476) as a large vessel built "à plates varangues", with a battery of heavy guns and two mortars on a platform in a hatchway before the mainmast. He calls such craft mobile fortresses for coast defence and refers to his plate A, fig. 14 ([n531](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n531/mode/1up)). The three keels are on Desbrière p. 90. Sitting on the mud is a sound inference: the harbour dried (2.10). Period. High.
- **The "Foudroyante" model (Musée national de la Marine 17 MG 18).** Confirmed as a type reference only. The English Wikipedia list of the Trianon models quotes the museum's record title: an artillery prame of 20 × 36-pounders, late 18th century. The list adds, unsourced, that it is a fictitious representative ship of Forfait's design ([Trianon model collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trianon_model_collection)). The museum's own catalogue refused automated access. Modern. Low for anything about the 1803 hull.
- **Guns: six a side (inference).** Unsupported as a fact, but fair as inference. Willaumez's "batterie" and James's "12 long 24-pounders" support a broadside battery. A dealer's description of Baugean's 1814 plate "Prame venant de mouiller" mentions about ten visible guns (see Additional facts). Medium-Low, as the note has it.
- **Slow; Ariadne "not above a third larger" than a prame.** Confirmed: James III p. 319 ([p. 319](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/319/mode/1up)). The same page has the Ville-de-Genève's mainmast cut away. Scholarly. Medium.
- **Names.** Mostly confirmed. **Corrected** in two places. (a) The Ville-de-Mayence also sailed with Ver Huell's division on 17 July 1805, with the Ville-d'Aix, Ville-d'Anvers and Ville-de-Genève and 32 first-class gun-vessels (James III p. 318). So in July 1805 it was with the Batavian flotilla, not at Boulogne. (b) The Ville-de-Lille at Dunkirk is on Desbrière III p. **222** (12 September 1803), not 223 ([n249](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n249/mode/1up)). Page 566 lists it with the Ville-de-Brest as the two prames at Boulogne, Ambleteuse and Wimereux on 29 June 1804. High.
- **Five prames came in at the end of March 1805; five went out on 12 August; six kept in the "bassin circulaire".** Confirmed: Desbrière IV p. 395 ([n52](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n52/mode/1up)); Dumas XII p. 76 ([n88](https://archive.org/details/prcisdesvnement10dumagoog/page/n88/mode/1up)), where I checked the date "12 août" on the page image; Corr. XI No. 9164, art. 4. Period. High for the decree; Medium for the sortie (French account).

### 2.3 Chaloupe canonnière

- **Design 76–80 × 17 × 5–6 pieds, brig rig; 3 × 24 and "a howitzer or mortar of 6–8 inches"; 22 seamen and 130 soldiers.** **Corrected** (wording). Desbrière III p. 90 says "I obusier de 6 à 8 pouces", a howitzer only. The mortar comes from James ("an 8-inch mortar", III p. 217). James also writes "an 8-inch howitzer" for Collet's gun-vessels of July 1805 (III p. 317). The draught table's "nouvelle construction" at 5½ pieds and "ancienne construction" at 8 pieds are confirmed: I read the scale myself. Linking the old type to the 1801 gun-brigs of 200–250 tons (James III p. 67) is a sound inference. Period. High for the design.
- **Rig: brig, with some brigantine or simplified rigs.** Confirmed: James III p. 217; Desbrière III p. 90 ("gréement de brick"); Desbrière III p. 88, where the First Consul wants the masting of chaloupes still to be built simplified, "de manière à éviter la complication d'une mâture de bâtiment carré". Willaumez p. 123 refers to pl. B fig. 10, "une canonnière de première espèce gréée en brigantin" ([n138](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n138/mode/1up)). **Corrected** page: the entry Brigantin is on p. **107**, not 106 ([n122](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n122/mode/1up)). Its content is as the note says: a smaller brig, after-yards shorter than the fore, flying topgallants at most, no royals, a mainsail that is always lowered, never a square main course. Hamelin's division of July 1805 had its six first-class gun-vessels "brig-rigged" (James III p. 324). The gaff mainsail in Lewis's view cannot be made out at the resolution available (1280 px). Period. Medium-High.
- **Guns and their places.** Confirmed, and more can be added. Desbrière III p. 88: the small chaloupes already built were to carry four 36-pounder carronades on the side and three 18- or 12-pounders fore and aft, and the 300 new ones three or four guns. The plan of 23 fructidor an XI (10 September 1803) arms every chaloupe with three 24-pounders and two 36-pounder carronades (III p. 98, [n113](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n113/mode/1up)). Willaumez p. 123: guns "tant à ses extrémités qu'en batterie", at the ends and along the side. Corr. No. 8925 (Desbrière IV p. 401): four chaloupes could fire only 8 guns. Placing the heavy guns at bow and stern is fair. A few side carronades should be allowed for. Period. Medium.
- **Oars and leeboards.** Confirmed: leeboards ordered for all chaloupes and bateaux, Desbrière III p. 88; Willaumez p. 123 on Swedish and Russian gun-sloops with 24 oars; Naval Chronicle XIV p. 482, "Gun-boats, which carry a number of oars". The note's account of French Wikipedia is accurate: 12 pairs, citing Roucaud 2019. Added: Napoleon's pencil note on the plan of 10 September 1803 gives a chaloupe canonnière 56 men for the oars, beside 36 for the guns and 4 officers (Desbrière III p. 99). That fits about 28 oars double-manned, like the bateau's 28 oars and 56 men. Collet's chaloupes "swept out" of Granville in July 1805 (James III p. 317). Period. Medium for the oars being used; Low for their number.
- **Garrisons in the roads: 50 men per chaloupe, 40 per bateau; six canonnières under 450 men.** Confirmed: Soult on 15 April 1804, Desbrière III p. 559 ([n590](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n590/mode/1up)). Napoleon, Corr. No. 8925 (Desbrière IV p. 401), counts six "canonnières" at no more than 450 men, but that six is four chaloupes plus the two corvettes canonnières. Period. High.

### 2.4 Bateau canonnier

- **Design 60 × 14 × 4½ pieds, lugger rig; one 24 and a howitzer or field gun; 6 seamen, 100 soldiers, 2 horses; loaded draught 3½ pieds.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 90 and the draught table, where I read the péniche and bateau line at 3½ pieds myself. Period. High.
- **A 24-pounder forward and a field piece aft; the plan of 10 September 1803; 40 boats with a bronze 12 and 100 with a light bronze 24.** Confirmed: James III p. 217; Desbrière III pp. 100–101 ([n115](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n115/mode/1up)). The field pieces were to stand at the stern on their own carriages to defend the boat. Forty boats carried a bronze 12-pounder field gun instead of the navy's 24, and 100 a light bronze 24 with its carriage aft. The note's "iron 24" is a reasonable gloss: the text says only that the 24 was supplied by the navy. Desbrière III p. 88 has the July 1803 order for a field piece "sur ses roues". Period. High.
- **Rig: lug, some schooners; two or three masts.** Confirmed and refined. James III p. 217: "some rigged as schooners, and some as luggers". James III p. 324: Hamelin's ten second-class boats of July 1805 were "two or three brigs, the remainder luggers". The number of masts is still undocumented. Medium.
- **Washboard of 18 inches (49 cm), removable, pierced for the oars, on the 100 Dunkirk boats and all new ones; leeboards.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 88 ([n99](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n99/mode/1up)). The text says the raised planking is to be crossed by the oars and easily taken down. Period. High.
- **Freeboard.** **Corrected** (internal inconsistency). Section 1 has "side under a metre plus an 18-inch washboard", and 2.4 "a little under a metre ... with the washboard above it". Together these allow up to about 1.5 m. The note's own table has 0.8–1.2 m including the washboard. From a loaded draught of 3½–4½ pieds and an undocumented depth, the hull side would be only about 0.3–0.7 m, plus the 0.49 m washboard. Use the table's 0.8–1.2 m. Inference. Low.
- **28 oars, 56 men.** Confirmed: Napoleon's pencil note, Desbrière III p. 101 ([n116](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n116/mode/1up)). French Wikipedia's 14 pairs (Roucaud 2019) is reported correctly. Period. High.

### 2.5 Péniche

- **Design 60 × 10 × 3½ pieds, lug; a 6-inch howitzer and a 4-pounder, or a Prussian howitzer, or an 8-inch mortar; 5 seamen and 66 soldiers.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 90. Period. High.
- **Willaumez p. 447.** Confirmed ([n494](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n494/mode/1up)). The Boulogne péniches were heavy and carvel-built ("à franc bord"). The first kind measured 60 pieds stem to sternpost, 53 pieds 8 pouces on the keel, 10 in beam and 5 deep, with 40 oars and 40 swivels or espingoles. The second kind measured 42 × 8½ × 3 with 28 oars. The coastguard boats were 50 × 10 × 5 with a 12-pounder and 12 espingoles, lug-rigged, with oars "à couples". "Double-banked" is a reasonable but not certain reading of "à couples". Period. High.
- **Shape and rig from the plate facing Desbrière III p. 90.** Confirmed ([n102](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n102/mode/1up)). The plate "Bateaux de 3e espèce", from the Archives de la Guerre, shows a three-masted péniche under lug sails with a jib on a bowsprit and the mizzen sheeted to an outrigger beyond the stern. A long pennant flies at the mainmast head. A two-masted péniche is half hidden by the fold. The details drawing has an open hull with mast steps for three masts and a round-bilged, fairly flat midship section. Its left end is cut off by the fold, so the count of about fifteen thwarts or tholes is approximate. My own measurement of the three-masted péniche gives a mainmast head 0.47–0.48 of the hull length above the waterline, as the note says. The same drawing shows the gunwale about 0.045 of the length above water, about 0.9 m for 19.5 m. That is more than the note's 0.5 m, but the drawing is not to scale. Period. Medium-High for the look; Low for the freeboard figure.
- **Oars: Napoleon's 64 men; Willaumez's 40 oars.** Confirmed: Desbrière III p. 101; Willaumez p. 447. French Wikipedia's 13 pairs is reported correctly. High that they were primarily rowing boats.
- **Role in the roads.** **Corrected** (wording and attribution). The ordre du jour of 16 April 1804 (Desbrière III p. 560, [n591](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n591/mode/1up)) puts picket péniches one cable ahead of the line, and "les autres péniches entre la terre et les extrémités de la ligne": the other péniches between the shore and the ends of the line, that is, inshore of its ends. They were not "at the ends of the line and between it and the beach". Bruix on 13 April 1804 had pickets at night "ahead of and on the wings of our line" (p. 559). The ordre du jour does not mention Prussian howitzers. The péniches "à obusiers prussiens" come from Desbrière IV p. 397 (June–July 1805) and Dumas XI p. 47 (August 1804), and the long-range howitzers of 12 August 1805 from Dumas XII p. 76. Period. High.

### 2.6 Other craft

- **Caïques: ships' launches with one 24-pounder; 41 of them (Desbrière III p. 90).** Confirmed. Page 90 has "Caïques (chaloupes des vaisseaux de ligne, I 24)" among the types, and "41 caïques" in the armaments actually carried out ([n101](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n101/mode/1up)). The page says armed or planned, not "built". Period. High.
- **Willaumez p. 123: oak gun-launches on Forfait's plan, 43 p 3 po × 10 p 6 po × 4 p, draught 2 p 11 po, a 36-pounder aft and a 36-pounder carronade forward on slides, a short deck of about 5 pieds at each end.** Confirmed. I read it on the page image ([n138](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n138/mode/1up)). Willaumez calls them "chaloupes canonnières construites en chêne". He also gives a keel of 37 p 3 po. The conversions are right (14.0 × 3.4 × 1.3 m, draught 0.95 m). The period draught table facing Desbrière III p. 90 puts "une caïque" at about 3 pieds loaded, which agrees ([n104](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n104/mode/1up)). The guns differ between the two sources: Desbrière's one 24, Willaumez's two 36s. Both are period, so the note should give both. Period. High.
- **The plate: one caïque with a sloop-like rig, one with two lugs.** Confirmed. The drawing "Bateaux de petite espèce; caïques" is on the same folding plate as the péniche, from the Archives de la Guerre ([n102](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n102/mode/1up)). The left boat has one mast with a gaff sail, two square yards and a jib on a long bowsprit. The right boat has two lug sails and a jib to a bowsprit. Both fly a long pennant. Period. Medium-High for the look.
- **Caïques in the 1805 lines.** Added. Napoleon on 4 September 1803 wanted about fifty caïques placed to protect the line, two or three in each interval between chaloupes canonnières (Desbrière III p. 161, [n180](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n180/mode/1up)). In 1805 they were few: the line that came in on 15 June 1805 had only 2 caïques (Desbrière IV p. 397, [n54](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n54/mode/1up)). A caïque carried 20 men (III p. 102). Period. High.
- **Bomb craft: 6 bombardes and 2 galiotes à bombes on 23 March 1804; 9 bombardes on 15 April 1804; 3 bombardes and a sloop-bombarde on 9 March 1805; 3 bateaux bombardes in June 1805.** Confirmed. Lafond's letter of 2 germinal an XII (23 March 1804) is on III p. 556 ([n587](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n587/mode/1up)); the 9 bombardes on p. 559 ([n590](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n590/mode/1up)); IV p. 395 ([n52](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n52/mode/1up)); IV p. 397 ([n54](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n54/mode/1up)). **Corrected** date: 15 June is the day the line with the 3 bateaux bombardes came back in. It had been out since 13 June. Period. High.
- **"The bomb craft kept a short distance behind (inshore of) the line" (III p. 560).** **Corrected** (reading). The ordre du jour says the bomb galliots and bomb boats were always to keep "à peu de distance sur l'arrière de la ligne" ([n591](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n591/mode/1up)). "Inshore" is the note's reading. It is a fair one, since the line faced the enemy at sea, but it is inference. Medium.
- **"Their rigs are not described in what was read."** Confirmed, with one figure added. The draught table puts "une bombarde" at about 8½ pieds (2.75 m) loaded, the same as a prame ([n104](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n104/mode/1up), read by me). So these were sizeable decked vessels, not boats. The galiotes were presumably the Dutch-type galliot, but that is inference. Period for the draught. Low for the look.
- **Corvettes canonnières Foudre and Audacieuse: 10 guns (four or six long 18-pounders, the rest brass 36-pounder carronades), over 80 men, called brigs by the British, at Boulogne with Hamelin at the end of July 1805.** Confirmed. James III p. 316 has the guns and men ([p. 316](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/316/mode/1up)). Page 324 calls them "the Audacieuse and Foudre brigs", and p. 325 has Hamelin reaching Boulogne. Desbrière IV pp. 401–404 ([n60](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n60/mode/1up)) has the fight of 10 June, the passage from Fécamp on 23 July, and the arrival at Boulogne reported by the ordre du jour of 26 July. So the arrival was between 23 and 26 July. Desbrière's French version of James says 6 long 18s and 4 carronades (IV p. 402). Scholarly and period. High.
- **Transports: the 1803 plan for 1,000 chasse-marées and fishing boats; the types and draughts in the period table; 474 transports at Boulogne in March 1804.** Confirmed. Page 89 asks for "1000 chasse-marée, bateaux de pêche de toute espèce" able to use ports like Boulogne ([n100](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n100/mode/1up)). I read the "Flotille des Transports" side of the draught table myself ([n104](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n104/mode/1up)). The Blankenberge boat is at about 2¾ pieds, the chasse-marée about 6⅓–6½, the goélette 7, the smack fitted as a stable 8, the sloop fitted as a stable about 8½–8¾, the brig of 80 tonneaux 9 and the Dieppe boat about 9¼–9½. All are within a quarter foot of the note. The March 1804 figures are on p. 558 (see 2.1). Period. High.
- **Saint-Haouen's plan sent the transports into the roads only on the eve of a general sailing.** Confirmed. In his report of 15 thermidor an XIII (3 August 1805), the transports go out after the horses are embarked, one tide before the general sailing, and anchor behind the canonnières of their escadrille, "la veille ou le jour de l'appareillage général" (Desbrière IV pp. 399–400, [n56](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n56/mode/1up)). That none lay in the roads on an ordinary day is the note's inference. It agrees with every line return I opened, none of which lists transports. Period. Medium-High.
- **The Batavian flotilla: schooners and armed schuyts of about 75 tons with three guns each, chiefly long 24-pounders, plus French prames; based at Ambleteuse, part at Calais; brought into Boulogne only by the decree of 30 August 1805.** Confirmed, with additions. James III p. 316 has the eight captured Gallo-Batavian schuyts at about 75 tons, three guns each, chiefly long 24-pounders. Page 326 has the flotilla at Ambleteuse as the fifth grand division, for Davout's corps. Desbrière IV pp. 397–398 ([n54](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n54/mode/1up)) gives the July 1805 arrivals in French classes. 3 prames, 26 canonnières, 38 bateaux canonniers, 23 péniches and 9 transports came to Ambleteuse. 1 prame, 10 canonnières, 5 bateaux, 3 péniches and 11 transports went into Calais. On 26 August Napoleon suggested shutting the Batavian flotilla up in Boulogne (Corr. XI No. 9137, [p. 143](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/143/mode/1up)). The decree of 30 August, art. 4, places the rest of the flotilla, "soit française, soit batave", afloat above the dam (No. 9164, pp. 165–166). When this was carried out is not stated. On 17–20 July the Boulogne line itself moved up to between Ambleteuse and Audresselles to cover the Batavians (IV p. 397), so Batavian craft passed Boulogne's roads but did not lie there. Period and scholarly. Medium-High.
- **Show craft: "a heavy gun-brig and two lugger-rigged yachts, painted with white bottoms and green sides, and richly gilt" (James III p. 230).** Confirmed: the passage is on p. 230, in the account of the Constitution cutter on 26 August 1804 ([p. 230](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/230/mode/1up)). The péniches and caïque for the First Consul are on III p. 558 (see 2.1). That such craft were the exception is inference. Scholarly. Medium.

### 2.7 Colours

- **No period document on the flotilla's paint.** Confirmed as a negative, within what I searched. I searched the full texts of Desbrière III and IV, Corr. XI, James III, the Naval Chronicle XIV and Willaumez for "peint", "goudron", "noir", "couleur", "painted" and "black". None of them says how the gun craft were painted. The hits are beside the point, such as rusty shore-battery guns in the Calvados that "needed painting" (Desbrière III p. 490, [n521](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n521/mode/1up)). The only period text on the paint of any flotilla craft is James's account of 26 August 1804: "a heavy gun-brig and two lugger-rigged yachts, painted with white bottoms and green sides, and richly gilt" (III p. 230, [p. 230](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/230/mode/1up)). Those are the exceptions. I could not search Dumas XII: the local copy of its text is an error page. Medium for the absence.
- **Lewis's watercolour of 1804.** Confirmed, and the date can be narrowed. The museum catalogues PAI6989 as "Plan and key. The Attack on Boulogne Oct 1804", a watercolour with pen and black ink. Its original title says the view was "taken at anchor by E. D. Lewis H.M.S. Tartarus off Boulogne". Keith's flagship Monarch is at the bottom centre ([RMG](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-157586)). I looked at the museum's image. The craft of the line have dark grey hulls with no band. Each has a tricolour on a staff at the stern, and most have a red pennant at the masthead. Most are two-masted; a few have three masts. Period. High for what the drawing shows; it is naive and small.
- **Hue's painting (Salon of 1806).** Confirmed. I looked at the detail by the jetties in the Commons copy. The hulls are dark brown to black. The luggers' sails are cream. The masts are dark brown. One two-masted craft at the left of the detail has dark brown sails. Hue painted it in the studio for the Salon. Period. Medium.
- **Pocock's watercolour.** Confirmed. In the watercolour signed 1805, the harbour craft show as a dense comb of thin, dark brown-grey masts behind the white fort and the jetty. No hull of a harbour craft can be seen. Period. High for what it shows.
- **Baugean's engraving.** Confirmed. It is a monochrome print. Its imprint says it was deposited at the "Bibliothèque Impériale", so it came out after May 1804. It tells nothing about colour ([Gallica](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/btv1b8413293m)). Period.
- **The Foudroyante model "in ebony and boxwood".** **Corrected** in part. The conclusion stands: the woods of a fine model are not evidence of paint. But I could not confirm "ebony and boxwood". The English Wikipedia list of the Trianon models says only that the collection used precious woods, such as ebony and ivory, for its carvings. It adds, unsourced, that this model is a fictitious representative ship, restored for the collection and never finished ([Trianon model collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trianon_model_collection)). The museum's own record (webmuseo record 9024) came back as an empty page. Modern. Low.
- **Inference for the painter: dark hulls, no bright bands.** Confirmed as inference. Two independent period images agree on dark hulls: Lewis from the roads and Hue from the heights. Neither shows a band. Neither can decide between black and a dark tarred brown. A pale strake on the prames is still unsupported. Dark, fouled bottoms are a sound inference, because the harbour craft sat on the mud at low water (see 2.10). Medium for dark hulls; Low for the exact shade.
- **Sails.** Confirmed as inference. Hue's craft with dark brown sails is there, as the note says. No document on the flotilla's sailcloth was found. Low-Medium.
- **Flags of linen ("toile").** **Corrected.** Willaumez p. 444 says that in France the ensigns, guidons, cornettes and pennants "d'armement" were generally of "toile", where foreign navies used "étamine", bunting ([n491](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n491/mode/1up)). Under Étamine (p. 273) he says that in France alone they were of "toile blanche" ([n299](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n299/mode/1up)). "Toile" is plain woven cloth: linen, hemp or cotton. It is not specifically linen. In 1825 the French ensign was plain white, so "toile blanche" describes the Bourbon flag. Willaumez does not say what the dyed tricolours of 1805 were made of. That French flags hung more limply is inference. Period (1825). Low-Medium.

### 2.8 Flags, pennants and numbers

- **Ensign: three equal vertical bands, blue at the staff, by the decree of 27 pluviôse an II.** Confirmed. I read the decree on the page image of the Archives parlementaires, vol. LXXXV p. 78, in Persée's edition ([p. 78](https://www.persee.fr/doc/arcpa_0000-0000_1964_num_85_1_31787_t1_0078_0000_2)). Article II: three equal bands, set vertically, the blue attached to the staff ("gaule"), the white in the middle, the red flying. Article III: the jack and the ordinary stern ensign are arranged the same way, in the sizes set by usage. Article V: the new flag goes up in every ship on 1 prairial an II (20 May 1794). I found no later change before 1814, but I did not search the regulations of the Consulate and the Empire. Lewis's view shows the flag on a staff at the stern. Willaumez p. 443 says the large stern ensign is hoisted at the mizzen gaff ("corne d'artimon") in every vessel that sets a "brigantine" ([n490](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n490/mode/1up)). Period. High for the flag; Medium for where luggers wore it.
- **Ensign size.** Confirmed. The OCR of p. 443 is garbled here, so I read the page image. The large stern ensign has the vessel's beam for its fly and two-thirds of the beam for its hoist. Page 444 gives the medium ensign two-thirds of the large and the jack a quarter ([n491](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n491/mode/1up)). Willaumez says these sizes are about the same in all nations. Under Flamme (p. 291) he says smaller vessels wear smaller flags and pennants ([n318](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n318/mode/1up)). That supports the note's inference for small craft. The 1794 decree leaves sizes to usage (article III), so Willaumez's rule is the nearest one found. The note's figures follow from it. Period rule (1825). Medium-Low.
- **Masthead pennant: the ensign's colours, a pointed end, at least twice the beam long.** Confirmed. Willaumez p. 291 ([n318](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n318/mode/1up)): pennants in the colours of the stern ensign fly at the main masthead to mark warships, and the flying end is pointed. A split end is never seen in a division, squadron or fleet, where all pennants are alike. Page 444: pennants are at least twice the beam long and one metre wide at the sleeve. So the note's 11 m for a chaloupe is a minimum, not a typical length. Page 291 also makes these pennants five or six times longer and narrower than the 25-pied signal pennants, with a small stick of about 18 pouces (0.49 m) in the sleeve. For a large ship that means 40 m or more. The two rules do not quite agree. On most mastheads in Lewis's view I saw a red pennant, but not on every one. Period. Medium.
- **"The order of the three colours along the 1805 pennant was not found."** **Corrected.** Article IV of the same decree, on the same page, says the pennant "sera pareillement formée de trois couleurs, dont un cinquième bleu, un cinquième blanc, et les trois cinquièmes rouge". On an 11 m pennant that is about 2.2 m of blue, 2.2 m of white and 6.6 m of red. This explains the red pennants in Lewis's view: from a distance the long red tail is what shows. The decree does not say which end is blue. "Pareillement" (likewise) and article II, with its blue at the staff, point to blue at the mast. The plate facing Desbrière III p. 90 fits: its "Ordinaire" pennant is hatched next to the staff, then plain, then tapers to its point ([n102](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n102/mode/1up)). The plate does not explain its hatching, and the proportions cannot be read at its size. Item 4 of the list stands. Period. High for the proportions; Medium for the blue at the mast.
- **Command marks.** Confirmed as Willaumez's rules, with a caveat. Page 444: the cornettes and command guidons are half the beam long and a sixth of it wide, split to a point for half their length. Under Guidon (p. 332, [n363](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n363/mode/1up)): the mark of a chef de division commanding at least three warships, in the national colours, flown at the main masthead without a stick. The same entry calls it longer than an ensign, which does not fit half the beam. These are the navy's rules of 1825. I found nothing on what the flotilla's division commanders flew in 1805. The plate facing Desbrière III p. 90 shows, at top right, a short swallow-tailed flag marked "Amiral" and a long pennant marked "Ordinaire" ([n102](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n102/mode/1up)). The "Amiral" flag is hatched at the hoist, plain in the middle and dark at the fly. It is about two and a half times as long as it is deep, so "broad" overstates it. James III p. 232 has Lacrosse's flag in the Ville-de-Mayence, "stationed in the centre of the line" ([p. 232](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/232/mode/1up)). Period and scholarly. Medium for the plate and for Lacrosse; Low for guidons in the 1805 flotilla.
- **Signals.** Confirmed: the ordre du jour of 16 April 1804, Desbrière III p. 560 ([n591](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n591/mode/1up)). When the general signals to rectify the line, the head, centre and rear craft hoist at the foremast "le pavillon, égal à tous", one and the same flag, as their distinguishing signal. Captains are forbidden private signals among themselves and must always use the flotilla's signals. Period. High.
- **Numbers.** Confirmed, each at the page given. Desbrière III p. 557: "le bateau de 1re espèce n° 1", Captain Auquiet, which ran for Ambleteuse or Wimereux in March 1804 with its bowsprit gone ([n588](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n588/mode/1up)). James III p. 233: the péniche No. 267, and Nos. 149 and 142 "stationed in the second line", October 1804 ([p. 233](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/233/mode/1up)). Desbrière IV p. 396: chaloupe No. 275, driven off in May 1805 and found at Fécamp ([n53](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n53/mode/1up)). Naval Chronicle XIV p. 339: gun-boats 62, 306, 291, 280, 305, 138, 34 and 61 ([p. 339](https://archive.org/details/navalchronicleco14londiala/page/339/mode/1up)). I read No. 306 on the page image. Where the numbers were painted I could not find either. Period. High for the numbers; unknown for their display.

### 2.9 Sizes, mast heights and apparent sizes

- **Hull figures: design dimensions from Desbrière III p. 90, draughts read from the table facing it.** Confirmed. Page 90 gives each type's length, breadth and "tirant d'eau" ([n101](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n101/mode/1up)). The third figure is therefore the draught, as the note treats it, not the depth. All conversions are right (see Recomputations). The loaded draughts are the ones the check read on the plate (2.2–2.5). Period. High.
- **Prame: 35.7 × 8.1 m, 2.8 m loaded.** Confirmed: 110 × 25 pieds; 8½ pieds loaded on the table, 8 pieds (2.6 m) in the design. Period. High.
- **Chaloupe canonnière: 24.7–26.0 × 5.5 m, 1.8 m loaded (new type).** Confirmed: 76–80 × 17 pieds, 5½ pieds. Period. High.
- **Bateau canonnier: 19.5 × 4.5 m, "draught loaded 1.1–1.5 m".** **Corrected** (label). Only the 1.1 m (3½ pieds) comes from the loaded table. The 1.5 m is the 4½-pied design draught of p. 90. Period. High.
- **Péniche: 19.5 m (2nd kind 13.6 m) × 3.25 m, 1.1 m.** Confirmed: p. 90, and Willaumez p. 447 (see 2.5). Period. High.
- **Caïque: 14.0 × 3.4 m, 0.95 m.** Confirmed as converted from Willaumez p. 123 ([n138](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n138/mode/1up)): 43 p 3 po long, 10 p 6 po wide, drawing 2 p 11 po. The figures themselves belong to 2.6. Period. High.
- **Side above water.** None of the sources I opened gives a freeboard. Every figure is an estimate.
  - Prame, 1.8–2.5 m to the top of the bulwark: unsupported, since no source gives a figure. On the Commons photo of the Foudroyante model, the rail stands about 0.08–0.095 of the hull length above the line of the copper. For 35.7 m that is about 3 m. The model is a type reference only. Low.
  - Chaloupe, 1.0–1.5 m: unsupported as a figure, but a fair inference. Low.
  - Bateau canonnier, 0.8–1.2 m with the washboard: agrees with item 9 of the list. Inference. Low.
  - Péniche, about 0.5 m: confirmed as an inference, and it has a period basis the note does not give. Willaumez gives the 1st-kind péniche 5 pieds of "creux" (p. 447, [n494](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n494/mode/1up)). Desbrière's table gives 3½ pieds of loaded draught. The difference is 1½ pieds, 0.49 m. Willaumez measures the creux from the top of the keel to the top of the beam (s.v. Creux, p. 194, [n209](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n209/mode/1up)), while the draught counts the keel too. So add a little: about 0.5–0.7 m. The 0.9 m on the plate (2.5) is not to scale. Period figures, inference. Low-Medium.
  - Caïque, about 0.5 m: by the same method, 4 pieds of creux less 2 p 11 po of draught is 0.35 m. With the keel added, about 0.4–0.6 m. Agrees. Low.
- **Mast heights are estimates; no sail plan was found.** Confirmed. I found none in the downloaded texts either.
- **Prame, 28–36 m, by Steel's rule.** The arithmetic is confirmed. (35.7 + 8.1) / 2 gives a 21.9 m mainmast, a 13.1 m topmast and a 6.6 m topgallant. With the note's deductions the main truck is 35.6 m. With Steel's masthead lengths (5 inches per yard of lower mast, 4 per yard of topmast; `victory-rig.md` 1.1) it is 35.1 m.
  - The proportions are Steel's. His 1794 establishment for 100 guns has a 117 ft mainmast, a 70 ft topmast (0.60) and a 35 ft topgallant (0.50). For Victory's 186 ft gun deck and 52 ft beam the half-sum rule gives 119 ft. Sources: `victory-rig.md` 1.1; the rule as cited from Steel I, "Mastmaking", pp. 39–40, in `session-17/small-craft.md`.
  - "Close to French practice" is **unsupported**: no French masting rule was opened.
  - The rule takes the gun-deck length. The 110 pieds is James's "extreme length", so the result may be about a metre too high.
  - The checks: the Foudroyante model's main topgallant stands at least a hull length above its waterline (my look at the Commons photo, whose frame cuts off the top). That confirms the proportion for the model. In Hue's painting the ship-rigged craft inside the jetty has its hull mostly hidden by the jetty and another craft, and its masts run out of the crop I have, so I could not confirm the proportion there. The Ariadne comparison is about hulls, not masts.
  - Inference. Low. The 28–36 m range stands, and its upper half is better supported.
- **Chaloupe canonnière, 16–24 m.** **Corrected** (one step).
  - The rule gives a 15.4 m mainmast. With the deck 1 m above the water, the heel 2.4 m below the deck and Steel's masthead lengths, the topgallant head stands at about 24.7 m. The note's 24–25 m with a topgallant is right.
  - Without a topgallant, the same rule still leaves the topmast head at about 21 m, not 16–20 m.
  - Steel's merchant brig of 150 tons (main 60 ft, topmast 30 ft, topgallant 16½ ft; `session-17/small-craft.md`) has a topmast only half the lower mast. With those proportions the head comes to about 23 m.
  - Only the "simplified" masting asked for in July 1803 (Desbrière III p. 88), which is not described, could bring the truck below about 20 m. The table's 18 m therefore assumes that rig. A brig with topmasts stands 20–25 m.
  - Inference. Low.
- **Péniche, about 9 m.** Confirmed: 0.47 × 19.5 m = 9.2 m, using the ratio the check measured on the plate (2.5). The drawing is not to scale. Low.
- **Bateau canonnier, 10–14 m.** Unsupported as a figure. It is an interpolation, 0.51–0.72 of the length. Two signs suggest it may be low:
  - On the plate facing p. 90, the lug-rigged caïque has its mastheads about 0.62–0.64 of its length above the drawn waterline. This is my own measurement, to about ±5 %.
  - In Hue's painting, the luggers by the jetty carry their mastheads about one hull-length above the water (a rough measure on a crop 1550 px wide). For a 19.5 m bateau that would mean 12–19 m. But Hue's luggers cannot be identified as flotilla craft, and he painted them in the studio.
  - Inference. Low.
- **Caïque, 7–9 m.** Unsupported: the note gives no method. On the plate, the lug-rigged caïque's mastheads come to 8.7–9.0 m, and its yard peaks to about 9.7 m. The sloop-rigged caïque beside it carries a topmast and stands taller. If its hull, cut by the fold, is as long, its masthead is about 13–15 m. So 7–9 m fits the lug-rigged caïque, at the top of the range. Period drawing, not to scale. Low.
- **The apparent-size table.** Confirmed. I recomputed all 32 angles: the hull as 2·atan(L/2d), the mast as atan(H/d). All of them agree to the last digit given.
  - The mast column measures from the waterline, as if the eye were at water level. From the painting's eye, 6 m up, the span from waterline to truck differs by 0.23° for the prame at 100 m, and by 0.01° or less from 300 m out.
  - The eye's level splits the span. At 1 km a chaloupe's waterline lies 0.34° below it and an 18 m masthead 0.69° above it.
  - Section 1's summary agrees: 2° and 1.7°, 1.45° and 1°, 1.1° and 0.6°, a third of that at 3 km, three times at 300 m.
  - Computed. High for the arithmetic. The mast columns keep the Low of the heights.
- **Section 1's size bullets** agree with the table, apart from the corrections above: a chaloupe with topmasts reaches 20–25 m, and the luggers' masts may be taller than given.

### 2.10 How they lay

**In the harbour**

- **"Nearly dry at low water ... a dam that held the water in to keep the craft afloat" (James III p. 217).** **Corrected.** James does say it: an estuary "nearly dry at low water, with only one quay", and later "by means of a dam the waters were confined, and the vessels kept afloat" ([p. 217](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/217/mode/1up)). But Desbrière's documents show that this is too generous. The arrêté of 14 March 1803 ordered a semicircular "bassin d'échouage", a drying basin, and a retaining dike with a flushing sluice to deepen the channel (III p. 149, [n168](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n168/mode/1up)). The dam itself was decided only on 21 April 1804: a provisional dam of earth held by piles, with a wooden lock 30 pieds wide and a swinging flushing gate (III p. 452, [n479](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n479/mode/1up)). It held water only upstream of itself. The decree of 30 August 1805 puts the craft not needed for sorties "à flot au delà du barrage", afloat above the dam (Corr. XI No. 9164, art. 4). Below the dam, along the quays and in the basin, the craft sat on the bottom at low water. Period and scholarly. High.
- **"Before the works even craft as shallow as the flotilla's lay aground almost all the time in most of the port, and the entrance could be used only for a short time around high water" (III p. 141).** **Corrected.** The words are on p. 141 ([n158](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n158/mode/1up)), but they are not limited to the time before the works. They open Desbrière's reconstruction of the port "en 1803–1805". His conclusion for those years is on pp. 147–148 ([n165](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n165/mode/1up)). Three fifths of the port lay above +4 m. Craft drawing 6 pieds floated there for about four hours at springs and three and a half at neaps. Nearly half the lower basin and almost all the upper basin could not float the canonnières at neaps. Outside the narrow channel, "l'échouage à peu près permanent était la règle pour les navires de la flottille". This supports item 3 of the list above. Scholarly from period plans. High.
- **The plan of Thermidor an XIII, "Dispositions d'appareillage de la flottille impériale du port de Boulogne": tiers of five to ten, bows to the quay, by escadrille; péniches round the basin, canonnières along the channel, prames and bomb craft by the basin entrance, transports farther up; reproduced by Desbrière IV as a folding plate.** Confirmed for what the plan shows, on the Commons copy (2.13): péniches round the semicircular basin, canonnières along both banks, prames at the entrance to the basin, transports up the river. The tiers and the bows to the quay were not checked. Saint-Haouen's report of 15 thermidor an XIII (3 August 1805) is on IV pp. 398–400 ([n55](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n55/mode/1up)). It ends by saying the craft must first be ranged in port "tels que les présente le plan". A folding plate follows p. 400. On the image of p. 400 its folded edge shows rows of hull symbols and part of a legend, but the plate itself could not be opened. **Corrected** in weight: Desbrière prints the report to show it was not serious. He is surprised that Dumas reproduced it "sérieusement" (p. 398), and after it he writes that its contradictions and material errors are too obvious to need comment (p. 400, [n57](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n57/mode/1up)). The plan shows a proposed order for a general sailing, not how the craft lay on an ordinary day. Period. Medium-Low for the arrangement in port.
- **"Forest of masts" (Naval Chronicle XIV p. 481).** Confirmed. The letter says that "the forest of masts which you see in the harbour is part of the grand flotilla", said to be above a thousand vessels ([p. 481](https://archive.org/details/navalchronicleco14londiala/page/481/mode/1up)). I read it in the full text and did not check the printed page on the image. The paintings were not re-examined in this pass. Period. High.
- **The decrees of 26 and 30 August 1805.** Confirmed, and item 3's citation of both stands. No. 9137 (26 August, to Berthier) asks that the whole Boulogne flotilla, except the prames and chaloupes canonnières, be kept "à flot au delà du barrage". On the same page it suggests shutting the Batavian flotilla up in Boulogne (Corr. XI [p. 143](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/143/mode/1up)). No. 9164, art. 4, places all the rest of the flotilla, French or Batavian, afloat above the dam, and the prames in the "bassin circulaire". Art. 3 keeps an armed division ready to go out, to hold the enemy off and to practise taking "leur ligne d'embossage et leur ligne de combat" (pp. 165–166, as in 2.1). A first reading in this session found the phrase only in No. 9164; it is in No. 9137 too. Both letters come after 10 August, so they say where craft were to be kept from September, not where they lay that evening. Period. High.

**In the roads**

- **What the line was: gun craft moored in the open roads outside the pier, under the shore batteries, to keep bombs off the harbour and town; divisions took turns; never more out than could come back on one tide; about 100 per tide by 1805, falling.** Confirmed. The Naval Chronicle letter has the craft "moored outside to prevent our bombs throwing shells into the harbour and town" (XIV p. 481, [p. 481](https://archive.org/details/navalchronicleco14londiala/page/481/mode/1up)). Dumas XI p. 49 has the divisions that "mouillaient sur la rade de Boulogne, et s'y relevaient successivement". Desbrière III p. 545 has the rule of one tide ([n576](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n576/mode/1up)), and p. 566 sets it at 100. IV p. 398 says the 100 kept falling to the end ([n55](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n55/mode/1up)). Added: the roads' wings were to be held by forts on the rocks of l'Heurt (left) and la Crèche (right), 2,400 toises (4.7 km) apart, with a fort between them for the centre (III p. 153, [n172](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n172/mode/1up)). Period and scholarly. High.
- **How many, 1804: 65 on 23 March; 120 on 15 April (2 prames, 30 chaloupes, 9 bombardes); over 150 on 16 April; 146 on 25 August; about 150 in a double line in October, the admiral's prame in the centre.** Confirmed. Lafond on 23 March: 15 craft of the 1st kind, 42 of the 2nd, 6 bombardes, 2 galiotes, 65 in all (III p. 556, [n587](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n587/mode/1up)). Soult on p. 559 and the ordre du jour on p. 560. Dumas XI has 62 + 42 + 6 bomb boats + 36 péniches, which makes 146, with the British on a parallel line a league and a half away. It is on p. 45 ([n58](https://archive.org/details/prcisdesvnement07dumagoog/page/n58/mode/1up); see 2.12). James III p. 232 has "about 150 vessels, moored in a double line outside the pier of Boulogne", with Lacrosse's flag in the Ville-de-Mayence prame "stationed in the centre of the line" ([p. 232](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/232/mode/1up)). Period and scholarly. High.
- **How many, 1805 (Desbrière IV pp. 395–398).** Confirmed for the entries I opened. There were 64 craft on 9 March (27 canonnières, 3 bombardes, 27 bateaux, 6 péniches and 1 sloop-bombarde), which came in six days later. On 13–15 June: 27 chaloupes, 21 bateaux, a brig, 3 bateaux bombardes, two divisions of péniches with Prussian howitzers, 2 caïques and armed boats. There were 47 on 21–25 June, 54 on 1–2 July, and the same 54 for eleven days from 10 July. On 17 July the whole line, 27 canonnières and 27 bateaux, moved up between Ambleteuse and Audresselles with 36 péniches. Everyone came in on 21 July ([n52](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n52/mode/1up), [n54](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n54/mode/1up), [n55](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n55/mode/1up)). None of these lines lists a prame. Period. High.
- **August 1805: no craft in the roads on 3–6 August, no mention of a line afterwards; the sortie of 12 August; Napoleon's "single line" of 26 July a flourish.** Confirmed. Desbrière IV p. 398: when the Emperor arrived on 3 August there was not one craft in the roads, and this was so until 6 August. After that Lafond's letters have many gaps, and neither the flotilla's reports nor the generals' make the slightest allusion to a line ([n55](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n55/mode/1up)). No. 9022 has the 2,000 craft forming "une seule ligne d'embossage dans toutes les rades, depuis Étaples jusqu'au cap Grisnez" ([p. 43](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/43/mode/1up)). The sortie of 12 August is confirmed in 2.2. I did not reopen Nos. 9069, 9073 and 9074. So for 9–11 August no line is documented, which supports item 2. Period. High for 3–6 August; Low for any line on 10 August.
- **Later in 1805: a line of 27 gun-boats, its head to the east (gun-boat No. 62), at the end of September; attempts on "the line of gun-vessels at anchor in the road" (James III p. 333).** Confirmed for the parts I opened. Lacrosse's report has "the Gun-boat No. 62, head of the Line to the East" (Naval Chronicle XIV p. 339, [p. 339](https://archive.org/details/navalchronicleco14londiala/page/339/mode/1up)). James's phrase is on p. 333 ([p. 333](https://archive.org/details/navalhistoryofgr03jameuoft/page/333/mode/1up)). On the page image the line, "consisting of 27 Gun-boats alone", had 23 British vessels in front of it: no prame. I did not reopen No. 9209. Period. High.
- **Where: the rade de Saint-Jean, 5–15 m, marl and mud, wide open, the Bassure de Baas no shelter; a foreshore 700–800 m wide at the head of the bay and 450 m at la Crèche, which kept the line far from the land (III p. 141).** Confirmed ([n158](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n158/mode/1up)). Added from the same page: the anchors "se perdent souvent", and the flood stream sets north-north-east, toward the English coast, at up to 4 knots. Saint-Haouen's "fond excellent" that rises gently toward the land is on IV p. 399 ([n56](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n56/mode/1up)). Scholarly from period plans. High.
- **The distances: 500 "fathoms" off the entrance in 1801; "four or five miles" of line in July 1804 with the British about three miles off; Dumas's league and a half; in 1805 the lines pushed beyond the batteries' reach.** Confirmed except the last. The French account of 4 August 1801 has a division anchored "at the distance of 500 fathoms before the entrance of the port", with the British at 1,900 "fathoms" from it (Naval Chronicle XIV pp. 400–401, [p. 401](https://archive.org/details/navalchronicleco14londiala/page/401/mode/1up)). If these are toises, as the note supposes, that is 975 m and 3.7 km. The 1804 letter and Dumas XI are as the note says. I could not reopen Dumas XII p. 39: the local copy of vol. XII's text is an error page. Period. Medium.
- **The low-water line and the wooden fort (list item 1).** Added; the list's figures stand, but one page needs changing. Desbrière cites a plan by the engineer Cadier, of 1768, marked with "3 m 40 of slope from the west jetty down to low water". He adds that low water lies 386 toises (750 m) from the jetty (III p. 142, [n159](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n159/mode/1up)). The fascine dike from the Pidou jetty ran 700 m out, "jusqu'à la laisse de basse mer de vive eau" (III p. 148, [n165](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n165/mode/1up)). **Corrected** page: the wooden fort at the end of that dike is on III p. **155**, not pp. 142 or 148. That page names it "fort en charpente", then fort Rouge, then fort de l'Expédition ([n174](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n174/mode/1up)). Page 158 adds that it could best be attacked at low water, "puisque alors il se découvre" ([n177](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n177/mode/1up)). So the fort stood at about the low-water line. Scholarly from period plans. Medium-High.
- **How far out the line lay (new evidence for item 1).** Added. On 4 September 1803 Napoleon found the line Bruix had traced weak, because "il y a 22 pieds d'eau à basse mer" there, about 7 m. That was enough for two-deckers to attack it. He wanted a first position for an advance guard as close to the shore as possible, under fort Rouge and the shore batteries (III p. 161, [n180](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n180/mode/1up)). Saint-Haouen in 1805 also wanted to make sure that at high water the enemy could not pass inshore of the line (IV p. 400, [n57](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n57/mode/1up)). Both put the line well seaward of the low-water line. That agrees with item 1 of the list. Period. Medium for 1803–1805 in general; Low for August 1805.
- **"A single line, or a double line in strength, parallel to the shore, about 1–2 km off the beach and beyond the low-water line, running from off the harbour mouth north-eastward toward Wimereux; head at the north-east end, left at the south-west end; for a camera looking east the head is on the left."** **Corrected.** (a) The head to the north-east and the left to the south-west are confirmed: "head of the Line to the East" (XIV p. 339), and the British "dans le sud-ouest ... au vent du dernier bâtiment de la gauche de la ligne" (Dumas XI p. 49). For a camera looking east the head is on the left. (b) "From off the harbour mouth north-eastward" is **refuted** as the whole extent. The roads ran from l'Heurt on the left to la Crèche on the right, with the centre off the harbour (III p. 153). The admiral's prame lay in the centre of the 1804 line (James III p. 232). On 16 April 1804 a section of 1st-class craft, 2 prames and some bombardes lay "dans l'ouest du port". Lafond's postscript calls them "notre gauche". They had to come in on the evening tide when the wind threatened to go round to the north (III p. 560, [n591](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n591/mode/1up)). So the line ran on both sides of the mouth, which supports item 6. (c) "1–2 km off the beach" should be measured from the pier heads for the camera. The documents give the 1801 division at about 975 m off the entrance and the 1803 trace in about 7 m at low water. Inference from period figures. Medium-Low.
- **Spacing and order: equal distances, bearings, head, centre and rear as guides (ordre du jour of 16 April 1804, III p. 560).** Confirmed ([n591](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n591/mode/1up)). The distances were to be equal "de manière ... qu'un bâtiment ne puisse nuire à un autre". The head, centre and rear craft hoisted a flag at the foremast as guides when the line was to be dressed. Period. High.
- **Saint-Haouen's order: prames and chaloupes in front, two prames side by side at each end and one every twenty canonnières, bateaux in a third line with péniches between, transports behind; four cables, two anchors (IV pp. 399–400).** Confirmed as text, **corrected** as evidence. The words are there ([n56](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n56/mode/1up), [n57](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n57/mode/1up)). Each craft had four cables, spliced two by two to port and starboard, and each was to carry at least 5 guns. Once the line held only prames and canonnières, each was to have two cables out on one anchor: it would seem to lie at its usual berth, as near the land as usual, but could drop a cable further out at will, and was to haul out only at low water on the eve of the general sailing. But this is a proposal for the day of a general embarkation. In it the prames and canonnières come out to take the place of the bateaux and péniches "qui faisaient partie de la ligne d'embossage", so the usual line was made of those smaller craft. Desbrière then dismisses the report as full of contradictions and errors (p. 400). The rule of prames in pairs at the ends and one every twenty brigs was never seen in any line. It must not be painted as the 1805 order, and section 1 must say so. Period. High for the text; Low as a picture of any real line.
- **Spacing in metres: 45–55 m (inference).** **Corrected**, in agreement with item 5. The 45–55 m assumes about 150 craft in a single row over 6–8 km. The 1804 line was double (James III p. 232). Napoleon's letter of 4 September 1803 places two or three caïques in each interval between chaloupes (III p. 161). With a caïque 14 m long and room to swing, that points to intervals of at least about 60 m. A double line of 54 craft, 27 to a row, at 80–110 m gives about 2.1–2.9 km, about the same length as a single row at 45–55 m. Inference. Low.
- **Pickets and boats (III p. 560); booms after October 1804; armed boats in by night (IV p. 397).** Confirmed for III p. 560 and IV p. 397. The bomb craft were "sur l'arrière de la ligne" (see 2.6). The picket péniches were one cable ahead, the other péniches "entre la terre et les extrémités de la ligne", inshore of its ends, as corrected in 2.5. The boats of the moored craft patrolled the line from sunset to sunrise. The armed boats that went out on 14 June 1805 came in before night "suivant leur habitude". I did not reopen the booms (Dumas XI p. 49 continued; James III p. 234). Period. High.
- **In and out on the tide; a day to two weeks out; in winter the roads held one day in eighteen.** Confirmed. Craft went out "à la marée du matin" (III p. 559, [n590](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n590/mode/1up)) and came in "à la marée du soir" (p. 560). Saint-Haouen calls the roads deadly in "un coup de vent ordinaire de l'ouest au nord-est" (IV p. 398). Page 565 has "un jour sur dix-huit" for November to April ([n596](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n596/mode/1up)). Period. High.
- **Heading at low water (list item 7).** Not a claim of the note; the list's inference stands, with one source added. Desbrière III p. 141 gives the flood setting north-north-east at up to 4 knots, so the streams run along the shore. Craft riding to the stream lay parallel to the shore and to the line, broadside to a camera at sea. Which way the bows pointed at low water depends on when the stream turns. No tidal-stream table for Boulogne was found in what I opened, so the north-north-east heading stays an inference. Low.

### 2.11 At anchor

- **Yards crossed and sails furled in the 1804 watercolour.** Confirmed. I looked at the museum's image of RMG PAI6989 (1600 px) and at crops of the French line ([RMG record](https://www.rmg.co.uk/collections/objects/rmgc-object-157586)). Almost every craft in the line has two masts with square yards crossed and the sails furled on them. Each has a tricolour on a staff at the stern and a long pennant at the masthead. A few craft in the line have a single mast with a gaff sail, and at least one has three masts. All the ensigns and pennants stream to the left and the bowsprits point right, so all the craft head the same way. Period. Medium: the drawing is naive.
- **Bare masts and yards in Hue's harbour.** Confirmed, with a nuance. I looked at the Commons copy (1945 px) and crops of it. Most craft in the harbour show bare masts and yards. In the channel by the jetty, a two-masted craft and several luggers are under sail. So the picture shows harbour traffic, not the line at anchor. Period, painted in the studio. Medium.
- **"Divisions weighed and fought within minutes" (Dumas XI pp. 45–48).** **Corrected** (wording). Dumas p. 45 describes 25 August 1804. The weather was fine and the sea "peu houleuse". At 2 p.m. a British corvette came in to observe the line and fired a few broadsides. It drew off as soon as the first division of chaloupes canonnières weighed and left the line. On the next day a division of first-kind craft weighed and formed a line of battle south-west of the Fort de l'Heurt (p. 48). Dumas gives no times. "Within minutes" is the note's own reading of "aussitôt", meaning as soon as ([Dumas XI](https://archive.org/details/prcisdesvnement07dumagoog); I read the passage in a full-text copy, not on the page image). Lacrosse's report of 1 October 1805 shows the same readiness: at midnight he made "immediate dispositions" for several divisions to go out (Naval Chronicle XIV p. 339). That sails stayed bent to the yards and furled is a sound inference. Period and inference. Medium.
- **Lug yards lowered at anchor.** **Unsupported.** No source I read says so. No image shows a lugger at anchor clearly enough to tell. Lewis's line shows square-rigged craft, Hue's luggers are under way and Baugean's are going out. This is general practice, an inference. Low.
- **Sails loosed to dry are furled again by evening.** Inference, with no source. Acceptable as inference. Low.
- **The ensign flies by day, and no French rule of 1805 on hauling it down at sunset was found.** Confirmed as the note states it. I found no such rule either. Willaumez's flag entries have no "coucher du soleil". Desbrière III–IV mention sunset only in the order of 16 April 1804 that boats row along the line "depuis le coucher du soleil jusqu'à son lever" (III p. 560, [n591](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n591/mode/1up)). The rule does not matter for the painting. Its light is that of 18:33 LAT, 52 minutes before sunset at 19:26 (`evening.md`), so the ensigns fly under any rule. Lewis shows them flying at anchor. Medium-Low for the rule. High that the ensigns fly at 18:33.
- **The masthead pennant marked a warship and stayed up.** Confirmed in part. Willaumez, s.v. Flamme, says that pennants in the colour of the stern ensign, worn at the head of the mainmast, mark warships ([n318](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n318/mode/1up)). He does not say the pennant stayed up at night; that is inference. Lewis shows pennants worn at anchor. For the pennant's colours see item 4 of the list above: mostly red. Period. Medium.
- **No riding lights are recorded.** Confirmed: I found none either. In Desbrière III and IV I searched for "fanal", "fanaux", "lanterne" and "feux". Volume III has only a lightship with three lights off the North Foreland and the lantern on Boulogne's jetty (see the last bullet). Volume IV has nothing. Willaumez, s.v. Fanal, describes glazed lanterns lit by large yellow wax candles. In squadrons they gave night signals, hoisted in combinations at mastheads or yardarms. Battle lanterns hung from the beams. Stern lanterns he calls a thing of the past ("autrefois"): flagships had three, with a fourth abaft the main top ([n307](https://archive.org/details/dictionnairedem00marigoog/page/n307/mode/1up)). He describes no light for a ship at anchor. Period. Medium: this rests on the absence of evidence.
- **At night the line relied on guard boats and pickets.** Confirmed. The ordre du jour of 16 April 1804 says that from sunset to sunrise the light boats of the moored craft rowed continually along the line, to warn of the enemy (Desbrière III p. 560, [n591](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n591/mode/1up)). Soult's report of 13 April 1804 posts péniches de vedette at night ahead of the line and on its wings (p. 559, [n590](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n590/mode/1up)). Period. High.
- **Night attacks were known only by gunfire and explosions (Naval Chronicle XIV p. 339; James III pp. 233–234).** Confirmed for the Naval Chronicle. In Lacrosse's report of 1 October 1805, at midnight, "the weather being very thick, and the Sea calm", he heard firing in the line. The enemy's pinnaces owed their safety "to the darkness of the night" ([p. 339](https://archive.org/details/navalchronicleco14londiala/page/339/mode/1up); I read the page image). James is a British account, not a French report. His account of the catamaran night of 2–3 October 1804 begins on p. 232. In the part I read, the admiral's prame slackens her cables and lets a fire vessel drift past, and the last explosion comes at about 3.30 a.m. I found no French lights mentioned there, but I did not read the whole account. Period. Medium.
- **Lanterns were used for night signals.** Confirmed: Willaumez, s.v. Fanal (above). Period (1825). Medium.
- **At dusk the line was dark but for a lantern or two, and the brighter lights were in the town and the camps.** A fair inference. One fact can be added. The harbour had a light. The arrêté of 14 messidor an XI (3 July 1803) had the lantern and the reflector light at the end of the jetty razed to make room for a 10-gun battery. A new lantern was to be built of wood, much higher, and far enough away not to hinder the battery (Desbrière III pp. 154–155, [n173](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n173/mode/1up)). Whether it was lit in wartime in 1805 was not found. Inference. Low.

### 2.12 Riding in a swell

- **"The flood sets north-north-east toward England at up to 4 knots" (Desbrière III p. 141).** Confirmed ([n158](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n158/mode/1up)). The flood "porte à la côte d'Angleterre" north-north-east, at a speed that reaches 4 "milles". This is Desbrière's own account of the roads, with no source named for the speed. Scholarly. High for the direction; Medium for the speed, which is a spring rate.
- **Tide-rode, bows into the stream, parallel to the coast; swinging to the wind at slack water or when the wind is the stronger.** Confirmed as inference (general seamanship). Medium.
- **"Moored with two anchors, the prames and canonnières swung little."** **Corrected.**
  - They were indeed moored with two anchors. In Saint-Haouen's plan, the canonnières in the roads first unmoor ("ayant désaffourché") before they sail (Desbrière IV p. 399, [n56](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n56/mode/1up)).
  - But mooring with two anchors only keeps the swinging circle small; it does not stop the swing. Sganzin's report of 6 December 1802 (Desbrière III p. 163, [n182](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n182/mode/1up)) says that canonnières moored on two anchors in the Canche "éviteront à chaque marée": they will swing with every tide.
  - So the whole line turned together at each change of the stream, and between the turns lay with its bows into it.
  - Period. Medium.
- **Seen broadside from seaward, all the same way: bows north-north-east on the ebb, south-south-west on the flood.** Confirmed as inference. The directions follow correctly from Desbrière's flood.
  - For the painting's moment (18:33, a quarter of an hour after the 18:18 low water; `evening.md` 4.1), I agree with item 7 of the list: bows to the north-north-east, to the viewer's left.
  - In the Strait the south-west-going stream runs through local low water and turns north-east an hour or two before local high water. This is general knowledge of the Channel's streams, not checked against a tidal-stream table.
  - A craft heading about 20° is seen 78° off the camera's axis (98°). That is nearly broadside, with the bows to the left and the stern and ensign to the right.
  - Inference. Low.
- **Sea state: "la mer peu houleuse", Dumas XI p. 45, 25 August 1804.** Confirmed ([n58](https://archive.org/details/prcisdesvnement07dumagoog/page/n58/mode/1up)). I read the passage in the full text and found its leaf with archive.org's text search. On that day the weather was fine and the sea "peu houleuse" while the line of 146 craft lay in the roads. "Slightly swelling" is a fair rendering of "with little swell". Period. High for that day. It shows that the line held in such weather, not how often the weather was like that. Desbrière III p. 141 gives the other side: south-west and west winds, which blew two days in five, generally made the roads untenable, and the Baas bank does not stop a heavy swell.
- **Natural roll periods: 3 s for a bateau canonnier or péniche, 4 s for a chaloupe, 5 s for a prame.** **Corrected** (inference).
  - The formula is right. With the note's inputs (k = 0.38 B, GM 1–1.5 m) it gives 2.8–3.5 s for the bateau, 3.4–4.2 s for the chaloupe, 2.0–2.5 s for the péniche (not 3 s) and 5.1–6.2 s for the prame.
  - The inputs are the weak point: the GM of 1–1.5 m is assumed.
  - A box-hull estimate for these craft, loaded with men, guns and masts high up, gives a different picture. BM = B²/12T comes to 0.8 m for the péniche, 1.5 m for the bateau, 1.4 m for the chaloupe and 2.0 m for the prame. That puts GM nearer 0.2–0.9 m and the roll periods at about 4–10 s, longest for the prame and the chaloupe.
  - Low.
- **"The swell is much longer, so they do not resonate. They tilt a degree or two each way in a half-metre swell, and their mastheads sweep a few decimetres. The péniches jerk more, the prames least."** **Corrected** (inference).
  - For a 0.5 m swell the steepest slope is 1.6° at a 6 s period, 0.9° at 8 s and 0.6° at 10 s. "A degree or two" holds only for a short swell.
  - Craft driven well below their own roll period follow the swell's period, slowly, and tilt by about the wave's slope.
  - But with the note's own prame period (5–6 s), an 8 s swell is amplified 1.6–2.5 times before any damping. The longer periods my estimate allows bring the chaloupes and prames nearer resonance still, though their flat floors damp the roll strongly.
  - So "prames least" is not secure for roll. It does hold for heave and pitch, because a 36 m hull spans more of a wave 56–156 m long.
  - Masthead sweep at 1° of roll: 0.35 m on a 20 m brig mast, 0.5 m on a 30 m prame mast. At 2° a prame's masthead sweeps about 1 m.
  - With their shorter natural period, the péniches add small quick jerks on top of the slow motion.
  - Inference. Low.
- **The period sources speak only of the bad side.** Confirmed.
  - Saint-Haouen (Desbrière IV p. 399, [n56](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n56/mode/1up)) says the flotilla's craft are so built that they cannot "se défendre de la mer". He says it of being caught at anchor by a gale from west to north-east.
  - The Naval Chronicle's correspondent, writing on 31 July 1804 (XIV p. 481, [p. 481](https://archive.org/details/navalchronicleco14londiala/page/481/mode/1up)), says the craft suffer more from the sea, when the wind shifts suddenly and blows onto them, than from the British ships. The note's "when the wind came onshore" is a fair paraphrase.
  - Period. High.

- Depth under the painted line (item 10): Cadier's slope is 3.40 / 752 = 0.45 %. The bottom d m outside the 752 m line stands at 0.30 − 0.00452·d m on Desbrière's scale. At 18:33 (water +1.45 m) the depth is 1.15 + 0.00452·d: 1.26 m at 24 m and 1.53 m at 84 m. A chaloupe (1.8 m) floats from d = 144 m, a prame (2.8 m) from d = 365 m. At the lowest springs (+0.30 m) a chaloupe needs d = 398 m, a prame 619 m. The eye, 1.1 km off, is about 350 m outside the line, over a bottom at about −1.3 m, as `evening.md` has it.

**Spacing and length of the line** (2.10, item 5)

- About 150 craft in one row at 45–55 m make 6.7–8.2 km; in two rows of 75 at 80–110 m, 5.9–8.1 km.
- The July 1805 line, 54 craft: one row at 45–55 m is 2.4–2.9 km; two rows of 27 at 80–110 m are 2.1–2.9 km. Centred off the harbour, it reaches about 1.0–1.5 km to each side of the mouth.

**Motion** (2.12)

- Roll period T = 2π·0.38B / √(g·GM) = 0.762 B / √GM. With the note's GM of 1–1.5 m: péniche 2.0–2.5 s (the note says about 3), bateau 2.8–3.5 s, chaloupe 3.4–4.2 s, prame 5.1–6.2 s.
- Box-hull BM = B² / 12T: péniche 0.77 m, bateau 1.51 m, chaloupe 1.42 m, prame 1.99 m. With an assumed centre of gravity, GM is about 0.15–0.4, 0.6–0.9, 0.3–0.7 and 0.4–0.8 m, and the periods about 3.9–6.4, 3.7–4.5, 5.0–7.7 and 6.9–9.8 s.
- Swell: λ = 1.561 T² gives 56 m at 6 s, 100 m at 8 s and 156 m at 10 s. The steepest slope of a 0.5 m swell, πH/λ, is 1.60°, 0.90° and 0.58°.
- Undamped amplification 1 / |1 − (Tn/Tw)²| for a prame in an 8 s swell: 1.6 at Tn = 5 s, 2.5 at Tn = 6.2 s.
- Masthead sweep θ·H: 1° × 20 m = 0.35 m; 1° × 30 m = 0.52 m; 2° × 30 m = 1.05 m.
- Heading: a craft heading 20° against a camera axis of 98° is 78° off the axis, nearly broadside.

**Time** (2.11)

- The painting's light, 18:33 LAT, against sunset at 19:26 LAT (`evening.md`): 53 minutes.

## Additional facts found

- James III p. 496, appendix No. 27, from Dumas XII p. 304: Boulogne in July 1805 had 13 prames, 11 bombardes, packet boats and avisos, 530 French gun-vessels, and 22 caïques, "corvettes de pêche" and péniches. James notes that the total does not quite agree with the items (2.1).
- Willaumez p. 476: the prame is built with flat floors, carries a battery of heavy guns and two mortars on a platform in a hatchway before the mainmast, and is a mobile fortress for coast defence (2.2).
- A dealer's description of Baugean's plate of 1814, "Prame venant de mouiller", mentions about ten visible guns. The plate was not seen. Low (2.2).
- The prame Ville-de-Mayence sailed with Ver Huell's Batavian division on 17 July 1805 (James III p. 318), so it was not at Boulogne that month (2.2).
- The prames were still in service at Boulogne in 1811: a British print shows seven prames, each with twelve 24-pounders, and fifteen brigs moored under the batteries in September 1811 (Royal Collection RCIN 735168; 2.13).
- The plan of 23 fructidor an XI (10 September 1803) arms every chaloupe canonnière with three 24-pounders and two 36-pounder carronades (Desbrière III p. 98). Napoleon's pencil notes on it give a chaloupe 56 men for the oars, 36 for the guns and 4 officers (p. 99), and a bateau canonnier 28 oars and 56 men (p. 101) (2.3, 2.4).
- Desbrière III p. 102: a caïque carried 20 men, and a division had 27 caïques (2.6).
- Desbrière III p. 141: anchors in the roads "se perdent souvent". The flood sets north-north-east at up to 4 knots. South-west and west winds blew two days in five and generally made the roads untenable; north and north-west winds, more violent still, came about as often. The Baas bank does not stop a heavy swell. A moored line therefore implies settled weather (2.10, 2.12).
- Desbrière III p. 153: the roads' wings were to be held by forts on the rocks of l'Heurt (left) and la Crèche (right), 2,400 toises apart, with a fort between them for the centre (2.10).
- Desbrière III pp. 154–155: the arrêté of 14 messidor an XI (3 July 1803) razed the lantern and reflector light at the end of the jetty for a 10-gun battery and ordered a much taller wooden lantern, set back from it. Whether it was lit in 1805 was not found (2.11).
- Desbrière III pp. 155, 158: the wooden fort (fort en charpente, fort Rouge, fort de l'Expédition) was built on the low-water line at the end of the fascine dike and uncovered at low water (2.10).
- Desbrière III p. 161: on 4 September 1803 Napoleon found Bruix's trace, in 22 pieds at low water, too exposed. He wanted a first position as close inshore as possible under fort Rouge and the batteries, and about fifty caïques in the intervals of the line (2.6, 2.10).
- Desbrière III p. 163: Sganzin, 6 December 1802, says that large canonnières moored on two anchors "en barbe de chat" swing at every tide; where there is very little current, boats can be moored head and stern (2.12).
- Desbrière III p. 398: in December 1803 the transports were to be numbered in series by kind, so a transport's number told its kind (2.8).
- Desbrière IV p. 397: on 19 and 20 July 1805 the Batavians lay off Ambleteuse and the line off Boulogne, in sight of the enemy (2.6).
- Desbrière IV pp. 399–400: Saint-Haouen's report of 3 August 1805. The canonnières in the roads unmoor ("ayant désaffourché") before sailing. Each craft has four cables, spliced two by two to port and starboard, and is to carry at least five guns. Once the line holds only prames and canonnières, they are to have two cables out on one anchor, so that they seem to lie at the usual berth but can move a cable further out at will. Right after the report Desbrière judges that its contradictions and material errors are too plain to need comment (2.10, 2.12).
- Desbrière IV p. 458: in the embarkation plan of 1805 the three bombardes and the caïques were to carry the men of the left corps who found no room in its two escadrilles (2.6).
- James III p. 232: on 1 October 1804 Keith anchored about five miles from the French line, and the Monarch re-anchored just out of gunshot of the batteries and flotilla (2.10).
- Naval Chronicle XIV p. 339: Lacrosse's line of late September 1805 consisted of "27 Gun-boats alone", with 23 British vessels in front of it. A gun-boat thrown up by an explosion had no other damage than "her windows broken", so at least some gun-boats had glazed windows, probably in the stern. The gun-boats kept their posts "without slipping or cutting" their cables (2.8, 2.10, 2.11).
- Corr. XI No. 9164, art. 3: the armed division kept at Boulogne after 30 August was to go out to practise taking "leur ligne d'embossage et leur ligne de combat" (2.10).
- Archives parlementaires LXXXV p. 78: Jeanbon Saint-André's report asked for a flag that shows its colours in the same proportion whichever way it hangs; the decree was adopted without change (2.8).
- Willaumez p. 291: signal pennants were of bunting, six to a set, 25 pieds long and 5 wide; national pennants carried a small stick of about 18 pouces in the sleeve; in 1825 privateers and merchantmen could wear a white or red pennant. Page 444: an admiral's square command flag was at most 10 by 8 pieds (2.8).
- Willaumez p. 194, Creux: the depth is measured from the top of the keel to the top of the beam, so depth less draught gives a lower bound for freeboard (2.9).
- Willaumez, Fanal: signal lanterns hoisted in combinations at mastheads or yardarms, battle lanterns, and stern lanterns, which he calls a thing of the past (2.11).
- Baugean's print carries the official medal "En l'an XII 2000 barques sont construites" (Denon, 1804), the same figure as Napoleon's flourish of 26 July 1805 (2.13).
- In Lewis's view all the French craft head to the right, with ensigns and pennants streaming to the left; for a view looking south-east that is bows roughly south-west. The state of the tide when he drew it is unknown. Low (2.12).
- The Foudroyante model (Commons photo): at 35.7 m scale the rail stands about 3 m above the copper line, and the main topgallant reaches at least a hull length above the waterline. Type reference only. Low (2.9).
