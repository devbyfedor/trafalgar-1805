# Track H2: Boulogne, the turn east, Ulm, and Cádiz up to the sortie

Research notes for scene 2 ("The road to Trafalgar"). These cover the invasion camps and flotilla (1803–05), Napoleon's turn east in August 1805, Ulm, and the watch on Cádiz from Nelson's arrival to the night of 20–21 October. The dawn of 21 October is already covered in `docs/history-notes.md` and `docs/research/dawn-setting.md`. Only the link to it is repeated here.

Claim ids (H2-xx) match the structured summary returned with these notes.

## Conventions

- **Dates** are civil dates unless marked. Royal Navy logs of 1805 still used the nautical day, which ran from noon to noon and carried the civil date on which it ended. An entry such as "October 3rd, P.M. at 6" is therefore the evening of civil 2 October. Nicolas's printed extracts are not consistent about this (see H2-42).
- **French Republican dates** are converted with the Correspondance's own equivalences: 1 vendémiaire an XIV = 23 September 1805, and 28 vendémiaire = 20 October.
- **Bearings** in logs and letters are compass (magnetic) bearings. Variation off Cádiz was about 20–21° W (Montañés log, 20° NW; NOAA gufm1 model, 21.1° W), so true = magnetic − 21°. Where it matters, positions are given both ways. The Euryalus noon latitude on 19 October fits only the magnetic reading (H2-41).
- **Units.** 1 league = 3 nautical miles. French *pied* = 0.325 m.
- **Confidence.** *High*: several independent primary or period sources agree. *Medium*: one good source, or a consistent inference. *Low*: an estimate, or the sources conflict.
- **Quotations.** Short quotations come only from public-domain period sources (Napoleon's *Correspondance*, Nelson's letters and diary, the 1805 logs, Beatty, James, Corbett, Desbrière's documents) and only where the exact words are the evidence. Everything else is paraphrased.

## 1. The invasion camps and the flotilla, 1803–1805

### H2-01 The camps (High)

- **Origin.** On 14 June 1803 (25 prairial an XI) the First Consul ordered six camps, meant to make up a single army: Holland, Ghent, Saint-Omer, Compiègne, Saint-Malo and Bayonne (letter to Berthier, printed in Desbrière, *Projets et tentatives*, III, ch. IX, about p. 184). A ministerial draft of 23 May had already proposed 50,000 men under the name "camp de Saint-Omer" (Desbrière III, p. 179). Desbrière calls the order of 14 June 1803 the origin of the Grande Armée.
- **Organisation by 1805.** In May 1805 the army was formally the **armée des côtes de l'Océan** (Napoleon's note and the war minister's letter to Davout, Milan, 30 May 1805; Desbrière IV, pp. 460–461). It had:
  - an advance guard under Lannes: Oudinot's grenadiers;
  - the *corps de droite*, Davout, still called the camp of Bruges;
  - the *corps du centre*, Soult, the camp of Saint-Omer;
  - the *corps de gauche*, Ney, the camp of Montreuil;
  - detached corps at Utrecht (Marmont) and Brest (Augereau);
  - a reserve of cavalry, the Italian division and dragoons, plus the Guard.
- **Names.** "Armée d'Angleterre" is the earlier (1797–98) name and the popular one; English writers say "Army of England". The 1805 orders use "armée des côtes de l'Océan" and the individual camp names.

### H2-02 The ports and camps of the invasion army (High)

- **Seven assembly ports.** James (III, p. 325) lists Étaples, Boulogne, Wimereux, Ambleteuse, Calais, Dunkirk and Ostend. Wimereux and Ambleteuse were new ports dug in 1803–04 (Napoleon, 2 July 1804).
- **Where the troops were, with their flotilla ports** (James III, pp. 325–326, from Mathieu Dumas, *Précis*, xii):

  | Port | Flotilla division | Troops | Commander |
  | --- | --- | --- | --- |
  | Étaples | 1st grand division, "left wing" | Camp of Montreuil (Ney) | Rear-Adm. Courand |
  | Boulogne | 2nd and 3rd divisions | Soult's two camps, right and left of the town | Rear-Adm. Savary; Capt. Le Ray |
  | Wimereux | 4th division, "right wing" | Lannes's grenadiers | Capt. Daugier |
  | Ambleteuse | 5th division, the Gallo-Batavian flotilla | Davout's corps | Vice-Adm. Ver Huell |
  | Calais | 6th division, reserve | Italian infantry and dragoons | Capt. L'Évêque |

- **Boulogne itself.** Soult's corps was split into a right camp on the cliffs north of the Liane (the Tour d'Odre plateau and the Terlincthun valley) and a left camp on the Outreau plateau. Napoleon's pavilion stood on the cliff top. His headquarters was the château of Pont-de-Briques (Wikipedia, *Camp of Boulogne*, paraphrased; consistent with "camp de droite" and "camp de gauche" in Napoleon's order of 26 August 1805, *Correspondance* XI, No. 9137).
- **Étaples.** Inrap excavated 19 huts of the camp of Montreuil at Étaples, with buttons of the 6e léger (Ney's corps). The camp was occupied from about October 1803 to August 1805 (napoleon.org newsletter 410 and an Inrap communiqué, via search summaries; the Inrap page itself returned 403).

### H2-03 Army size (Medium: figures differ by date and by what they count)

- **Napoleon, 2 July 1804** (to Latouche-Tréville, *Correspondance* IX, No. 7832, p. 406): the craft at Étaples, Boulogne, Wimereux and Ambleteuse could carry "120,000 hommes et 10,000 chevaux".
- **War minister's embarkation return, spring 1805** (Germinal an XIII; Archives nationales AF IV 1204, printed in Desbrière IV, pp. 432–433):

  | Group | Troops | Vessels |
  | --- | --- | --- |
  | Camp of Montreuil (Ney) | 27,050 | 342 |
  | Camp of Saint-Omer (Soult, at Boulogne) | 45,907 | 684 |
  | Camp of Bruges (Davout) | 33,012 | 432 |
  | Reserve | 41,693 | 596 |
  | **Total** | **147,662** | **2,054** |

  - The total includes 142,601 combatants. It needed space for 15,764 horses.
  - After deducting 16,132 naval crewmen, the vessels could carry 127,500 men and 9,975 horses. That left 20,162 men and 5,789 horses without a place.
- **July 1805 table** (James III, Appendix 27, from Dumas): the flotilla was to carry 163,645 men, 16,783 of them sailors, and 9,059 horses.
- **Napoleon, 23 and 25 August 1805**: he would be in Germany with 200,000 men. That figure includes Marmont from Holland, Bernadotte from Hanover and the Bavarians, not only the Boulogne army.
- **Other figures.** Wikipedia (*Camp of Boulogne*) gives "about 60,000 soldiers" in the two Boulogne camps in 1805, unsourced. A napoleon.org newsletter gives "more than 175,000" for the coastal camps as a whole. Neither was checked.

**Best-supported figure for the scene:** about 150,000 men embarkable at Étaples, Boulogne, Wimereux and Ambleteuse in 1805. Soult's corps at Boulogne itself was about 46,000. Wikipedia's "about 60,000" for Boulogne may include the grenadiers at Wimereux and the Guard; not checked.

### H2-06 What the crossing needed: Napoleon's own words (High)

Command of the Strait for a short time was the condition, and he put a figure on it several times:

- **2 July 1804**, to Latouche-Tréville (*Corr.* IX, No. 7832, p. 406): "Que nous soyons maîtres du détroit six heures, et nous serons maîtres du monde!" This is the source of the familiar English line about being masters of the Channel for six hours. Note "détroit" (the Strait), not "Manche".
- **20 July 1805**, to Ganteaume (*Corr.* XI, No. 8998, p. 26): Ganteaume was to come before Boulogne, where, "maître trois jours de la mer", he would let the Emperor decide England's fate.
- **26 July 1805**, to Villeneuve (*Corr.* XI, No. 9022, pp. 42–43): "si vous me rendez maître pendant le seul espace de trois jours du Pas-de-Calais", he would end England's existence.
- **13 August 1805**, to Villeneuve (*Corr.* XI, No. 9073, p. 87): three days, and if only twenty-four hours, would be enough.
- **22 August 1805**, to Villeneuve (*Corr.* XI, No. 9115, p. 115): "Paraissez vingt-quatre heures, et tout est terminé."

So the figure moves from six hours of the Strait in 1804 to three days of the sea in July 1805, then back down to twenty-four hours in August 1805, as his hopes of the fleet faded.

## 2. Napoleon at Boulogne in August 1805, and the march east

### H2-07 Arrival (High)

- Napoleon's letters are dated from Saint-Cloud up to 2 August and "Camp de Boulogne" from **3 August 1805** (15 thermidor an XIII) (*Corr.* XI).
- James and Corbett both give 3 August (James III, p. 326; Corbett p. 240). James adds that Napoleon had the army embarked twice in his presence, to see how fast it could be done.

### H2-08 The decision, 22–26 August (High)

- **22 August.** News that Villeneuve had left Ferrol reached Boulogne; it had reached Paris on the 21st (Desbrière, *Trafalgar*, p. 110). Napoleon wrote to Ganteaume and Villeneuve to come into the Channel (Nos. 9114, 9115).
- **23 August**, to Talleyrand (No. 9117, p. 117):
  - If the fleet arrived there was "encore temps: je suis le maître de l'Angleterre".
  - Otherwise: "je cours au plus pressé: je lève mes camps", replacing the field battalions with third battalions. By 1 vendémiaire he would have 200,000 men in Germany: "Je marche sur Vienne".
  - The same day he told Marmont (through Berthier, No. 9120) that, if the fleet were held at Cádiz, he would put off the expedition "à une autre année". He ordered Bernadotte's corps to concentrate at Göttingen (No. 9119).
- **24 August.** First movement orders: cuirassiers to Landau, dragoon divisions toward Strasbourg (No. 9128). Desbrière calls this the definitive order to break up the camps (p. 113).
- **25 August**, to Talleyrand (No. 9130, pp. 133–134):
  - Austria's preparations had suspended his embarkation.
  - "Mon parti est pris. Mon mouvement est commencé."
  - He admitted he would not have believed the Austrians so determined, "mais je me suis tant trompé en ma vie, que je n'en rougis pas".
  - Corbett, p. 276, quotes this as the moment Napoleon told Talleyrand all was over with the invasion.
- **26 August.** The Grande Armée was named, in a note that was made public by the order of the day of 29 August (Alombert and Colin, *Campagne de 1805 en Allemagne*, I, introduction, p. 218). The general order of march from the coast to the Rhine went to Berthier (No. 9137, pp. 141–144).
- **Napoleon did not yet know** that Villeneuve had gone to Cádiz. The news arrived about 1–2 September, after the decision (Desbrière pp. 114–116; Corbett p. 278).

### H2-09 Leaving the coast (High)

- **Order of 26 August** (No. 9137):
  - The first divisions of Davout (left-hand road), Soult (middle) and Ney (right-hand) leave on 10 fructidor, **28 August**.
  - Second divisions on 30 August, third on 31 August.
  - Twenty-four days' march to the Rhine, arriving by 1 vendémiaire (23 September).
- **Confirmation.** Berthier wrote, "Les premières divisions des trois corps d'armée partent demain matin, 10" (Alombert and Colin I, p. 373). On 28 August Napoleon told Dejean, "Les premières divisions sont parties".
- **Advance parties.** Artillery convoys left on 29 August (Alombert and Colin I, p. 235). Dragoons and grenadiers were warned on 25–26 August to march next day (Maude pp. 121–122).
- **What stayed behind.**
  - Nineteen battalions guarded the camps: nine whole battalions and ten third battalions, split between Ambleteuse, Boulogne and Étaples.
  - The flotilla was concentrated at Boulogne, with the Étaples craft to go up toward Montreuil if possible.
  - The seamen were armed as infantry (No. 9137).
  - On 13 September Napoleon planned to keep about 500 vessels and 80,000 men at Boulogne as a standing threat (No. 9209, as summarised by Desbrière, p. 120).

### H2-10 Napoleon leaves Boulogne (High)

- **2 September 1805**, to Decrès (No. 9177): "je pars dans une heure pour Paris". To Eugène, the same day: "Je serai demain à Paris".
- He was at Montreuil that evening (Desbrière p. 116) and wrote from La Malmaison on 4 September (No. 9179).

### H2-11 The line of march (Medium-high)

1. **Coast to the Rhine** (late August to about 25 September).
   - Three roads.
   - Davout's first stage ran by Ardres, Watten and Cassel (Berthier's correction, Alombert and Colin I, p. 219).
   - The **centre road (Soult's corps from the Boulogne camps) passed through Metz**. Artillery convoys on the centre road had to collect matériel there (Alombert and Colin I, p. 236).
   - From Metz, Soult's divisions marched on 18–19 September by Courcelles, Saint-Avold, Sarreguemines, Bitche, Lembach and Wissembourg to Germersheim (itinerary in Alombert and Colin I, p. 414).
   - Davout's divisions went by Saarbrücken, Zweibrücken, Kaiserslautern and Dürkheim to Frankenthal, Oggersheim and Speyer.
   - Changed orders of 30 August put Davout facing the Rhine near Mannheim, Soult in a square Germersheim–Lauterbourg–Bergzabern–Wissembourg, and Ney between Haguenau and Seltz (Alombert and Colin I, pp. 220–221).
   - The stages between Boulogne and Metz were not traced for the centre corps. The dragoon routes ran by Arras, Bapaume, Péronne, Saint-Quentin and Laon.
2. **Rhine crossing, 25–26 September** (3–4 vendémiaire an XIV):
   - Murat's cavalry and Lannes at Kehl (Strasbourg);
   - Davout opposite Mannheim;
   - Soult at Speyer;
   - Ney on the 26th (Alombert and Colin II, pp. 30–31 and the movement orders; Maude p. 149).
   - Napoleon left Paris on 23 September (Desbrière, p. 123) or 24 September (the usual modern date). He was at Strasbourg from 26 September to 1 October and at Ettlingen on 2 October (*Corr.* XI).
3. **Rhine to the Danube.**
   - Ney by Karlsruhe, Pforzheim, Ludwigsburg and Aalen.
   - Soult from Speyer by Heilbronn and Nördlingen to Donauwörth.
   - Davout from Mannheim by Neckarelz and Öttingen to Neuburg.
   - Marmont and Bernadotte by Würzburg (Maude pp. 149–150).
   - Vandamme (Soult's corps) seized Donauwörth on the evening of 6 October. The French crossed the **Danube on 7 October**, and Napoleon was at Donauwörth that day (Maude pp. 199–200).
4. **To Ulm.** The army closed on Ulm from the east along both banks: Günzburg 9 October, Haslach 11 October, Elchingen 14 October. Napoleon's headquarters was the abbey of Elchingen from 15 to 21 October.

**For the dashed line (Stage B):** Boulogne → Metz → Bitche → Speyer (Rhine) → Heilbronn → Nördlingen → Donauwörth (Danube) → Elchingen → Ulm. This is Soult's corps, the one from the Boulogne camps. Maude: 400 miles from the coast to the Rhine.

## 3. Ulm

### H2-12 What happened, 17–20 October (High)

Main sources: Alombert and Colin, III/1, introduction, pp. 216–229, which quote an Austrian staff officer's *Relation de la prise d'Ulm* and the Vienna inquiry; Maude, pp. 243–251; *Correspondance* XI.

- **15–16 October.** Ney summoned Ulm on the 15th; Mack refused. On the 16th Napoleon offered terms: prisoners of war unless relieved, officers free on parole.
- **17 October, noon.** The **capitulation was signed**: the garrison became prisoners of war unless relieved within eight days, to midnight of 25 October (Maude) or "jusqu'au 25 inclus" (Alombert and Colin). That day Napoleon wrote that about 20,000 men in Ulm were capitulating (No. 9386, to Murat).
- **18 October, 9–10 a.m.** A brigade of Ney's corps under Labassée took over a gate. Alombert and Colin call it the Stuttgart gate, at 10 a.m.; the Austrian account says the "porte Neuve" was handed over at 9. That these are the same gate is not checked. French soldiers flooded into the town.
- **19 October, about midday to 2 p.m.** Mack rode to Ober-Elchingen, saw Napoleon, and signed an additional convention with Berthier. The garrison would march out at once, on the **20th**; in return Ney's corps would stay at Ulm until the 25th.
- **20 October.**
  - From 11 a.m. Marmont's corps crossed the town to complete the ring of troops.
  - From **2 p.m.** the garrison marched out and laid down its arms (Alombert and Colin, p. 228). The Austrian eyewitness says they left by the Frauentor, crossed the length of a French square and grounded their arms near the "porte Neuve". Napoleon watched from a rise at the foot of the Michelsberg ("mont Saint-Michel"), with part of the Guard, the 2nd and 6th corps and the dragoons present.
  - The 8th Bulletin (No. 9404, p. 342) says Napoleon was "sur la hauteur d'Ulm" from 2 p.m. until 7 p.m. while the army filed past.
  - The Austrian officers left on the 21st and later. Napoleon's proclamation of 21 October (No. 9405) begins "Soldats de la Grande Armée, en quinze jours nous avons fait une campagne."

**For the scene:** on **20 October 1805**, the day before Trafalgar, Mack's army marched out of Ulm and laid down its arms before Napoleon. The capitulation itself is dated 17 October.

## 4. Nelson joins the fleet off Cádiz; British strength

### H2-14 Departure (High)

- Nelson left Merton at 10:30 p.m. on Friday 13 September and reached Portsmouth at about 6 a.m. on the 14th. He embarked from the beach "at the Bathing Machines" at 2 p.m. and joined Victory at St Helens (his diary, Nicolas VII, p. 35).
- Victory's log: flag hoisted on 14 September; "Sunday, 15th; 8, A.M., weighed and made sail to the S.S.E. Euryalus in company" (Nicolas VII, p. 36).
- Ajax and Thunderer joined off Plymouth on 17–18 September (James III, p. 379; Desbrière p. 146).
- Nelson sent Euryalus ahead on 26 September to warn Collingwood: no salute, no colours (James; Beatty p. 3).

### H2-15 Joining (High)

- **Nelson's diary, 28 September** (Beatty, 3rd ed., pp. 95–96, headed "Saturday, Sept. 28th"; Nicolas VII, p. 53, prints "Sunday"):
  - At noon he saw eighteen sail.
  - "In the evening joined the Fleet under Vice-Admiral Collingwood."
  - He saw 35 or 36 enemy ships of the line in Cádiz.
- **Victory's log**, as printed by Nicolas (VII, p. 53 n.): at 6 p.m. on 28 September, under studding sails, she joined Collingwood's fleet. At 8 p.m. San Sebastián's lighthouse bore E by N, 5 or 6 leagues.
- **Nelson to Lady Hamilton, 1 October** (Nicolas VII, p. 60): he joined "late on the evening of the 28th of September, but could not communicate with them until the next morning."
- **Captain Duff to his wife, 1 October** (Nicolas VII, p. 70 n.): "On Saturday night we were joined by Lord Nelson".
- **Nelson to the Admiralty, 2 October** (Nicolas VII, pp. 62–63): he arrived on the evening of the 28th and took over command on the morning after.
- **Historians.** Corbett (p. 317): evening of the 28th, command on the morrow, his birthday. Mahan: 6 p.m. on Saturday 28 September. Desbrière (p. 147): 6 p.m., 28 September.
- **Weekdays check.** 28 September 1805 was a Saturday and 29 September a Sunday; 21 October was a Monday.
- **Disagreement.** Some secondary accounts give 27 September (a blog) or "arrived on the 29th, his birthday". The primary record is 28 September in the evening, with command on the 29th.

Earlier strength (High): Collingwood had 26 of the line once Calder joined with 18 on 30 August (Desbrière p. 145; James III, p. 379).

**Reconstruction** (inference, Medium): 29 under Nelson by 29 September, including the three he brought. Taking Louis's five on 2 October, and Leviathan away at Gibraltar until the 8th, leaves 23, which is Nelson's own figure of 5 October ("I have twenty-three Sail with me", Nicolas VII, p. 75). The arrivals and departures below then lead to 29 at the peak and 27 at Trafalgar.

### H2-17 Louis's detachment (High for the ships, Medium for the dates)

- **Ordered on 2 October** to Gibraltar and Tetuan for water and stores (Nelson to Marsden, 2 October, Nicolas VII, pp. 62–63).
- **When he left.** Nelson to Ford, 2 October: Louis "will leave the Fleet this day" (p. 66). Victory's log: "October 3rd. P.M. at 6, Queen, Canopus, Spencer, Zealous, Tigre, and Endymion, parted" (p. 63 n.). That is the evening of civil 2 October if the log was kept in nautical time.
- **Ships.** Queen 98, Canopus 80, Spencer 74, Tigre 80, Zealous 74, and the frigate Endymion. Zealous and Endymion were going in for repairs. Nelson called it "six Sail of the Line"; James and Beatty name the five ships of the line.
- **Turned back.** On 3 October (James) or 4 October (Beatty), news from Blackwood that the troops were embarking made Louis turn back. Nelson took it for a ruse and sent him on.
- **Tetuan.** He completed his water there by 15 October. Westerly winds forced him back to Tetuan, where on 17 October he received the order to escort the Malta convoy past Cartagena (Corbett pp. 331 and 335).
- **Absent from the battle.** On 20 October he was about 200 miles east (Corbett).

### H2-18 Arrivals and departures, 7–17 October (High for the ships, Medium for some dates)

| Ship | Event | Date and source |

| Leviathan 74 | rejoined from Gibraltar | 8 Oct (James) |

| Donegal 74 | sent into Gibraltar for a ground tier of casks | 17 Oct (James, Clowes); 18 Oct (Beatty) |

### H2-19 Peak and battle strength (High)

- **Peak.** 29 of the line off Cádiz, with five more away with Louis (James III, p. 383; Clowes V, p. 128).
- **Under Nelson's command overall.** "thirty-three Sail of the Line; but six having been sent to Gibraltar …" (Nicolas VII, p. 130).
- **At Trafalgar.** 27 of the line: 3 of 100 guns, 4 of 98, 1 of 80, 16 of 74 and 3 of 64. With them were the frigates Euryalus, Naiad, Phoebe and Sirius, the schooner Pickle and the cutter Entreprenante. Already in the scene 1 notes.

## 5. Out of sight: the station, the inshore squadron and the chain

### H2-20 The main fleet's station (High)

Nelson's own figures, in leagues west of Cádiz:

| Figure | Source |
| --- | --- |
| "from sixteen to eighteen leagues West of Cadiz" | to Blackwood, 4 October (Nicolas VII, p. 73) |
| "The position I have taken for this month is from sixteen to eighteen leagues West of Cadiz" | to Barham, 5 October (p. 75) |
| "the body of the Fleet from fifteen to eighteen leagues West of Cadiz" | to Ball, 11 October (p. 111) |
| "whose station is from fifteen to twenty West of Cadiz" | diary, 14 October (p. 122) |

- **Letter headings**, giving Cádiz's bearing and distance from Victory: East 17 leagues (4 Oct); 16 leagues west of Cádiz (6 Oct); due East 19 leagues (9 Oct); N 71 E 15 leagues and East 13 leagues (10 Oct); E.S.E. 16 leagues at noon on 19 Oct (Nicolas VII, pp. 73, 80, 96, 110, 132).
- **In miles.** About 50 (Mahan; Nicolas's narrative: "about fifty miles W.S.W."). "Fifty or sixty miles westward of Cadiz", in "the vicinity of Cape St. Mary's" (Beatty, p. 4). The rendezvous was "between Cape St. Mary and Cadiz" (Corbett p. 317).
- **Rules.**
  - In fresh easterly breezes, work up toward Cádiz, "never getting to the Northward of it".
  - If the enemy came out, crowd sail south toward Cape Spartel and Larache (to Blackwood, 4 October).
- **Reasons.**
  - Not to be caught near Cádiz in a westerly gale and driven into the Strait with so many three-deckers, leaving Cádiz open "as they served Lord Keith in the late War" (to Barham, 5 October).
  - To hide his strength and give the enemy an apparent chance of escape (Corbett pp. 326–327; Beatty).
- **Collingwood's arrangement before Nelson came.** The fleet in sight 15–20 miles off, with an inshore squadron under Louis (Mahan; James). On 3 September Lauriston reported that from the San Sebastián tower 5 British ships of the line and 2 frigates could be seen at 3 leagues, and 17 more at 6 leagues (Desbrière pp. 125 and 145).

### H2-21 Blackwood's inshore squadron (High for the composition, Medium for the stations)

- **29 September.** Nelson "Sent Euryalus to watch the Enemy with the Hydra off Cadiz" (diary, Nicolas VII, p. 53). Beatty: Euryalus and Hydra were to keep off the entrance to Cádiz.
- **Early October.** On 4 October Nelson had only three frigates with the fleet and expected Phoebe, Sirius, Naiad and Niger from Gibraltar. He sent Naiad to Blackwood on 8 October, with Phoebe and Weazle to follow (pp. 74 and 87).
- **9–10 October.**
  - Blackwood's captains named on 9 October: Capel (Phoebe), Parker (Amazon), Mundy (Hydra), Prowse (Sirius) (p. 96 n.).
  - On 10 October: "Keep your five Frigates, Weazle and Pickle" (p. 110).
  - Nelson to Ball, 11 October: "five Frigates, a Brig, and a Schooner watching them closely" (p. 111).
- **19 October** (Euryalus's log; Desbrière p. 161 n.): Euryalus (Blackwood), Sirius (Prowse), Phoebe (Capel) and Naiad (Dundas), the sloop or brig Weazle and the schooner Pickle. On the 19th Weazle was sent with the news to Gibraltar and Tetuan, and Pickle to Cape Spartel.
- **Stations by day.** Close off the harbour mouth, usually 2–8 nm west or south-west of San Sebastián. Positions from Euryalus's own bearings are in section 9.
  - On 8 October she stood in until San Sebastián bore S½W, 2¼ miles (James III, p. 380).
  - At dawn on 19 October she was about 4 nm south-west of it.
  - At noon on 19 October, 7–8 nm west-south-west, with an observed latitude of 36°28′ N.
  - Blackwood wrote on the 19th that he was within four miles of the enemy.
- **At night.**
  - Nelson suggested anchoring a frigate between Cádiz and the San Pedro shoals in easterly winds, from two hours after dark to two hours before day (5 October, p. 76).
  - Pickle was "useful in the night close in shore" (9 October, p. 96).
  - Nelson's informants expected an escape at night, "placing Frigates on the Porpoises and Diamond, and the Shoal off Cadiz" (p. 96). These are the Las Puercas and El Diamante shoals.
- **Linking.** "if you throw a Frigate West from you, most probably, in fine weather, we shall communicate daily" (4 October, p. 73).

### H2-22 The chain of ships of the line (High)

- **4 October, to Duff (Mars)** (Nicolas VII, p. 70): Mars, Defence and Colossus to keep "from three to four leagues between the Fleet and Cadiz" and to use distant signals.
- **4 October, to Blackwood** (p. 73): Mars, Colossus and Defence "four leagues East from the Fleet, and one of them advanced to the East towards Cadiz, and as near as possible in the latitude."
- **Corbett, p. 357.** Nelson gave Duff four ships: Mars, Colossus, Defence and Ajax. After the memorandum of 9–10 October, Belleisle and Agamemnon were added to the connecting line, and Nelson called the group his Advanced Squadron. Corbett's p. 327 slips and calls Duff "of the Defence"; Duff commanded Mars.
- **14 October, diary** (p. 122): "Placed Defence and Agamemnon from seven to ten leagues West of Cadiz …"
- **15 October, to Hope (Defence)** (p. 122): take station with Agamemnon 7–10 leagues west of Cádiz, with two or three ships between the fleet and them. Their task was to support the frigates if the enemy tried to drive them off.
- **19 October.** "Colossus, Mars, Defence, and Agamemnon formed the cordon of communication with the frigates in shore: the Fleet was lying to" (Beatty, p. 7). James: Mars, with Defence and Agamemnon, formed the cordon. Euryalus at 8 a.m.: "The Defence in sight from the mast head west."
- **19 October, evening.** The Advanced Squadron was enlarged for the chase. Nelson's diary orders Mars, Orion, Belleisle, Leviathan, Bellerophon and Polyphemus "to go ahead during the night, and to carry a light, standing for the Straits' Mouth" (p. 133). Others kept touch with Blackwood (Corbett p. 334).
- **20 October, night memorandum** (Nicolas VII, p. 136): "Captain Blackwood to keep with two Frigates in sight of the Enemy …"
  - So the night chain ran: Euryalus and one frigate in sight of the enemy → two frigates → Defence → Colossus → Mars → Victory.

### H2-23 How the signal ran on 19 October (High)

- **Sirius, nearest inshore.** At 6 a.m.: "Enemy have their topsail yards hoisted". At 7 a.m. No. 370: "The enemy's ships are coming out of port or getting under sail" (Corbett p. 333).
- **Euryalus's log** (NRS *Logs* II, pp. 145–146):
  - At daylight, enemy topgallant yards across; eight ships had topsails hoisted to the mastheads.
  - At 7, the northernmost ships under way.
  - At 7:20 she sent Phoebe "to repeat signals between us and the English fleet".
  - At 8, 19 of the enemy under way; Defence in sight west from the masthead; Phoebe WNW, firing three-minute guns.
  - At 8:10 Naiad was ordered to repeat signals between Euryalus and Phoebe.
- **At the fleet.** At 9:30 Mars repeated the signal to Victory, about 16 leagues off (Nelson's diary, Nicolas VII, p. 133; James; Beatty). Blackwood to his wife: "Within two hours, though our Fleet was at sixteen leagues off, I have let Lord N. know of their coming out" (Nicolas VII, p. 131 n.). He was talking to Nelson "by means of Sir H. Popham's signals ... repeated along by the rest of the Frigates".
- **Nelson's reply.** "General chase, S.E.", made with a single flag, two guns and the SE compass flag, meaning chase in the south-east quarter (Corbett p. 333 n.). Then prepare for battle.
- **Afternoon.** At 3 p.m. Colossus signalled "the Enemy's Fleet is at sea" (diary; James). Beatty: about 2, repeated by Colossus and Mars.
- **Standing orders for news of a sortie.**
  - To Collingwood, 10 October: the vessel bringing word was "to fire a gun every three minutes, and burn a rocket from the mast-head every half-hour" (Nicolas VII, pp. 109–110).
  - To Blackwood the same day: guns every three minutes by the watch, and rockets from the masthead at night (p. 111).

### H2-24 Why it worked: out of sight (Medium)

- **Reports.**
  - Corbett (p. 323): after Nelson took command, the shore signal stations saw nothing of the fleet but an inshore squadron of five cruisers and an occasional glimpse of linking ships of the line.
  - Beurnonville (Madrid, 20 October): Nelson's force off Cádiz "n'est point en vue"; he kept only frigates in observation (Desbrière, annex, p. 70).
- **Geometry** (my computation).
  - Cádiz kept watch from the San Sebastián tower (Lauriston, 3 September) and from "la Tour de Cadix" (Prigny's report, 20 October). The latter is probably the Torre Tavira, the port's official watchtower since 1778, about 45 m above sea level (Spanish Wikipedia); the identification is mine.
  - From 45 m the horizon is about 14 nm. A 74's topgallant masthead, about 55 m up, clears the horizon at about 15 nm. A masthead therefore shows to about 29 nm in clear air, and a hull with 5 m of freeboard to about 18–19 nm.
  - Nelson's main body, at 45–60 nm, was invisible.
  - Defence and Agamemnon, at 21–30 nm, sat at the limit, where their upper sails could be glimpsed. This fits Corbett's "occasional glimpse".

## 6. Napoleon's September orders, Rosily, the council of war, and the decision to sail

### H2-25 The orders to Villeneuve (High)

- **14 September 1805**, "Instructions pour le vice-amiral Villeneuve" (*Corr.* XI, No. 9210, pp. 195–196, Saint-Cloud, 27 fructidor). Villeneuve was to:
  - put to sea at the first favourable opportunity, with at least 2½ months' food;
  - go first to Cartagena to collect the Spanish squadron there;
  - then go to Naples and land the troops embarked in the fleet to join Saint-Cyr;
  - seize any British or Russian warships at Naples, cruise there to intercept a convoy for Malta;
  - then refit at Toulon;
  - and attack the enemy wherever he was found in inferior force.
- **Decrès's covering letters** of 16 September added cruising against convoys (Desbrière p. 121).
- **Received at Cádiz.** "Le 5 de ce mois (27 septembre)", per Decrès's report to Napoleon (Desbrière, annex, pp. 33–34). Desbrière's narrative (p. 137), Clowes (V, p. 128) and James ("the very day on which Lord Nelson arrived") give 28 September. Corbett (p. 322) says the order reached Cádiz as Nelson appeared off Cape St Vincent.
- **Troops.** Embarked on 30 September (Desbrière p. 127 n.; James: the 30th).

### H2-26 Rosily: appointment and journey (Medium-high)

- **15 September**, Napoleon to Decrès (No. 9220, pp. 204–205): send a special courier to Villeneuve; "comme son excessive pusillanimité l'empêchera de l'entreprendre", send Admiral Rosily to replace him. Rosily would carry letters ordering Villeneuve home to account for his conduct.
- **17 September.** Instructions for Rosily (No. 9230, pp. 216–217, Saint-Cloud, 30 fructidor), repeating the Mediterranean mission. Decrès wrote to Rosily on 18 September (Desbrière p. 122).
- **20 September.** Decrès's letter telling Villeneuve he was superseded. It had **not reached him** by 18 October. Neither Decrès nor the ambassador Beurnonville told Villeneuve (Desbrière pp. 122–123 and 128 n.).
- **Journey.**
  - Rosily was to start on 24 September (Fraser p. 37).
  - Bayonne to Madrid took eleven days: "J'ai mis onze jours à me rendre en cette ville" (Rosily, Madrid, 12 October; Desbrière, annex, p. 117).
- **At Madrid.**
  - He arrived in the night of 10–11 October (Beurnonville, 14 October, annex p. 68). Beurnonville's letter of 10 October has him arriving as the post was closing, with a broken carriage spring that kept him a day. Desbrière's narrative (p. 128): 10 October.
  - Corbett (p. 331) gives 12 October, the date of Rosily's own letter.
  - The posting system had broken down, and a band of robbers worked the road south of Córdoba. He waited for escorts and planned to take miquelets on foot from Córdoba.
- **Left Madrid** on the morning of 14 October with Rear-Admiral Gourdon. Cádiz was expected in ten days (Beurnonville, 14 October).
- **Arrival at Cádiz.** Expected on 1 brumaire, 23 October (Beurnonville, 20 and 23 October). Fraser gives 25 October twice (pp. 303 n. and 379). His first letter from Cádiz is dated 3 brumaire, 25 October (Desbrière, annex, p. 118).

### H2-27 When Villeneuve heard (Medium)

- **15 October.** Private letters from Bayonne said Rosily had passed through on a mission to Cádiz. Villeneuve wrote that afternoon that he was pleased (Fraser pp. 62–63; Desbrière p. 141).
- **18 October.** "Par le bruit public", he learned that Rosily had reached Madrid on the 10th to take command. He wrote: "si le vent me permet de sortir, je partirai demain" (Desbrière pp. 141–142; Corbett pp. 331–332).
- **Fraser's version.** The Madrid news came "not many hours later" than the Bayonne letter, which would put it about 15–16 October. Desbrière and Corbett date it, through Villeneuve's own letter, to the 18th.

### H2-28 The council of war, 8 October (High)

- **7 October.** With a north-easterly or easterly wind, the signal to prepare to sail was hoisted and then annulled. Villeneuve said the wind was too strong and would be contrary in the Strait (Desbrière p. 137; Corbett p. 323).
- **8 October.** The council met on board Bucentaure, at Gravina's request.
  - The procès-verbal is signed by Prigny, Galiano, Lavillegris, Maistral, Cosmao, MacDonnell, Cisneros, Magon, Escaño, Dumanoir, Alava, Gravina and Villeneuve (Desbrière pp. 137–138; annex p. 97).
  - It concluded that the fleet should wait for a favourable occasion: bad weather driving the enemy off, or the enemy dividing to protect its Mediterranean trade and convoys.
  - All declared themselves ready to fight whenever the Emperor wished.
- **The debate.** Escaño's account (in Corbett pp. 323–325) has a violent exchange between Magon and the Spanish brigadier Galiano, with Prigny arguing that the allied ships were untrained. The vote was to stay at anchor.

### H2-29 The decision to sail, 18 October (High)

- **The wind.** From the 10th to the 17th hard westerly gales blew. At midnight on the 17th the wind went east (James III, p. 385).
- **Morning of the 18th.** Villeneuve ordered Magon to slip out at night and drive off Blackwood's frigates: with three ships (Desbrière p. 141) or "seven of the line and a frigate" (Corbett p. 332).
- **News by coast signal** (Desbrière p. 141):
  - a British convoy had left Gibraltar with 4 ships of the line;
  - another ship of the line lay in Gibraltar without her mainmast;
  - a third was going there.
  - So Nelson seemed six short.
- **Decision.** With the Rosily rumour on top of this, he decided to sail next day and told Gravina.
- **Evening.** At 5:15 p.m. the signal to hoist in boats and unmoor; by 10:30 p.m. the cables were hove short. He did not try to leave in the dark (Desbrière p. 160). A line of gunboats drew up across the harbour entrance (James).

## 7. The sortie and the two nights, 19–21 October

### H2-30 19 October (High for the date, Medium for the count)

- **Signal to weigh.** Hoisted between 5 and 6 a.m. (French reports), with a light N by E breeze. James and Nicolas give 7 a.m.
- **Who got out.** The wind died, and only the advanced ships got out.
  - Desbrière (p. 161): at about 10 a.m., the wind having gone SSW, Rear-Admiral **Magon** left the bay with **Algésiras, Achille, Argonaute, Neptune, Héros, Duguay-Trouin and Bahama**, 7 of the line, and the frigate **Rhin**. The frigate Hermione was towed out by her boats, and Thémis followed in the evening. The last were through the channel by about 1 p.m.
  - The count varies: "Une division de neuf vaisseaux" (Prigny); "12 ships only" (James III, p. 385; Nicolas VII, p. 130).
  - Euryalus's log (NRS II, p. 146): at 4 p.m., "9 of the enemy's ships out"; at 6 p.m., "12 in number" standing north on the larboard tack. Probably the frigates are counted in.
- **The rest.** Lay at anchor or tried and failed. Euryalus counted 19 under way at 8 a.m.
- **Where Magon's division was.**
  - About 6 p.m., 2 leagues WSW of the Cádiz light (Desbrière p. 161). Euryalus had them ENE of her, about 3 miles off.
  - Villeneuve signalled them to anchor; Magon chose to stay under sail.
  - In the night Bahama and Achille anchored north-west of the Cádiz light near Rota (Desbrière p. 162).
- **Weather.** In the bay: light N by E, then calm. In the afternoon a light WNW breeze; at 8 p.m. SW (James, who misprints the time as "8 a.m."; Euryalus's log shows the evening). Nelson's fleet, 50 miles out, had light southerly airs.

### H2-31 20 October: the combined fleet (Medium)

- **Weighing.** From 6:30 a.m. the signal was to weigh as quickly as possible, with a light southerly or south-easterly breeze in the bay. The ships had the flood tide.
- **Out by late morning.** The fleet was clear of the passes by about 11 a.m. (Desbrière p. 163), "nearly noon" (Corbett p. 337). Rayo was last.
- **Strength.** 33 of the line, 5 frigates and 2 brigs (James; Pickle's log at 3 p.m.).
- **Outside.** The wind was SSW to SW, fresh, with heavy rain and thick weather. Villeneuve ordered reefs and a WNW course, then the larboard tack.
- **Between 1 and 3 p.m.** He wore together and ordered three columns on the starboard tack, heading about SSW (Desbrière pp. 163–164). James and Nicolas say five columns: a 21-ship line in three squadrons plus a 12-ship reserve under Gravina and Magon.
- **About 6 p.m.** Achille sighted 18 British sail to the WSW.
- **About 8 p.m.** He ordered the line of battle on the starboard tack, forming on the leewardmost ships, with Gravina at the head carrying a light. He signalled a night course of SW¼S, which is about S by W true.
- **At nightfall** they were at most 8 or 10 miles south-west of Cádiz, by Desbrière's reading of the British frigates' logs ("8 ou 10 milles au plus", p. 164).
- **Disagreement about the night.**
  - James (III, p. 388): about 5 p.m. they tacked and stood for the Strait's mouth; at 7:30 p.m. Aigle signalled 18 British ships in line to the south, and the fleet wore and stood north-west.
  - Nicolas and Beatty: "The Enemy wore twice during the night."
  - Desbrière (p. 178): Nelson twice received *inaccurate* reports that the allies were going about.
  - Corbett: the mean course must have been about south. Desbrière: they kept the starboard tack.
- **At dawn on the 21st.** Heading south on the starboard tack in great disorder. Villeneuve put himself 4 leagues from Cape Trafalgar (Desbrière pp. 165–166; scene 1 notes).

### H2-32 19–21 October: the British fleet (High for the sequence, Medium for the positions)

1. **19 October, 9:30 a.m.** About 16 leagues WSW of Cádiz (James), "about fifty miles W.S.W." (Nicolas), or "nearly fifty miles to the westward" (Corbett).
   - Nelson's diary: Cádiz "E.N.E. by compass, distant sixteen leagues". Corbett (p. 333 n.) thinks this a slip for E.S.E.
   - Nelson's noon letter: Cádiz "E.S.E., 16 Leagues".
   - **Victory's log, noon: 36°36′ N 7°30′ W** (Corbett p. 333 n.).
   - General chase SE in light southerly airs.
2. **19 October, evening.** The fleet was to follow the admiral's motions in the night. Britannia, Prince and Dreadnought, heavy sailers, could keep station as convenient. Six ships went ahead with lights toward the Strait.
3. **20 October, 1 a.m.** Hove to (Corbett p. 335).
4. **Dawn.** "between Trafalgar and Cape Spartel" (Nelson's diary), close to the Strait's mouth and in sight of Gibraltar (Corbett). No enemy in sight, thick rain and a fresh SSW wind.
5. **About 7 a.m.** The fleet wore and stood NW. Phoebe signalled the enemy to the north.
6. **8:00–9:11.** Victory hove to; Collingwood and the captains of Mars, Colossus and Defence came on board (Beatty).
7. **Noon.** "Cadiz N.E. 9 leagues" (Nelson's diary, p. 137). Other figures: "within eight or nine leagues" SW (James); "some twenty miles south-west" (Corbett); about 25 miles SW (Desbrière); about 28 miles SW (Clowes).
8. **2 p.m.** The wind shifted suddenly from SSW to WNW and the fleet was taken aback.
9. **4 p.m.** Wore, heading north.
10. **About 5 p.m.** Victory told Blackwood "that he depended on the Euryalus for keeping sight of the Enemy during the night". Euryalus answered at 5:40 by her clock.
11. **About 6 p.m.** Naiad signalled 31 enemy sail NNE.
12. **8 or 8:40 p.m.** Wore and stood SW. Nelson used night signal No. 31 and burned blue lights to show his position (Corbett p. 340 n.). Africa missed the signal and held on north.
13. **About midnight.** Some 20 miles SW of Cádiz and 5–6 miles from the allies (Desbrière p. 178).
14. **21 October, 4 a.m.** Wore again: N by E by Victory's log, "to the N.E." by Nelson's diary.
15. **Dawn.** Cape Trafalgar E by S, about 7 leagues; see the scene 1 notes.

### H2-33 Night signals, 20–21 October (High)

- **Nelson's memorandum, 20 October** (Nicolas VII, p. 136): "If the Enemy are standing to the Southward, or towards the Straits …"
- **Euryalus** (NRS II, pp. 148–149):
  - At 6 p.m. she made several lights and burned false fires to show the enemy's position to Nelson.
  - She wore at 8:30 and 9:50, then kept "upon the enemy's weather beam, about 2 or 3 miles", firing guns and burning false fires as needed.
  - At midnight the body of the enemy was **SE by S** about 3 miles (NRS printing; Nicolas prints **SE by E**), and the light of the English fleet S and W, 5 or 6 miles.
- **Sirius:** burned blue lights and rockets all evening, "as did the Phoebe, Euryalus, Naiad, and all our look-out Ships". At 7:40 p.m. the enemy was making signals with guns and rockets.
- **Phoebe:** her blue lights and rockets were "answered by our Fleet".
- **Naiad:** repeated blue lights and sky-rockets.
- All three logs are in Nicolas VII, pp. 134–135.
- **Nicolas's narrative** (p. 136, after Beatty): a set number of guns with false fires and blue lights reported each change of course, each wear and each change of sail, repeated from Euryalus down to Victory. Nelson's diary: the frigates "told me by signals which tack they were upon."

### H2-34 Wind and weather, 20–21 October (High)

- **20 October, morning.** "Fresh breezes SS W. and rainy" (diary). Strong breezes and thick weather with rain (Euryalus).
- **Noon.** "fresh gales and heavy rain" (diary).
- **About 2 p.m.** The wind shifted WNW and the weather cleared (James).
- **Night.** Moderate breezes, light by 11:30 p.m. (Sirius: "light airs, inclinable to calm"), with squalls, rain and lightning (scene 1 notes).
- **21 October, 4 a.m.** "Light breezes and hazy" (Euryalus). Wind WNW (James).

## 8. Fleet sizes at Cádiz and at Trafalgar

### H2-35 The combined fleet (High)

- **Sailed:** 33 of the line, 18 French and 15 Spanish (Collingwood's dispatch; Nicolas VII, p. 130; James).
  - French: 4 eighty-gun ships and 14 seventy-fours.
  - Spanish: Santísima Trinidad (130 or 136 guns); two of 112, Santa Ana and Príncipe de Asturias; Rayo 100; two eighties; eight seventy-fours; and San Leandro 64.
  - With them: 5 French frigates (Cornélie, Hermione, Hortense, Rhin, Thémis) and 2 brigs (Argus, Furet). The Spaniards had no frigates with the fleet.
- **Left in port:**
  - **Terrible 74** (Spanish): damaged at Finisterre, disarmed, her crew shared out (James III, p. 385; Clowes V, p. 128).
  - **San Fulgencio 64**: "for some unknown reason detained" (James; Clowes).
  - **Observateur**, a French light vessel: stayed at anchor by order (Desbrière p. 174).
  - This is consistent with Clowes (V, p. 121): Villeneuve's 29 from Ferrol plus 6 Spanish ships under Alava already at Cádiz made 35, and 35 − 2 = 33.
- **British counts of the enemy:**

  | Count | Source |
  | --- | --- |
  | 35 or 36 of the line | Nelson, 28 September |
  | about 36 | Nelson, 2 October |

### 9.2 Where the combined fleet lay inside Cádiz bay (H2-36; Medium for the description, Low for the coordinates)

The words in the sources:

- **Inner harbour**, late August to early October.
  - On 20 August Argonauta anchored between the Puntal and Matagorda forts (Escaño, in Desbrière, annex p. 8).
  - Within ten days of arrival Villeneuve brought his fastest ships under Magon "down to just within the harbour mouth" (Fraser p. 29).
- **"in the outer harbour, apparently ready for sea"**: Euryalus on 1 October (James III, p. 380; Clowes).
- **"not in the bay, but crowded up in the harbour"**: Corbett, about 29 September (p. 318).
- **Harbour mouth and roadstead from 9–10 October** (James p. 385; Fraser p. 53, "from the inner harbour of Cadiz to the roadstead"; Mahan, "from the inner harbor to the Bay of Cadiz"). Nelson's diary, 14 October: "Enemy at the harbour's mouth."

## 10. Small corrections and slips found along the way

### H2-41 Magnetic versus true (Medium)

- The Euryalus noon fix on 19 October has an observed latitude of 36°28′ N. Her bearing of San Sebastián, E½N 7–8 miles, fits that latitude only when read as magnetic. Read as magnetic it gives 36°28.3′ N; read as true, 36°31.0′ N.
- The bearings in letter headings were probably compass bearings too. Nelson's rule "never getting to the Northward of it" is consistent with a fleet kept slightly south of Cádiz's latitude (my inference).
- Corbett used the magnetic reading.

### H2-42 Slips in the printed sources (High)

1. Nicolas (VII, p. 53) prints the diary heading "Sunday, September 28th". 28 September 1805 was a Saturday, as Beatty prints it.
2. James (III, p. 385) gives "At 8 a.m." for the wind going SW on 19 October. Euryalus's log shows the evening.
3. Corbett (p. 327) has "Captain Duff of the Defence". Duff commanded Mars, as on Corbett's p. 357.
4. Correspondance XI prints No. 9113 as "22 mai 1805"; the sequence shows 22 August.
5. Nicolas's extracts from Victory's log mix the nautical and civil day:
   - "October 3rd, P.M. at 6" for Louis leaving matches civil 2 October;
   - "28th September, P.M. At 6" for joining the fleet matches civil 28 September.
   - The joining date stands on Nelson's letters and Duff's "Saturday night"; Calder's and Africa's dates differ by a day between the log and the narratives (H2-18).
6. Euryalus's midnight bearing of the enemy on 20–21 October is SE by S in the NRS edition and SE by E in Nicolas.

## 11. Open questions and doubts

1. **Where exactly the combined fleet lay in the bay**, both the inner harbour and the harbour mouth. A period plan of Cádiz bay (Tofiño 1789, or Spanish naval archives) would settle the glyph positions.
2. **Victory's master's log for 1–21 October** (ADM 52), for daily noon positions. Only the 19 October noon position (from Corbett) and the letter headings were available.
3. **How many ships got out on 19 October:** 7 of the line plus frigates (Desbrière), 9 (Prigny) or 12 sail (James, Nicolas, Euryalus).
4. **Whether the combined fleet wore during the night of 20–21 October.** James says it stood NW after 7:30 p.m.; Nicolas and Beatty say it wore twice; Desbrière says the British reports of wearing were wrong.
5. **British strength on 28–29 September:** 27 (Beatty, James) or 29 (Victory's log, Mahan).
6. **Rosily's dates:** Madrid 10–11 October (Beurnonville) or 12 October (Corbett); Cádiz 23 October (expected) or 25 October (Fraser).
7. **When Villeneuve heard Rosily was in Madrid:** 18 October (Desbrière, Corbett) or about 15–16 October (Fraser's narrative).
8. **Day-by-day dates** for Louis (2 or 3 October), Calder (13 or 14 October), Africa (13 or 14 October) and Donegal (17 or 18 October). These come from the nautical and civil day conflict, and from James against Beatty.
9. **The army's route from Boulogne to Metz.** The towns between Boulogne and Metz on the centre road were not traced in the itineraries.
10. **Numbers at Ulm:** about 23,000 marched out (eyewitness), 25,365 prisoners (staff history) or 30,000 (Napoleon's bulletin). The modern figures are 25,000–27,000.
11. **Not consulted first-hand:** Adkins, Knight, Schom, Gardiner (ed.), Chandler, and Desbrière's Spanish-language annex in full. Their figures may differ; the Wikipedia citations of Chandler (pp. 399–400) are only a pointer.

### How the notes were made

The full texts of the period books were downloaded from archive.org into a temporary folder outside the repository and searched there. Nothing was saved in the repository.

The positions in section 9 were computed from the logged bearings, taken as magnetic with variation 21° W, by flat-earth plane sailing. Over 10–60 nm the error is under 0.1′.
