## Scene 2: the road to Trafalgar

Facts for scene 2, from the invasion camps at Boulogne to the fleets' positions at dawn on 21 October: Villeneuve's run to the West Indies and back with Nelson in pursuit, Calder's action off Cape Finisterre, Ferrol and Cádiz, Napoleon's turn east to Ulm, Nelson's watch on Cádiz, the sortie, the signals and the names on the chart. They come from the research notes in `research/scene-02/` (`h1-campaign.md`, `h2-boulogne-cadiz.md`, `h3-signals.md`, `h4-names.md`), their independent checks (`v-*.md`) and the adjudications of every contested verdict (`a-*.md`), which are the final word on the points they rule on; `research/scene-02/README.md` says what each file is. The confidence scale is the one at the top of this file.

Dates are civil, times are ship's times as logged, and bearings are by compass unless marked true; "Dates and conventions" below gives the rules. Positions are in degrees and minutes, distances in nautical miles (nm), with leagues (3 nm) where a source uses them. NRS II is *Logs of the Great Sea Fights* vol. II (Navy Records Society, 1900), printed by civil day.

### Dates and conventions

- **Civil and nautical days.** Dates here are civil, midnight to midnight. British logs of 1805 kept the nautical day, which began at noon of the civil day before, so a log's afternoon of "the 10th" is the civil 9th. Nicolas prints the Victory's log in civil order and corrects it where he noticed ("March 27th [i.e. 26th]", Nicolas VI 374); the Sally's "Sunday 16th June, P.M. 7" is civil Saturday 15 June (Nicolas VI 474). James and Clowes give some British events by the log: Gibraltar 7 May (civil 6), Lagos 10 May (9), Knight's arrival 12 May (11), Antigua 13 June (12), Tetuan "24th at noon" (23). High.
- **The Republican calendar.** French documents are dated by it. Year XIII began on 23 September 1804 and year XIV on 23 September 1805. 1 germinal XIII is 22 March 1805, 1 floréal 21 April, 1 prairial 21 May, 1 messidor 20 June, 1 thermidor 20 July and 1 fructidor 19 August, so 9 germinal is 30 March, 24 floréal 14 May, 16 prairial 5 June, 14 thermidor 2 August and 27 thermidor 15 August; 29 vendémiaire XIV is 21 October. High (computed; Desbrière's own glosses agree).
- **James's one-day shift.** James converted Republican dates himself and came out a day early. Only the dates he took from French documents are affected: Toulon 29 March (for 30), Cartagena 6 April (7), Gibraltar and Cádiz 8 April (9), under way "on the 9th" (10), Martinique sighted 12 May and reached 13 May (13, 14), Martinique left 4 June (5), Vigo left 30 July (31). Clowes copies them. Other dates in his French narrative are right: the San Rafael at Martinique on 16 May, Diamond Rock 29 May to 2 June, Basse-Terre 6 June, the convoy 8 June, Corvo 30 June; his 9 July for the gale is his conversion of the Moniteur letter's "21 messidor" (10 July) and matches Reille's 20 messidor (9 July) only by chance (James III 351, 353). Two are two days early (Vigo reached 26 July for 28, out of Ferrol 11 August for 13), and his A Coruña on 1 August is tied to the British being blown off station that morning, so it is not simply his slip. No James date can be corrected mechanically (`research/scene-02/a-h1ac.md`, H1-02b). High.
- **The Atlas's tables.** Desbrière prints the 74 Atlas's noon positions in two tables. In the first (Toulon to Cádiz, p. 9) a row labelled N germinal gives the noon position of N+1 germinal (the label is the day on which that noon-to-noon run began), so "18 germinal", the Strait, is 9 April; the frigates' sightings and Orde's letters confirm it. Whether the second (the crossing, p. 26 n.) follows the same rule is disputed, so the crossing gets no day marks (`research/scene-02/a-h1ac.md`, H1-07). His "7 h. 45 du matin" for the anchoring off Cádiz is a slip for the evening. High for the first table, low for the second.
- **Positions, leagues and times.** French longitudes, reckoned from Paris (2°20′14″ E of Greenwich), are converted to Greenwich. A sea league is 3 nm. Mid-ocean positions are dead reckoning: at the Martinique landfall the French reckoning was 36 leagues too far east, at Finisterre about 2° too far west. Times are ship's times as logged (local apparent time). High.
- **Bearings.** Logs and letters give bearings by compass unless they say "true". The variation in 1805 (NOAA's gufm1 model) was about 20° W off Toulon, 18½° W south of Sardinia, 21° W off Cádiz, Cape St Vincent and São Miguel, 24° W at the Finisterre action and 25° W off Ushant, but 2½–3° E in the Lesser Antilles; with west variation, true = magnetic − variation. Positions worked from bearings say which reading they use. High for the model, medium for each worked position.

### Toulon to the West Indies, 30 March to 11 June

- **The plan.** Napoleon's instructions of 2 March 1805 sent Villeneuve, with his 11 of the line, from Toulon to Cádiz to collect the Aigle and the Spanish ships ready there, and on to Martinique to wait 40 days for Ganteaume. Ganteaume was to come out of Brest with 21 of the line, attack the seven or eight British ships off Ferrol, collect Gourdon's four and the Spanish ships there and cross to the West Indies; joined there by Villeneuve and Missiessy, about 40 of the line were to come back by Ushant and make straight for Boulogne. If Ganteaume did not come, Villeneuve was to land 1,300 troops, cruise off the Canaries for the India convoys and go back to Cádiz (Desbrière 1907, pp. 4–5). Ganteaume never got out: he anchored in Bertheaume roads on 27 March, the British appeared in strength, and under orders not to attack he went back into Brest, on the day, Desbrière writes, that Villeneuve sailed (p. 5). The Emperor's new orders of 13–14 April, sent on the 17th with Magon, told Villeneuve to wait about a month (35 days) after Magon's arrival, taking what British islands he could, then return to Ferrol, collect the ships there, free Ganteaume at Brest and enter the Channel with more than 50 of the line; Decrès's dispatch of 29 April, with a postscript of 1 May, pressed the attack on the islands and the return to Ferrol in any case. The Didon carried both and reached Martinique first, on 30 May (pp. 30–34; the other dates of her arrival are under "At Fort-de-France"). High.
- **Toulon, 30 March.** Villeneuve weighed at about 4 p.m. on 30 March 1805 and was a league south-east of Cap Sépet by 7 p.m. (Reille and the Atlas's log, Desbrière 1907, pp. 7, 9; his letter of 9 germinal). Nelson's frigates Active and Phoebe saw him at 8 a.m. on the 31st (10 a.m. in his note to Ball, Nicolas VI 400; James, III 339, and Clowes have the afternoon), ten leagues south of Cape Sicié, steering SSW (Nicolas VI 396–398). Nelson dated the sailing "in the night of Saturday, March 30th"; the "31st" of two other letters is the sighting. "29 March" is James's slip (III 339), copied by Clowes. The scene uses 30 March. High.
- **His force.** 11 of the line, four 80s and seven 74s (Bucentaure, Formidable, Neptune, Indomptable; Pluton, Mont-Blanc, Berwick, Atlas, Swiftsure, Scipion, Intrépide), six frigates and the brigs Furet and Naïade (Reille's "Pléiade" is an error), with about 3,070 troops under Lauriston (Desbrière 1907, pp. 6–7, 27). The British frigates counted seven frigates; James has 3,500 troops. High for the ships, medium for the troops.
- **Inside the Balearics.** He meant to pass east of the Balearics, but on 1 April a merchant ship, Ragusan by James and Corbett, told him Nelson was south of Sardinia, and he went between the islands and Spain. He lay becalmed off Cartagena on the morning of 7 April; Salcedo's six Spanish ships there did not join. A fresh easterly took him past Cabo de Gata that night (Desbrière 1907, p. 8; Corbett 1910, pp. 56, 61); a neutral's report to the Leviathan, and Nelson's order to Capel of 18 April, have him off the cape on the 7th (James III 341; Nicolas VI 408). High.
- **The Strait, 9 April.** The Rock and Ape's Hill were in sight in the morning, and at about 9 a.m. the fleet entered the Strait in two columns, frigates ahead (Desbrière 1907, p. 9). Every British document written at or near the scene gives the 9th: Orde's two letters of 10 April, Gardner's of 22 April, the Admiralty's order of 25 April and the Beagle's sighting off Cape Spartel; so do the American consuls at Cádiz and Lisbon. "8 April" goes back to a Ragusan's report that reached Nelson off Toro on 18 April; he passed it to the Admiralty that day, and his order to Capel of the same day has the enemy off Cabo de Gata on the 7th and through the Strait the day after (Nicolas VI 408). Nicolas's chronology, Mahan (for Gibraltar) and Adkin carry it; James gives it both as the news that reached Nelson (III 341) and in his French narrative, where it is his one-day slip (III 342), and Clowes copies him. The scene uses 9 April. High.
- **Orde.** Sir John Orde lay off Cádiz with five of the line: Glory 98, Defence 74 and the 64s Polyphemus, Agamemnon and Ruby. Strachan's Renown, which had seen the French at daybreak off Tarifa, joined him about midday with the warning, and the six drew off towards Lagos; the French frigates signalled them about 4 p.m. (Corbett 1910, pp. 62–63; Desbrière 1907, p. 17 n.). High.
- **Cádiz, 9–10 April.** The French anchored outside the bay at nightfall on 9 April, about 7.45–8 p.m., and were under sail again by about 2 a.m. on the 10th, a stop of about six hours (Desbrière 1907, pp. 9–11; Corbett 1910, p. 68, has "less than four hours"). Out to them came the French Aigle 74, the corvette Torche and the brig Argus, and Gravina with Argonauta 80, San Rafael 80, Firme 74, Terrible 74, América 64, España 64 and the frigate Santa Magdalena, with 1,930 Spanish troops (Decrès's return; James: 1,600). The scene anchors the French in open water 3.2 nm west of Las Puercas, an estimate, where `research/scene-02/a-h2c.md` gives 12–16 m of water today (see "Routes on the chart"). High for the times, low for the position.
- **Fourteen crossed together.** The San Rafael grounded coming out. At dawn on 10 April only Argonauta was with the 12 French, and América rejoined on the 12th, so 14 crossed in company. Firme, Terrible and España, with the Magdalena, crossed on their own and were found off Fort-de-France on 14 May; San Rafael came in on the 16th (Reille, in Desbrière 1907, pp. 11, 26; Troude III 342; `research/scene-02/a-h1ac.md`, GLYPH-crossing). `research/scene-02/h1-campaign.md` (section 6) has "17 sailed together", which is wrong. High.
- **The crossing.** South along the Moroccan coast to Cape Cantin, west from about 15 April, north of the Canaries and close to the Salvages, then south-west; westerlies forced a run SSW from about 26 April to 2 May, then west along 18–14½° N (Desbrière 1907, p. 25; a Spanish log captured later, Nicolas VI 468). Formidable, Intrépide and above all Atlas held the fleet back. At 1 p.m. on 13 May a frigate signalled land seven leagues to the west, 36 leagues before the reckoning expected it (Desbrière 1907, p. 26 n.). High for the route, medium for the daily positions.
- **Martinique, 14 May.** At daybreak the fleet entered the St Lucia Channel and during the day anchored off Fort-de-France (Fort Royal), fired on in passing by the British post on Diamond Rock: 17 of the line that day, 18 when San Rafael came in on the 16th (Villeneuve's report, Desbrière 1907, pp. 25–26). James's 13 May is his slip; Clowes gives both. High.
- **At Fort-de-France.** The Didon brought Decrès's dispatch of 29 April with a copy of the Emperor's orders of 14 April: take British islands, wait 35 days after Magon's arrival, then return to Ferrol (see "The plan"). She arrived on 29 May (Mahan), 30 May (Reille), 31 May (Corbett) or 1 June (James). Diamond Rock was attacked from 31 May; its garrison, 107 (James) or 128 (Desbrière), surrendered on 2 June (Corbett: 3 June). On 4 June Rear-Admiral Magon joined with Algésiras and Achille and 861 soldiers (James: 840), making 20 of the line, 14 French and 6 Spanish (Desbrière 1907, p. 36); James has the junction at sea, on the day the fleet sailed or the next (III 351). High, except the Didon's date and where Magon joined.
- **North through the islands.** The fleet sailed at 5 a.m. on 5 June (James: the 4th), passed under the lee of Dominica on the 6th, embarked troops off Basse-Terre, Guadeloupe, that afternoon and spent the night of the 7th under the lee of Antigua (Desbrière 1907, pp. 40–41). At 10 a.m. on 8 June, to leeward of Barbuda, it sighted a convoy of 15 sail from St John's and after a five-hour chase took 14 (James: all 15). The escort escaped: the schooner Netley under Lieutenant William Carr by Corbett (p. 166), the Barbadoes and Netley under Harward by James (III 351). High.
- **The turn, 10 June.** The prisoners said Nelson was at Barbados with 12 or 14 of the line. By 10 June the borrowed troops were in four frigates for Guadeloupe and the fleet stood NNE for the Azores and Ferrol (Corbett 1910, p. 167; Desbrière 1907, pp. 41–43). Villeneuve's letter is dated at sea on 11 June, and Desbrière once has him sailing that day; James has "the 9th or 10th". The scene puts "10 June" at the turn, about 19°14′ N 61°44′ W on Corbett's chart. Medium.

### Nelson's pursuit

- **Where he was.** After showing himself off Barcelona, Nelson anchored in the Gulf of Palmas, south-west Sardinia, at 4.40 p.m. on 26 March (the log's "March 27th [i.e. 26th]", Nicolas VI 374; James and Nicolas's chronology give the log date, 27 March). He weighed at 6.15 p.m. on 30 March, about two hours after Villeneuve, anchored in Pula Roads at 5.30 p.m. on the 31st and sailed again at 5.30 a.m. on 3 April (Nicolas VI 390, 392). High.
- **The news.** The Phoebe rejoined at about 10 a.m. on 4 April, some six leagues WSW of Toro (Nicolas VI 393). Nelson had 11 of the line, four frigates and two corvettes (Nicolas VI 398 n.); the eleven names, his ten later West Indies ships and the Royal Sovereign, are inferred. High for the count, medium for the names.
- **East first.** Fearing for Sicily, Naples or Egypt, he waited 48 hours between Sardinia and Galita (4–6 April), bore up for Palermo from the south end of Sardinia on the 7th, and was off Palermo on 9–10 April. He turned west on the 9th by his letter of 18 April, or the 10th by one dated off Palermo that day (Nicolas VI 401–408); Corbett's arrival at Ustica on the 7th is wrong (`research/scene-02/a-h1b.md`, S3-PALERMO). High; medium for the 9th or 10th.
- **Against the wind.** Beating back took nine days to regain Sardinia. Off Toro on 18 April he learned the French had passed the Strait, and on the 19th he was still ten leagues west of Toro with the wind dead against him (Corbett 1910, pp. 93–95). He sighted the Rock on 30 April, watered at Tetuan from 10.30 a.m. on 4 May and lay in Gibraltar Bay from 2 to 6 p.m. on 6 May (James: the 7th, a log date). There, by Campbell's own later complaint, Rear-Admiral Donald Campbell of the Portuguese navy came aboard as Nelson "passed the Gut" and told him where the enemy had gone (Nicolas VI 431 n.); Mahan and Desbrière put the visit at Lagos. High; medium for Campbell.
- **Lagos and the bear-away.** He anchored in Lagos Bay at 9 p.m. on 9 May to clear Orde's storeships, weighed at 9.10 a.m. on 11 May (Corbett's move off the cape on the 10th is not in the log), met Rear-Admiral Knight with Craig's troop convoy at 4 p.m., gave him the Royal Sovereign, and at 6.50 p.m. bore up for the West Indies with Cape St Vincent NW by N 7 leagues, about 36°47′ N 8°41′ W (Nicolas VI 433; `research/scene-02/a-h1b.md`, S3-11MAY). The sloop Martin, which Nelson had written on the 10th was going "this day", parted ahead that afternoon by the log. High for the times, medium for the position.
- **The squadron.** Ten of the line: Victory, Canopus, Superb, Spencer, Donegal, Tigre, Leviathan, Belleisle, Conqueror and Swiftsure, with the frigates Decade, Amphion and Amazon (Nicolas VI 432 n.; Corbett 1910, p. 108). James and Clowes print nine names. High.
- **Barbados, 4 June.** He was 38 leagues from Madeira on 14 May and saw it on the 15th. Barbados was in sight at 6 a.m. on 4 June, and the squadron anchored in Carlisle Bay at 4.50 p.m. (Nicolas VI 436, 445; Desbrière too prints 4.50; the "4.30" of `research/scene-02/h1-campaign.md` was an OCR misreading). He had run 3,227 miles from Cape St Vincent and gained about ten days on Villeneuve (Nicolas VI 471; Corbett 1910, p. 160). No source gives a date other than 4 June. Cochrane's Northumberland and the Spartiate made 12 of the line; Dacres had kept four of Cochrane's ships at Jamaica (James, Clowes) or five of his six (Corbett). High; medium for Cochrane's ships.
- **Trinidad.** On a report from St Lucia that 28 sail had passed Gros Islet on the night of 28 May, Nelson embarked about 2,000 troops and sailed south at 9.30 a.m. on 5 June. Off Tobago at 6.10 p.m. on the 6th a schooner made, by chance, the signal for the enemy at Trinidad. On the 7th he went through the Bocas into the Gulf of Paria ready for battle and found nothing; at daylight on the 8th he learned the enemy had been at Martinique on the 4th (Nicolas VI 446–451). High.
- **Antigua.** Off Grenada on 9 June he learned, from the Jason at 1 p.m., that the enemy had passed Dominica on the 6th, steering north. He was off Guadeloupe and Montserrat on the 11th, anchored in St John's Road, Antigua, at 7 p.m. on the 12th, sent the brig Curieux home with dispatches, and sailed for Europe at 11.50 a.m. on 13 June with 11 of the line, the Northumberland staying (Nicolas VI 451–456). High.
- **Homeward.** Thinking the allies bound for Cádiz or Toulon, he kept south of their track: south of the western and central Azores, then through the archipelago between Terceira and São Miguel, passing north of São Miguel on 8–9 July (log, Nicolas VI 468; Mahan). Villeneuve had gone north of the whole group, by Corvo. His frigates left on the way: the Decade and the sloop Martin on 19 June, the Amazon on 13 July, the Amphion on a day not recorded. He made Cape St Vincent on 17 July and anchored in Rosia Bay, Gibraltar, at 7.30 a.m. on 19 July (Mahan once has the 20th); ashore on the 20th, he noted it was his first time since June 1803 (Nicolas VI 471–475). High.
- **North.** He watered at Tetuan on 22–23 July. Off Tarifa at about 3.30–4 a.m. on 25 July (3.30 by Mahan; "four o'clock" in Nelson's letter to Collingwood) the sloop Termagant brought a Lisbon paper with the Curieux's news, and he went north at once against northerly winds; his diary has 39°1′ N 16° W on 3 August and 41°8′ N 18°14′ W on the 7th (Desbrière 1907, pp. 74–75). On 15 August he joined Cornwallis off Ushant (24 of the line in sight at noon) and left him nine ships, then anchored with Victory and Superb at Spithead at 8.30 a.m. on 18 August and struck his flag on the 19th (Nicolas VII 4–8). High.

### Cape Finisterre, 22 and 23 July

- **Villeneuve's run home.** From the turn the fleet stood NNE (Desbrière 1907, p. 43) to about 33–35° N on 21–24 June (Corbett's chart, after Desbrière's), then ENE, north of the whole Azores group, sighting Corvo on 2 July (Reille, in Desbrière 1907, p. 57). The American schooner Sally saw "about 22" large ships at 7 p.m. on 15 June near her noon position, 27°28′ N 60°58′ W (Nicolas VI 463; her log has the nautical "16th"). High for the route, medium for the positions.
- **The Curieux.** Bettesworth, carrying Nelson's dispatches, saw the allies on 19 June, by his report as far north as 33°12′ N, in 58° W (Corbett 1910, p. 181; James III 354), but Corbett's own chart, after Desbrière's, marks the sighting near 30°13′ N 58°06′ W. He reached Plymouth on 7 July and the Admiralty late on the 8th (Corbett) or by daylight on the 9th (Mahan, Desbrière), and Barham at once sent Stirling's Rochefort squadron to join Calder off Ferrol and both to cruise 30–40 leagues west of Finisterre. High for the date, low for the position.
- **Corvo to Finisterre.** Five frigates rejoined about 20 leagues NE of Corvo on 30 June (James); Reille has Corvo sighted on 2 July. On 3 July the fleet took the Liverpool privateer Mars and her prize, the Spanish galleon Matilda. A north-east gale on 9 July cost the Indomptable her main topmast; on the 12th the fleet was within about 300 miles of the coast west of Oporto (Corbett; about 365 nm on his chart), was then driven back west for four days, and from the 19th ran east, in fog on the 21st and 22nd (Corbett 1910, p. 195; Desbrière 1907, p. 57). Villeneuve wrote of twenty days against north-easterly winds. High for the events, medium for the 30 June and 2 July positions.
- **Calder's squadron.** After Stirling's five joined on 15 July, Vice-Admiral Sir Robert Calder had 15 of the line, four 98s, one 80, eight 74s and two 64s, with the frigates Egyptienne and Sirius, the lugger Nile and the cutter Frisk (James III 356–357; Calder's dispatch). High.
- **Villeneuve's fleet.** 20 of the line, 14 French and 6 Spanish, with 7 frigates, 2 brigs and the Matilda (James III 357; Troude III 352). Calder reported 20 of the line, three large ships armed en flûte, five frigates and three brigs. High.
- **Fog.** On the morning of 22 July the allies steered ESE by compass (about east true) for Ferrol in three columns, the Spaniards on the right, in thick fog and a light WNW breeze. It lifted a little about noon, and the Defiance reported 24, then 27 sail to the SSW (Corbett 1910, p. 196; James: SW). High.
- **Where.** There are three British noon positions: 43°30′ N 11°17′ W in Calder's dispatch (the primary one), 43°54′ N 11°38′ W in James, and 44°17′ N 11°52′ W at 11.30 a.m. in Desbrière. The allies were about 16 miles SSW of the British. The French reckoning (13°53′ W) ran about 2° too far west, and Corbett's chart (12°20′ W) and his figure of 300 miles from Ferrol are also too far west. The fight lay within about 43°20′–43°55′ N, 11°15′–12°00′ W, 95–130 nm WNW of Cape Finisterre; the scene puts its smoke at 43°35′ N 11°40′ W, 113 nm from the cape (`research/scene-02/a-h1ac.md`, H1-26). Medium.
- **The action.** At about 5.15 p.m. the Hero tacked and the Argonauta opened fire (Desbrière: about 5). The lines fought on the starboard tack heading about WSW, in fog and smoke so thick that a ship could see little beyond her own length (James III 361). The firing stopped at about 7.30 in the dark (Desbrière), at nine (Calder at his trial), or at 9.30, an hour after the signal to discontinue (James). High for the start, medium for the end.
- **Two prizes.** The San Rafael 80 (84 in the Gazette) and the Firme 74 drifted dismasted into the British line and struck: soon after 8 p.m. by the British account, at daybreak on the 23rd by the French (James III 361; Desbrière 1907, p. 64). Their captains were Montes and Villavicencio. High for the ships, medium for the hour.
- **Losses.** British 41 killed and 158 wounded, ship by ship in Calder's return; James's 39 and 159 is his own count. Allied: 155 killed and 341 wounded in Corbett's table (p. 202), 476 killed and wounded in James, about 580 with Desbrière's footnote for the prizes. A caption can say "about 500". High for the British, medium for the allies.
- **23 and 24 July.** At dawn the fleets were about 17 miles apart. Calder drew together round the crippled Windsor Castle and his prizes and stood NE; Villeneuve, now in the frigate Hortense, bore down between noon and 3 p.m., then hauled his wind three or four leagues off. On the 24th the wind went round to the north and Calder, now to windward, did not attack; they lost sight of each other that evening (James III 363–365) or the next morning (Corbett 1910, p. 205); Mahan has the 26th. High.
- **Calder's fate.** At his own request he was tried in Portsmouth harbour on 23–26 December 1805 and cleared of cowardice and disaffection, but for an error in judgment in not renewing the action he was "severely reprimanded" (Naval Chronicle XV 174–175). Nelson let him go home in his own flagship, which parted on 14 October by the Victory's log, so he missed Trafalgar. High.

### Ferrol, the turn south and Cádiz

- **Vigo.** On 25 July the Didon sighted Cape Finisterre ESE. With the north-east wind rising and sickness spreading, Villeneuve chose Cádiz, then Vigo, tried for Ferrol again on the 26th, and on the 27th wrote from 40 leagues WSW of Finisterre. The fleet anchored in Vigo Bay at nightfall on 28 July (Desbrière 1907, pp. 67–69, 85; Mahan); the Cíes came in sight at 6 p.m. on the 27th or the 28th. James's "evening of the 26th" is two days early. High for 28 July, low for the Cíes.
- **To A Coruña.** Leaving the Atlas (with about 800 sick), España and América at Vigo, he sailed on 31 July (James: the 30th) with 15 of the line, 13 French and 2 Spanish, into a north-west gale. The wind went south-west on 1 August, blew Calder off Ferrol and let Villeneuve double Cape Finisterre that evening; he entered the bay on 2 August (Desbrière 1907, p. 86; Corbett 1910, p. 219, dating the junction). Napoleon's order of 16 July kept the French out of Ferrol: the 13 French anchored at A Coruña and the two Spaniards went on into Ferrol. James and Mahan, and Corbett's narrative (p. 218), have him in on 1 August; James ties his date to Calder's being blown off that morning, so it is not simply his slip. The scene uses 2 August. Medium.
- **Twenty-nine of the line.** With Gourdon's five French and nine Spanish from Ferrol, the combined fleet had 29 of the line, 18 French and 11 Spanish, with 6 frigates and 4 corvettes: one three-decker, the Príncipe de Asturias (112 guns by Desbrière, 120 by Corbett), and six 80s; the San Julián stayed behind (Desbrière 1907, pp. 98–99; Corbett 1910, p. 239). The ships came out between 8 and 10 August to the outer bay off Ares, the last three joined before noon on 13 August, and by 2 p.m. all were under sail (Desbrière 1907, pp. 97–98; Villeneuve's letter, "25 thermidor (13 août)"). James has the fleet out at sea on the 11th, two days early. On the 10th Villeneuve had written that he would steer for Brest or Cádiz as circumstances allowed. High.
- **West-north-west.** The fleet ran west in a fresh north-easterly, stood NNE towards Cape Prior for some hours on sighting a British ship, and steered NW again from 2 a.m. on the 14th. The frigate Iris watched it, on a course W by N ½ N at 44°17′ N 9°26′ W, until about 2 p.m. (Corbett 1910, p. 259 n.). At sunset the Dragon saw 26 sail; a Danish merchantman she had boarded, told she belonged to a fleet of 25 of the line, passed the story to a French frigate (Corbett 1910, p. 262; James III 374). High.
- **His orders.** The Emperor's letter and Decrès's dispatch of 16 July, with Decrès's private letter of the 17th (received at Ferrol only on 6 August), told Villeneuve, once he had Gourdon's ships, to free the Rochefort squadron and make France master of the Strait of Dover: by joining the Brest and Rochefort squadrons, or the Brest squadron alone, or by taking the Rochefort squadron round Ireland and Scotland. After a defeat, a separation or any change in the situation, he was allowed to go to Cádiz (Desbrière 1907, p. 81). The Emperor's letter also kept the French out of Ferrol itself (p. 86). His letter from Cádiz of 22 August rests the turn on the dispatch of 16 July, and Decrès told Napoleon on the 23rd that it was his only ground (p. 112 and n.). High.
- **The turn, 15 August.** Still standing WNW against the north-east gale, Villeneuve turned for Cádiz "à l'entrée de la nuit" on 15 August, the third day out (his letter of 22 August, Desbrière 1907, p. 112 n.). Desbrière's narrative once calls the 16th the decisive day (p. 102), and his print of the same letter on p. 103 reads "13 août"; both are slips, since the days described are the 14th and 15th. Reille's noon put the fleet 40 leagues WNW of Finisterre (Corbett 1910, p. 263 n.): about 43°39′ N 11°48′ W if the bearing is true, as Corbett reads it (p. 261 n.), about 42°50′ N 12°00′ W if by compass; the Iris's fix of the 14th favours the first. Villeneuve wrote 80 leagues, which Corbett shows to be impossible; Desbrière's staff map has the fleet near 44°40′ N 12°25′ W; James implies the evening of the 14th. The scene bends the line south at about 43°45′ N 12°20′ W. High for the date, low for the position.
- **South.** The fleet ran south before the north-easterly, out of sight of land, and was off Cape St Vincent on 17 August (Desbrière's text) or 18 August (James; Desbrière's map), where it burned three merchantmen of a convoy whose escort, the brig Halcyon, escaped; from the 18th Blackwood's Euryalus shadowed it. At 10 a.m. on 20 August, with Cádiz NE about nine leagues, it sighted Collingwood's three ships right ahead, chased them towards the Strait (Magon led, says Desbrière; Gravina, says Corbett), bore up for the harbour at 3 p.m. and anchored in Cádiz road that evening (James III 375; Desbrière 1907, p. 107). Medium for St Vincent, high for Cádiz.
- **20 August.** The date is in James (from the ships' muster rolls), Desbrière, Corbett, Escaño's day-by-day relation and Collingwood's letter of the 21st, which says "yesterday". 21 August is in the Précis des événements militaires, which James rejects, and in Escaño's later letter to MacDonnell; Troude's 17 August is impossible, since the turn came only at nightfall on the 15th, some 450–500 nm away. Desbrière's staff map "Situation au 15 août" prints "19 Août" beside the six Spanish ships in Cádiz, while the fleet's track ends in an arrow off the port; if the label dates the arrival, it is a day earlier than his own text (p. 107). The scene uses 20 August. High.
- **Collingwood.** He had the Dreadnought 98, Colossus 74 and Achille 74 with the frigate Endymion and the bomb Thunder, "only three poor things with a frigate and a bomb", as he wrote next day (Corbett 1910, p. 270). He drew off about 21 miles towards the Strait; the Mars rejoined from Tangier about midnight, and he was back off the port before daylight (James III 375; Desbrière: back the same evening, with four). High.
- **After.** 29 of the line came in; with the six Spaniards already in the harbour James counts 35, and Collingwood's "36 sail of men-of-war" counts frigates too. Bickerton's ships joined Collingwood from 22 August, and Calder came south from Cornwallis's fleet on 30 August with 18, giving about 26 of the line (James III 379; Corbett 1910, p. 267); the news Allemand had from a captured brig on 11 September also gave 26 (Desbrière 1907, p. 108). Desbrière's total of 32 (p. 109) counts the Minotaur, Mars and Bellerophon twice; without the double count his lists give 29, because he has Calder bring 21, the Dragon and Goliath among them, "ralliés en route", as Cornwallis's orders intended (Desbrière p. 103 n.; Corbett p. 247); the Dragon was off Vigo again on 10 October (Desbrière p. 108 n.). Calder's ships included eight of the eleven Nelson had brought back from the West Indies (Desbrière 1907, p. 109 n.) (`research/scene-02/a-h1b.md`, S6-BRIT-CADIZ; `a-h1ac.md`, S5-reinf). Where the fleet lay in the bay is under "Positions on the chart". High for 29 in, medium for 35 and 26.

### Boulogne and the invasion army

- **The camps.** Napoleon ordered six camps on 14 June 1803; Desbrière calls that order the origin of the Grande Armée (Desbrière, *Projets* III 184). By 1805 the army was the armée des côtes de l'Océan, set out in Napoleon's note of 15 May 1805 and the war minister's letter of 30 May: Lannes's advance guard (Oudinot's grenadiers), Davout's corps (the camp of Bruges), Soult's (the camp of Saint-Omer, at Boulogne), Ney's (the camp of Montreuil), corps at Utrecht and Brest, a reserve and the Guard (*Projets* IV 459–461). English writers called it the Army of England. High.
- **The ports.** Seven: Étaples, Boulogne, Wimereux, Ambleteuse, Calais, Dunkirk and Ostend. Wimereux and Ambleteuse were made flotilla ports in 1803–04: Napoleon called both new ports he had built (Corr. IX No. 7832, 2 July 1804); James has Wimereux formed into a port and Ambleteuse's existing harbour deepened and enlarged (III 217). Ney's corps lay at Étaples and Montreuil, Soult's two camps either side of Boulogne, Lannes's grenadiers at Wimereux, Davout's corps at Ambleteuse and the reserve at Calais (James III 325–326). High; the two accounts of Wimereux and Ambleteuse stand side by side.
- **Boulogne.** The right camp stood on the cliff plateau north of the Liane by the Tour d'Ordre, where Napoleon had his hut; the left camp on the plateau south of the river; headquarters inland at Pont-de-Briques (Corr. IX 77, 488; XI No. 9137). Medium (`research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R15): the right and left camps (No. 9137), the Tour d'Ordre hut and Pont-de-Briques are in period documents; Outreau for the left camp, and Terlincthun for the right camp's extent, rest on an uncited Wikipedia page.
- **Huts, not tents.** From the autumn of 1803 the troops lived in wooden huts (*baraques*) built for the winter, in rows by regiment: one division of Soult's corps had 748 of them in May–June 1804 (*Projets* III 206–207, 432–433). The huts excavated at Étaples had tiled roofs and glazed windows (Fondation Napoléon, newsletter 410). Tents were campaign kit, sent from Metz to Strasbourg in August 1805 (Corr. XI No. 9137). High. The scene draws rows of small huts along camp streets.
- **The army.** The embarkation return of spring 1805 counted 147,662 men (142,601 combatants) and 15,764 horses: Montreuil 27,050, Saint-Omer (Soult, at Boulogne) 45,907, Bruges 33,012, the reserve 41,693. Its 2,054 craft had room for only 127,500 men and 9,975 horses (*Projets* IV 433). Napoleon's own round figure was 150,000 men and 10,000 horses (Corr. XI No. 9209). High. The modern figures of about 60,000 at Boulogne and over 175,000 in the camps have no source and are not used (`research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R12–R13).
- **The flotilla.** The count of 20 July 1805 found 2,293 craft in the seven ports (1,339 armed, 954 transports); at Boulogne alone 578 prames and gun craft and 526 transports, 1,104 in all (James III 217, 325; Clowes V 62). Medium: other counts are right for their own dates (1,301 built in 1803–04, 2,054 in the spring of 1805; `research/scene-02/h2-boulogne-cadiz.md`, H2-05).
- **The craft.** Prames: three-masted and ship-rigged, 110 French feet, twelve 24-pounders. Chaloupes canonnières: brigs of 76–80 ft. Bateaux canonniers and péniches: luggers of 60 ft. Fishing boats and merchant craft served as transports (*Projets* III 90 and the plate facing it; James III 216–217, who has some gun-vessels as schooners and the péniches "chiefly as schuyts"). High for the designs, medium as built. The flotilla is drawn as rigged gun craft, not barges.
- **Basin and road.** Boulogne's harbour was the Liane estuary, nearly dry at low water, given quays, a dug basin and a dam to keep the craft afloat, with a line of heavy gun-vessels moored across the road (James III 217). On the evening of 19 July 1804, with a strong wind from the north-north-east, 45 brigs and 43 luggers were left at anchor in the road after others had got under way (III 227). At the start of October 1804 about 150 craft lay moored in a double line outside the pier (III 232). After he left, Napoleon meant to keep gun-vessels "in the basin and road" (III 332). High for 1804; medium for August 1805.
- **What the crossing needed.** Command of the Strait (*détroit*, Pas-de-Calais) for a short time, and the span changed often: eight hours of night (November 1803), six hours (2 July 1804; April and June 1805), four or five days (16 July 1805), three days (20 and 26 July, 13 August), twenty-four hours (13 and 22 August) and, looking back, fifteen days (13 September) (Corr. IX–XI; `research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R10). High. Beat 1's "command of the Channel" stands. The six-hours line is No. 7832 (2 July 1804); the Tower of London sentence is genuine but from a letter to Brune of 27 July 1804 (No. 7874), and popular versions splice the two. High.
- **Napoleon at Boulogne.** From 3 August 1805 (his datelines; James III 326; Corbett 1910, p. 240). He had the army embarked twice in his presence. High for the date; medium for the two embarkations, which rest on James alone (III 326).

### The turn east and Ulm

- **The decision, 22–26 August.** Word that Villeneuve had left Ferrol reached Boulogne on 22 August (Desbrière 1907, p. 110). On 23 August Napoleon told Talleyrand "Mon parti est pris", and had Marmont told that if the fleet were held at Cádiz he would put the crossing off "à une autre année" (Corr. XI Nos. 9117, 9120; No. 9120 is misprinted "23 mai"). Cavalry orders followed on the 24th, "Mon mouvement est commencé" on the 25th, and the general order of march on the 26th, when the Grande Armée was named; it was announced on the 29th (Nos. 9128, 9130, 9137; Alombert and Colin I 216–218). High.
- **Why east.** Austria had joined Russia in a new coalition. Russia first approached her in October 1803; the courier with Austria's definite adherence left Vienna on 7 July 1805, and the two signed a treaty on 9 August (Maude 1912, pp. 76, 90). On 23 August Napoleon told Talleyrand that Austria was playing for time, and that by spring he would face Russians in Poland paid by England. So he would raise his camps, be in Germany with 200,000 men by 1 vendémiaire (23 September) and march on Vienna (Corr. XI No. 9117). On the 25th he wrote that Austria's preparations had suspended his embarkation (No. 9130). Mack's columns were to cross the Inn into Bavaria on 8 September (Maude 1912, p. 98). The brief's "against Austria (the Third Coalition)" stands. High for Napoleon's letters; medium for the coalition's dates, which rest on Maude alone (the treaties, and Britain's part, were not researched).
- **Not because of Cádiz.** Napoleon knew Villeneuve was in Cádiz by 31 August at the latest, from a postscript dated 13 fructidor (Corr. XI 157). Corbett's 1 September (p. 278) reads the same postscript; Desbrière's 1–2 September (pp. 115–116) is the arrival of the full dispatches. All of it came after the orders of 23–26 August, and he had weighed the Cádiz case on 22–23 August. James (III 332) has the news arriving about four days after the 22nd, and the Daru story of the Emperor turning east in fury at it (James III, App. 28) does not fit the dates (`research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R9). High. Beat 3 may put Cádiz before the turn east, but must not make it the cause.
- **Put off, not abandoned.** He left nineteen battalions at Ambleteuse, Boulogne and Étaples, concentrated the flotilla at Boulogne and armed its seamen as infantry (No. 9137). On 13 September (a date the editors supply) he set out a plan to keep an army of 60,000–80,000 men on the heights of Boulogne and 500 craft able to carry 40,000–50,000 of them, as a standing threat (No. 9209; Desbrière 1907, p. 120, rounds it to 500 craft and 80,000 men). James (III 332) has 30,000–40,000 men kept encamped on the heights, with the gun-vessels in the basin and road. High. "Gives up on England" (beat 3) is acceptable; "puts off" is nearer his words.
- **The army leaves.** Oudinot's grenadiers marched on 26 August. The first divisions of Davout's, Soult's and Ney's corps left on 28 August (10 fructidor), the second on 30 and the third on 31 August, the rest from 1 September; by Berthier's order nobody marched on the 29th, though Napoleon wrote on the 28th that the second divisions would go "demain" (No. 9137; Alombert and Colin I 337; No. 9155). Napoleon left Boulogne on 2 September and was at Montreuil that evening (No. 9177; Desbrière 1907, p. 116); James (III 332) has 4 September. High. Date line: 28 August 1805.
- **To the Rhine.** About 400 miles from the coast to the Rhine (Maude 1912, p. 123), in 24 days' march by three roads (No. 9137). Soult's corps, from the Boulogne camps, took the centre road through Metz and Bitche (Alombert and Colin I 236, 414). The army crossed between 25 and 30 September: Lannes and Murat at Kehl on the 25th, Davout at Mannheim on 26–29, Soult at Rheinhausen by Speyer on 26–30, Ney between Pforz and Durlach on the 27th (Alombert and Colin II 39–42). Napoleon's 1st Bulletin rounds this to 25–26 September. High.
- **The Danube.** Crossed at Donauwörth on 7 October. Napoleon directed the crossing and slept in the town while his headquarters stayed at Nördlingen, which is why his papers of the 7th bear that name (Schuermans 1908, p. 180; Maude 1912, pp. 199–200). High for the date; medium for the hour (afternoon by Schuermans, morning by Maude).
- **The dashed line.** A composite, labelled as the army. Boulogne–Metz–Bitche–Speyer–Heilbronn–Nördlingen–Donauwörth was Soult's IV corps; from Donauwörth Soult went south by Augsburg and Landsberg, then west to Memmingen and the Biberach road, while Ney's VI corps took Elchingen and Ulm (`research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R6). High. Dates beside it: Boulogne 28 August, the Rhine 25–30 September, the Danube 7 October, Ulm 20 October.
- **Ulm.** Mack's capitulation was signed on 17 October (at noon by Alombert and Colin; the Austrian eyewitness has Berthier come at 2 p.m.), the garrison to become prisoners unless relieved by 25 October. A convention of 19 October brought the march-out forward. On 20 October, from 2 p.m. (it had been announced for 3), the garrison marched out by the Frauentor and laid down its arms before Napoleon, who watched from a rise at the foot of the Michelsberg until about 7 p.m. (Alombert and Colin III/1 225–228; 7th and 8th Bulletins, Corr. XI 339–342). High. The scene uses 20 October; "lays down its arms" is exact, "surrenders" defensible. The 19 October of some modern summaries has no period support.
- **How many.** About 25,000: 25,365 prisoners (Alombert and Colin III/1 229; Maude 1912, p. 250), "vingt-trois mille" by the eyewitness. Napoleon's figures grew from 15,000 and 20,000 on the 17th to 27,000 with 18 generals in the 7th Bulletin (the source of the modern 27,000) and 30,000 in the 8th. Taken with them: 40 colours, 63 guns, 2 howitzers and 13,600 muskets (page image; the OCR's 43,600 is a misreading). Medium.

### Nelson off Cádiz

- **The passage.** Nelson left Merton at 10.30 p.m. on 13 September and sailed from St Helens at 8 a.m. on the 15th (Nicolas VII 33–36). Ajax and Thunderer joined off the Lizard on the morning of the 18th (Nelson to Marsden, VII 45; James and Beatty say off Plymouth). On 26 September he sent the Euryalus ahead to ask for no salute and no colours. High.
- **Joining.** He joined Collingwood at about 6 p.m. on Saturday 28 September, could not communicate until morning, and took command on the 29th, his forty-seventh birthday (Victory's log, Nicolas VII 53 n.; his letters, VII 60, 62–63; Duff, VII 70 n.; Codrington 1873, I 51). Nicolas heads both 28 and 29 September "Sunday", a slip. High. Date line: 28 September 1805.
- **Strength on arrival.** 29 of the line, counting Victory, Ajax and Thunderer: 23 in the main body and 6 inshore under Rear-Admiral Louis (Victory's log and signal log; Nelson to Acton, 30 September, VII 54; Collingwood's 26, named ship by ship in Desbrière 1907, pp. 143–145, plus Nelson's three). James (III 379) gives 27, 22 cruising about 15 miles off Cádiz and 5 inshore; Beatty (p. 2 in both editions) gives 27, with five or six inshore. High for 29 under his command; medium for all 29 in company (`research/scene-02/a-h2b.md`, H2-16).
- **Louis's detachment.** Ordered on 2 October to Gibraltar and Tetuan for water and stores, he sailed that evening with Queen 98, Canopus 80, Spencer 74, Tigre 74 and Zealous 74, the frigate Endymion and five merchantmen (Nelson to Marsden, VII 63; Francis Austen, Hubback 1906, p. 148). The log's "October 3rd, P.M." is the nautical day; Desbrière's 3 October reads it literally. Euryalus's news turned Louis back on the morning of the 3rd; he rejoined on the 4th (Beatty) or the 5th (Austen) and was sent off again, while Zealous and Endymion went on to Gibraltar. Medium-high; the return date is disputed.
- **The count through October.** 23 by 5 October (Nelson's own count); 24 on the 7th (Defiance); 26 on the 8th (Leviathan back from Gibraltar, and Royal Sovereign); 27 on the 10th (Belleisle); 28 on the forenoon of the 13th (Agamemnon); 27 that evening, when Calder went home in the Prince of Wales; 28 when Africa joined (13 or 14 October); 27 from the 17th, when Donegal went to Gibraltar for casks, to the battle (Nicolas VII 75–121; the diary, Beatty 1825, p. 97). James and Clowes give a peak of 29, which needs Africa to have joined before Calder sailed; no source shows that (`research/scene-02/a-h2b.md`, H2-19a). Medium-high; high for the 27.
- **At Trafalgar.** 27 of the line: 3 of 100 guns, 4 of 98, 1 of 80, 16 of 74 and 3 of 64; the frigates Euryalus, Naiad, Phoebe and Sirius; the schooner Pickle (joined 1 October) and the cutter Entreprenante (joined 4 October) (Nicolas VII 64, 74, 130). Louis's five and Donegal were away. In all, 34 different ships of the line served under Nelson off Cádiz. High.
- **Louis on 18–21 October.** He left Gibraltar eastward on the 18th with the Malta convoy, five of the line and three frigates (Austen, Hubback pp. 152–153), and was some 200 miles east of the Strait at dawn on the 20th (Corbett 1910, p. 335). Medium. He is outside the Gulf frame.
- **The station.** Nelson kept the body of the fleet "from sixteen to eighteen leagues West of Cadiz" (to Blackwood and Barham, 4–5 October; "15 lgs. to twenty" in his diary of 14 October, Hudson 1917, p. 21; 15–18 in a letter to Ball, `research/scene-02/v-draft-h3.md`, 5), never north of the port's parallel, ready to crowd sail for Cape Spartel if the enemy came out (Nicolas VII 73–75, 111, 122). His reasons: not to be caught close in by a westerly gale and swept into the Strait, and to hide his strength and tempt the enemy out (to Barham, 5 October; Corbett 1910, pp. 326–327). High.
- **Out of sight.** From a watchtower about 45 m up (probably the Torre Tavira) a masthead shows to about 29 nm in clear air and a hull to about 18–19 nm, so the main body at 45–60 nm was invisible and the inner ships of the line at 21–30 nm were at the limit (computed; `research/scene-02/h2-boulogne-cadiz.md`, H2-24). Beurnonville wrote from Madrid on 20 October that Nelson's force was not in sight and kept only frigates on watch, which he put down to the weather (Desbrière 1907, annexes 70–71). Medium.
- **Blackwood's squadron.** The Euryalus and Hydra were off Cádiz from 29 September; Blackwood had "five Frigates, a Brig, and a Schooner" by 11 October (Nicolas VII 53, 111). On 19 October: Euryalus (Blackwood), Sirius, Phoebe and Naiad, the brig Weazle, sent that day to Gibraltar and Tetuan with the news, and the schooner Pickle, sent to Cape Spartel (NRS II 146). By day they kept close off the harbour, usually 2–8 nm west or south-west of San Sebastián. On 8 October the Euryalus stood in until San Sebastián bore S½W, 2¼ miles, in the mouth of the bay (James III 380). At night a frigate might anchor off the port and the Pickle worked close inshore (Nicolas VII 76, 96). High.
- **The chain.** From 4 October Mars, Defence and Colossus (Duff's squadron, with Ajax at first) stood about four leagues east of the fleet ("from three to four leagues" in the order to Duff, four in the letter to Blackwood), one pushed east toward Cádiz "as near as possible in the latitude", using distant signals and guns "by day or night" (Nicolas VII 70–73; `research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R1). From 14–15 October Defence and Agamemnon lay 7–10 leagues west of Cádiz and Mars and Colossus 5 leagues east of the fleet (diary, 14 October: Nicolas VII 122; Hudson 1917, p. 21). On 19 October the cordon was Colossus, Mars, Defence and Agamemnon (Beatty, p. 6 in both editions; Codrington 1873, I 57). Frigates at the Cádiz end, ships of the line beyond: beat 4's "chain of frigates" is wrong. High. How the signal ran is under "The signal chain, 19 to 21 October".
- **The British track, 19–21 October.** After the general chase at 9.30 the fleet ran south-east for the Strait (the night's signals and lights are under "The signal chain, 19 to 21 October"). It hove to at 1 a.m. on the 20th (Corbett 1910, p. 335). At daybreak it lay "between Trafalgar and Cape Spartel", in sight of Gibraltar, with no enemy in sight, in rain and a fresh SSW wind (diary, Nicolas VII 137; Corbett 1910, pp. 335–336): about 36°00′ N 5°58′ W. It wore and stood north-west, and at 7 the Phoebe signalled the enemy to the north (James III 390). At noon Cádiz bore NE, 9 leagues (diary, Nicolas VII 137). That puts the fleet about 36°07′ N 6°33′ W if the bearing is by compass, 36°13′ N 6°43′ W if true. James has 8–9 leagues, Corbett about 20 miles, Desbrière about 25 (p. 177), Clowes about 28 (V 130). At 2 p.m. a shift of wind to WNW took the fleet aback. At 4 it wore and stood north on the larboard tack (James III 390; Desbrière has it tack at 4.40, p. 178). At 8 (8.40 by James) it wore to the south-west, and the Africa, missing the signal, held on north (Corbett 1910, p. 340 n.). At 4 a.m. on the 21st it wore again: N by E by the Victory's log, "to the N.E." by Nelson's diary (Corbett 1910, p. 340; James III 390). Desbrière's midnight estimate, about 20 miles south-west of Cádiz with the allies 5–6 miles off (p. 178), cannot be joined to the dawn position to better than 5–15 nm (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-32i). High for the sequence; low for the positions.

### Orders, Rosily and the sortie

- **The orders.** On 14 September Napoleon ordered Villeneuve to sail at the first chance with at least two and a half months' food, collect the Spanish squadron at Cartagena, land his troops at Naples, fight any enemy found in inferior force, and refit at Toulon (Corr. XI No. 9210). The orders reached Cádiz on 27 September by Decrès's report (Desbrière 1907, annexes 34) or on the 28th (Desbrière p. 136; James; Clowes). The troops embarked from 30 September. High; the day of arrival is disputed by one.
- **Rosily.** On 15 September Napoleon told Decrès that Villeneuve's "excessive pusillanimité" would keep him in port and that Rosily should replace him (No. 9220); Rosily's instructions are of 17 September (No. 9230; Desbrière's narrative says the 18th). Decrès's letter of 20 September superseding Villeneuve had not reached him by 18 October: neither Decrès nor Beurnonville had told him (Desbrière p. 142). High.
- **Rosily's journey.** Eleven days from Bayonne to Madrid, arriving in the night of 10–11 October (Beurnonville; Desbrière p. 128 has the 10th; Corbett's 12 October is the date of Rosily's own letter). With the posts broken down and robbers on the road beyond Córdoba he left Madrid only on the 14th, and reached Cádiz on 23–25 October: expected on the 23rd, his first letter from Cádiz dated the 25th (Desbrière 1907, annexes 68–73, 117–118; Fraser 1906, p. 379). Medium-high.
- **What Villeneuve knew.** On 15 October private letters said Rosily had passed Bayonne. On 18 October he heard "par le bruit public" that Rosily was in Madrid to take command, and wrote that he would sail next day if the wind allowed (Desbrière pp. 141–142; Corbett 1910, pp. 331–332). Fraser (p. 63) puts the Madrid news within hours of the Bayonne letters, about 15–16 October. Medium.
- **The council of war, 8 October.** Gravina had asked for it on the 6th, and Villeneuve called it on board the Bucentaure; fourteen officers signed, the Spanish brigadier Hore among them, whom Desbrière's narrative leaves out. It resolved to wait for weather that drove the British off, or for them to divide (Desbrière 1907, annexes 97–100). The argument that the allied crews were unpractised was Escaño's, Gravina's chief of staff, not Prigny's as Corbett (p. 324) has it. High.
- **Into the roadstead.** The fleet entered Cádiz on 20 August (Escaño's relation; his later letter says the 21st) and lay at first in the inner harbour, the Argonauta between the Puntal and Matagorda forts (Desbrière 1907, annexes 8). Within ten days Villeneuve brought his fastest ships down to just within the harbour mouth (Fraser 1906, p. 29). Corbett has the fleet still crowded up in the harbour about 29 September (p. 318), and on 1 October the Euryalus saw it at anchor in the outer harbour, apparently ready for sea (James III 380). From 9–10 October it moved down to the roadstead at the harbour mouth (Fraser 1906, p. 53; James III 385; Nelson's diary, 14 October: "Enemy at the harbour's mouth"), out of range of the city's guns and so moored with springs (Escaño, annexes 98), with two anchors, NW and SE (so the Montañés, annexes 372). Not every ship moved: the Fougueux lay between Puntal and Matagorda until she warped out on the 19th, and the innermost ships stayed at anchor that day (annexes 213, 216, 273). High.
- **The decision to sail.** Hard westerly gales blew from the 10th to the 17th; at midnight on the 17th the wind went east (James III 385). Coast signals reported four of the line gone from Gibraltar with a convoy, one there dismasted and another going in, so Nelson seemed six short (Desbrière p. 141). On the 18th Villeneuve ordered Magon out that night with four French and three Spanish of the line and the Hermione to catch the inshore frigates (his letter, annexes 101; Desbrière's narrative says three ships), then decided to sail next day. The signal to unmoor went up at 5.15 p.m., and the cables were hove short by 10.30 p.m. (Desbrière p. 160). High.
- **19 October.** The signal to weigh went up at first light (5–6 a.m. by the French; 7 by James), but the breeze died and only seven of the line got out: Algésiras, Achille, Argonaute, Neptune, Héros, Duguay-Trouin and the Spanish Bahama, with the frigates Rhin and Hermione, the Thémis following in the evening (Philibert's journal, Desbrière 1907, annexes 229–230; the Montañés, annexes 373). Other French counts: "six à sept" (the Rhin), "7 ou 8" (the Fougueux), eight French (the Observateur), a division of four at 4.30 p.m. (the Thémis) and nine (Prigny, writing from memory) (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-30c). British observers counted 9 sail at 4 p.m. and 12 at 6 p.m., frigates included, and that is where James's and Nicolas's "twelve ships" comes from (NRS II 146; `research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-30c). High.
- **The night of 19–20 October.** Magon stayed under sail off the port. After midnight the Bahama and Achille anchored north-west of the Cádiz light, near Rota; at daylight the Euryalus saw nine under sail and four at anchor (Desbrière p. 162; NRS II 147). Nelson was told of "near forty Sail" outside, an overcount (diary, Nicolas VII 136–137). High.
- **20 October.** The signal to weigh came at first light (6 a.m. by the Montañés and the Observateur, 6.30 by Desbrière). The whole fleet was out of the bay by about 8–8.30 a.m., the Rayo last at about 10, and the Euryalus counted 34 out at 8.50–9.10 (Desbrière 1907, annexes 158, 213, 216, 373; NRS II 147–148). Desbrière's time of about 11 (p. 163) and Corbett's "nearly noon" (p. 337) describe the fleet formed and standing off. High. The scene avoids "at noon".
- **At sea.** 33 of the line, 5 frigates and 2 brigs (Pickle's log at 3 p.m.; the Thémis). In rain and a fresh wind from SSW to SW they stood WNW, then wore onto the starboard tack in mid-afternoon: between 1 and 3 (Desbrière p. 164, adding that many reports say 3; the Sirius's master's log already has the van on the starboard tack after 1, NRS II 313), at 3.30 (the Sirius's log as Nicolas prints it, VII 134), or about 4 (Corbett; Prigny). James (III 388) keeps them on the larboard tack until they tacked toward the Strait at about 5. They formed in three columns (Prigny, Fougueux, Desbrière) or five (James; the Spanish official report, per Nicolas). About 6 p.m. the Achille saw 18 British sail to the WSW. About 8 p.m. Villeneuve ordered the line of battle on the starboard tack, Gravina leading with a light, and a night course "S.-O. 1/4 S.", that is SW by S by compass, about S by W true (Desbrière p. 164). Whether they wore in the night is disputed. James (III 388) has them wear and stand north-west after the Aigle signalled 18 British ships in line to the south at 7.30. Beatty (1825, p. 10) and Nicolas (VII 136) say they wore twice in the night. Desbrière (p. 178) says the two reports of their going about that reached Nelson were wrong, and the French reports keep them on the starboard tack (see "Which half of the code"). Corbett (p. 338) makes the mean course about south. At nightfall they were at most 8–10 miles south-west of Cádiz by the British frigates' logs (Desbrière p. 164): about 36°25′ N 6°27′ W, or 36°23.5′ N 6°23.5′ W if the bearing is by compass. High for the order and the sequence; the hour of the wear and the columns are disputed (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-31e, H2-31g); low for the night's track.
- **The combined fleet.** 18 French (4 of 80 guns, 14 of 74) and 15 Spanish: the Santísima Trinidad (130 guns in Nicolas VII 130, 136 in `research/dawn-setting.md`, 140 in Desbrière p. 134), Santa Ana and Príncipe de Asturias (112), Rayo (100), two of 80, eight of 74 and San Leandro (64). With them the French frigates Cornélie, Hermione, Hortense, Rhin and Thémis and the brigs Argus and Furet (Collingwood's dispatch; James III 386). High.
- **Left in port.** The bay had held 35 of the line: Villeneuve's 29 from Ferrol and 6 Spanish fitted out at Cádiz (Clowes V 121; Desbrière pp. 134–135). The Terrible 74 was disarmed, and the San Fulgencio 64 docked to survey her bottom; their crews manned the newly commissioned ships (Gravina to Decrès, 16 September, Desbrière 1907, annexes 84). The corvette Observateur stayed behind by order (Desbrière p. 174). British counts from the offing ran from 34 to 36. High.

### Signals in 1805, by day and by night

- **Flags.** The 1799 *Signal Book* numbered every signal and hoisted the number as numeral flags, one per digit (No. 370 took three), in bold designs of red, white, blue and yellow. Their values were changed in 1803–04, and over 80 signals were added by hand (Perrin 1922; FOTW, from a 1799 copy). High; the 1804 values were not transcribed.
- **Popham's telegraph.** A diagonal red and white flag announced a message, one hoist per word or phrase from Popham's numbered vocabulary (1803). Issued to ships of the line from 1803; in September 1805 the Admiralty decided every ship, cruisers included, should carry it (Corbett 1910, pp. 298–301). High. Which half was red is disputed: Popham says only "a diagonal red and white flag" (1803, p. 6), Perrin white and red, Wilson red over white and Kent white over red, and Barraclough found both in contemporary books (all via FOTW); invisible at chart scale.
- **Repeating.** A signal stayed flying until the ships in turn had repeated it to the commander-in-chief, and his answer went back down the line (Popham 1803, p. 6; *Signal-Book* 1796). This rule made a chain work. High.
- **Guns.** Guns drew the eye to a hoist or were part of a signal: two for a general chase. On 10 October Nelson ordered the ship bringing news of a sortie to fire a gun every three minutes, and rockets from the masthead (Nicolas VII 109–111). On 20 October the Euryalus's signals went with one, two, three or seven guns (NRS II 165, 167). High.
- **Distant signals.** When colours could not be read, shapes took their place: "a flag, a ball, and a pendant" at different mastheads (Cumby 1899, p. 719), as Nelson had ordered (Nicolas VII 70–71). Medium: one eyewitness, writing years later.
- **Reading range.** No period figure was found; colours were probably readable at about 4–11 nm, depending on haze. On 19 October the Mars's colours could not be read with a glass when she was hull-down, some 15–20 nm off (`research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R22). Low.
- **Night numerals.** The 1799 *Night Signals* made a number from guns (tens), lights in figures (one to four) and false fires or rockets (five); guns went five seconds apart, and a signal was answered with one light. Near an enemy, lantern figures replaced the guns only for the signals the book draws on p. 25: the fleet's manoeuvres, and No. 8 (a ship has passed an enemy without taking possession); the look-outs' reports of the enemy (Nos. 17, 21–29, 33, 35–37) kept their guns (`research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R15). High.
- **Watching at night.** Watching ships were to keep the enemy in sight, hide their lights from him, report by secret signals the Admiral set, and uncover signal lanterns all at once and keep them shown (*Night Signals* 1799, pp. 39–41). High.
- **Night lights.** Lanterns were plain; no coloured glass is recorded, and red and green sidelights date from 1848 and 1858. False fires, a blue flame in a wooden tube, burned for half a minute to several minutes (Smyth 1867, the only definition found); blue lights, or Bengal lights, are taken to burn alike, with a bluish-white glare (inference from period compositions). Rockets threw gold sparks, strong colours coming only after about 1811 (Physics World; RSC). Gun flashes were orange-white, the report seconds behind (Fraser 1906, p. 94), and the French saw *amorces*, brief priming flashes (Desbrière 1907, annexes p. 154). French observers remarked on the colour of the British fires (Cornélie, Argus; Lucas in Fraser 1906, p. 91); the likeliest reading is bluish blue lights against their own yellow lanterns and gold rockets (`research/scene-02/h3-signals.md`, 3.8). Medium.
- **Nelson's night code.** His memorandum of 20 October: if the enemy stood south or towards the Straits, "burn two blue lights together, every hour"; if west, "three guns, quick, every hour" (Nicolas VII 136). High.

### The signal chain, 19 to 21 October

Times are ship's times as logged, and the clocks disagreed: the Euryalus ran about 45 minutes ahead of the Victory on the 20th (Corbett 1910, p. 339 n. 2), and the Phoebe and the Mars logged the same hailing at 0.20 and 1.25. Compare times across ships with that in mind. Bearings in the logs are by compass; the variation was about 20–21° W, so true = compass − 20–21°.

- **The chain as set.** Nelson's orders, his station and the cordon of 19 October are under "Nelson off Cádiz" ("The station", "Blackwood's squadron", "The chain"): the fleet out of sight to the west, the frigates inshore, ships of the line between. Of the 27 of the line off Cádiz (`research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R7), 3–4 were links on the 19th and 23–24 lay with Nelson. High.
- **The relay of 19 October.** The inshore links are timed in the Euryalus's log and signal log; the middle links are not logged; the last link, the Mars to the Victory at 9.30, is in Nelson's diary (Hudson 1917, p. 22) and Beatty (1807, p. 7), and only how it was made, the distant signal, rests on one eyewitness (Cumby 1899, p. 719). The Agamemnon was in the cordon (Beatty 1807, p. 6), but nothing shows she passed the signal (`research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R5–R6). High for the logged links, medium for the rest.

- **How long.** About 2½ hours on the logged clocks: 7.00 by the Euryalus, 9.30 by the Victory. Blackwood wrote "within two hours"; Browne's 10 a.m. makes three; if the Euryalus's clock already ran 45 minutes ahead of the Victory's, as Corbett found on the 20th, it was about 3¼ (`research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R10). Medium.
- **Direction and length.** Nelson's original diary has Cádiz "ESE by compass", 16 leagues, at the chase (Hudson 1917, p. 23; Beatty 1807, app. p. 97): about due east true. The noon letter ("E.S.E., 16 Leagues", Nicolas VII 132), Blackwood (16 leagues) and Browne ("east, 16 leagues", NRS II 194) agree; Corbett and Mahan round the distance to fifty miles, and Robinson's "twenty leagues" (1858) is a late memory. The Victory's noon position, 36°36′ N 7°30′ W, puts it 19 leagues off (Corbett 1910, p. 333 n. 1). So the chain ran about due west (true) of Cádiz, 48–58 nm long. The "E.N.E." in Nicolas VII 133 is a copying slip, and the "W.S.W." of James (III 389) and Nicolas's narrative probably derives from it. High.
- **Weather and light.** Off Cádiz, clear with light airs, calm by noon (NRS II 145–146); at the fleet, an easterly wind, then very light airs from S to SSW at the chase (Hudson 1917, p. 22; Beatty 1807, p. 7). Sunrise about 6.26; the sun stood about 6° up at 107° true at 7.00 and 32° up at 135° at 9.30 (computed, local apparent time). High.
- **19 and 20 October.** That night rockets and blue lights went along the chain as the fleet stood for the Strait, six fast ships ahead carrying lights (NRS II 146–147, 302; Hudson 1917, p. 23). On the 20th, in rain, signals went with minute guns. At 5 p.m. by the Victory (5.40 by the Euryalus, NRS II 149; "a little before sunset" in Beatty 1807, p. 9) Nelson signalled that he relied on Blackwood to keep sight of the enemy, and the Naiad reported 31 sail NNE (at 5 in the original diary, Hudson 1917, p. 24, and in Beatty's appendix; "shortly afterwards" in James; 6 p.m. in Nicolas VII 137, from a facsimile). High.
- **The night chain, 20–21 October.** Nelson's memorandum set it: Blackwood with two frigates in sight of the enemy, two more between him and the Defence, the Colossus between the Defence and the Mars, and the Mars in touch with the Victory (Nicolas VII 136). The logs put the Euryalus and Sirius by the enemy, the Phoebe and Naiad between. High.
- **Its length.** The Euryalus kept 2–3 nm on the enemy's weather beam. At midnight the enemy's body lay about 3 miles SE by S of her (NRS II 149; SE by E in Nicolas VII 134 n.) and the British fleet's light 5 or 6 miles to the south and west, so the night chain was under 10 nm long, not 50. High for the stations, low for the distances.
- **What the logs record.** From about 6 p.m. the frigates burned blue lights and false fires and fired rockets and guns all night, and the fleet answered; the Mars saw blue lights NE at 2 a.m. and ENE at 3.20 (NRS II 149, 245, 306, 313). Some links worked by hailing: the Phoebe spoke the Mars at 0.20 (1.25 by the Mars's clock), the Naiad the Colossus at 1.30 (2.00) (NRS II 245, 265, 303, 306). Nelson wrote that the frigates and look-out ships told him by signals which tack they were upon (Hudson 1917, p. 24), and Beatty that counted guns with false fires and blue lights reported each change and were repeated from the Euryalus to the Victory (1807, pp. 9–10). High.
- **Which half of the code.** The Combined Fleet stood S to SSW on the starboard tack most of the night (Desbrière 1907, annexes pp. 159, 178; Fraser 1906, pp. 91–92): the case for paired blue lights. James (III 388) has it standing north-west after 7.30 p.m., against the French reports, and Beatty (1807, p. 10) has the enemy wearing twice in the night, which the French reports cited here do not mention (see "At sea"). No log says whether the frigates also used the 1799 book's tack signals. Medium.
- **The fleet's wears.** At 8 p.m. (8.40 in James III 390) the fleet wore to the SW by Night Signal No. 31, the Victory burning blue lights to show her position (Corbett 1910, p. 340 n. 2). At 4 a.m. it wore again, to the NE by Nelson's diary (Hudson 1917, p. 24) but N by E by the Victory's log (Corbett 1910, p. 340), the wind WNW (Mars at 4, Dreadnought and Conqueror 4.20, Phoebe 4.30 by their clocks). Beatty's narrative (p. 11) has 2 a.m., against his own appendix and the logs. By the book each ship showed three lights while wearing; no log records it. High for the wears, medium for the lights.
- **Dark and light.** Moonless, cloudy and very dark. The watching ships hid their lights (an officer of the Defence, Fraser 1906, p. 95), and the British side showed little but the Admiral's lights, answers and blue lights. The Combined Fleet showed stern and top lights along a line some six miles long, with rockets, flares and guns of its own (stern and top lights and rockets: Prigny, Desbrière 1907, annexes p. 139; flares: Lucas, Fraser 1906, p. 92; six miles: Fraser 1906, p. 95). High.

### How the chart shows the signals

**By day: the relay of 19 October (beat 4).**

- **Geometry.** A loose line about due west (true) of Cádiz, 48–58 nm long and dipping south at the Defence: the frigates within 17 nm of San Sebastián, the Defence about 27 nm out, the Mars and Colossus 15 nm short of the fleet (the Defence–Mars gap about 15.5 nm, inside masthead range; `research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R31), the Victory with 23–24 of the line near 36°34′–35′ N 7°24′ W (table below). Never south-west. Medium for the line, low for single ships.
- **One link.** Two or three flag dabs at the masthead, hand-tinted red, white, blue and yellow, and a counted pair or three of white gun puffs at the side; after a pause the next ship does the same. Hoists stay up until the Victory answers, so lit links accumulate westward, then come down as the answer runs back (the rules; not logged that day). Medium.
- **The ends.** At first light, the Sirius's seven telegraph hoists under the red and white flag. From 7.20 the Phoebe makes sail (to the northward by the Sirius's log) and by 8 lies due west (true) of the Euryalus, a puff every three minutes of story time; the Naiad joins between after 8.10. At the Mars, coloured flags the fleet cannot read, then three dark shapes at three mastheads. At the Victory, one flag, the SE compass flag and two puffs, and the fleet makes sail for the Strait, first about E by N, then about E by S true. Medium.

**By night: 20–21 October (beat 5).**

- **Geometry.** A short chain between the closing fleets, running SW from the enemy's western flank: the Euryalus about 3 nm NW to W of the enemy's body, the Sirius 4 nm WSW of it, then the Phoebe, Naiad, Defence, Colossus and Mars, and the Victory 5–6 nm SW of the Euryalus (table below). Place them on the enemy's track, itself uncertain (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-32i). Low.
- **Paired blue lights, hourly.** The leading motif: two small, intense bluish-white flares together at a frigate, lighting her sails and their own smoke for half a minute or more; minutes later the same at the next link, down to the Victory, answered by one light. Medium.
- **Between them.** Gold rockets from the masthead, a streak of a few seconds and a small burst; counted orange-white gun flashes, each leaving a lit puff; brief priming flashes. Busiest about 6 p.m., 9 p.m. and midnight. Medium.
- **The fleet's wears.** At 8 p.m. the Victory's No. 31 (three guns and two lights, or near an enemy a square of four lanterns) and her blue lights; then two lights at each ship's ensign staff and one at her bowsprit end while the fleet wears in succession from the rear. At 4 a.m. the same (the Africa saw gun flashes WSW at 4, NRS II 297); no log says whether the Victory burned blue lights then. Medium.
- **Lanterns and the dark.** The British side: a few warm points, the Admiral's lights and answering lanterns shown all at once and held; the watching ships dark. The enemy: a string of warm points about 6 nm long, with its own gold rockets and gun flashes. No moon until a thin crescent rises at about 5.20 a.m. High.
- **Range.** Lanterns carry about 1–3 nm, blue lights about 5–14 nm, rockets and gun flashes further (estimates, `research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R21). The night chain could see itself end to end; British hulls stayed unseen. Low.
- **Date line.** "20 October 1805" until midnight and "21 October 1805" after it, since the hailings (0.20–2 a.m.), the Mars's blue lights (2 and 3.20) and the 4 a.m. wear fall on the 21st; or "20–21 October 1805" for the whole night beat. High.

**What would be wrong.**

- **Later inventions.** Semaphore arms or shutter telegraphs on ships; flashing lamps (adopted in 1867), beams or searchlights; red or green lights or lanterns; International Code flags. High. Vivid red or green fireworks are wrong too: before about 1811 firework colours were weak reds and yellows at most (Physics World; RSC). Medium.
- **Wrong in kind.** A glowing line or travelling light between the ships (also on the avoid-list); an instant relay; a chain of frigates only; a day chain drawn south-west; lantern figures as the frigates' reports; flags at night; a brightly lit British fleet; moonlight. High.

### Positions on the chart

- **Method.** Logged positions are worked by plane sailing from San Sebastián light (36°31.7′ N 6°19.0′ W), compass bearings corrected for 21° W variation. Every point was checked against the chart's own land (Natural Earth 10 m), its 15″ ETOPO tile and modern depths (EMODnet, from the Spanish hydrographic model of the bay; open water today, not 1805 soundings). In the bay they were also checked against Faden's *Plan of the Harbour of Cadiz* (London, 22 September 1797), from the Spanish chart of Vicente Tofiño (the sheet gives no date; his *Atlas Marítimo de España* appeared in 1789), georeferenced on eight forts and towers (Santa Catalina at El Puerto, Puntal, Matagorda, San Sebastián, Torregorda, Puertas de Tierra, Fort San Luis, Rota) with a residual of 0.18 nm rms over the eight points and about 0.1 nm at the forts round the bay. Medium.
- **The bay in 1797.** The plan sounds the entrance between Las Puercas (36°32.9′ N 6°18.2′ W) and the shoals of El Diamante (about 36°33.6′–34.6′ N 6°17.4′–18.0′ W) at 6–7 (fathoms or Spanish brazas, about 10–13 m), where French ships anchored after the battle "entre les Porques et le Diamant, par les 7 brasses d'eau", and the outer bay north-east of it at 5¾–7. It draws a line of seven Spanish ships of 1797 in the outer bay, running about north–south (true) along about 6°15.8′ W from 36°33.5′ N to 36°31.8′ N toward the narrows. Two more stand in the narrows between the Puntal and Matagorda forts, and the rest of that fleet beyond them in the inner bay toward Fort Louis and La Cantera. Its north points to the left of the sheet, about 30° above the horizontal. It is a guide to the water, not to the moorings of 1805. The outer bay is a few metres shallower today (about 6–12 m in the glyphs' box), and the narrows are bridged. Medium.

### Assumptions and licences

1. **The British heading at the handoff.** The Victory's log has the fleet steering N by E by compass, about 350° true, at first light and at sunrise; it bore up after signal No. 76 at 6.50 by her clock to ENE, about 047° true, and steered E by N (058°) from 8 a.m. The chart draws the British on 047°, the course some 20–30 minutes after scene 1's sunrise, for continuity with scene 1's Victory sailing east (scene 1 keeps a stylised west-to-east view; `docs/decisions.md`, "The dawn fleets are spread out").
2. **The dawn spacing.** The lab opens the spacing within each fleet a few times over the true distances, so that the ships read one by one from the Gulf: the combined line runs about 16 nm, its northern glyph 4.8 nm off the chart's coast, and the British spread over about 11 nm. The sources give a line nearly five miles long, 7–9 nm or more off the coast, and British ships 3–4 nm apart (see "Where this differs from scene 1"). The line's centre stays where the sources put it. Drawn to the true spacing, each fleet would show as one ship at the Gulf key; whether to tighten it is the owner's call.
3. **Glyphs and counts.** Ships are glyphs drawn far larger than true scale, as a chart convention, and each is kept to the open water beyond it. The lab shows the anchorage of 10–19 October and the dawn of 21 October at once, with six ships for each; the full scene gives each moment its beat and its count.
4. **The narrows.** Natural Earth has no Matagorda shore, so the chart shows open water east of the isthmus of Cádiz and no narrows. The narrows ship stands where today's channel is (see "Outer and inner ships").
5. **Boulogne's flotilla in the road.** On the chart's coast the Liane basin is land, so the visible flotilla lies in the road outside the pier, as the lines of 1804 did; its layout in August 1805 is not documented.
6. **The night tracks.** No source joins the night positions of 20–21 October to the dawn positions to better than 5–15 nm, so the night tracks are drawn loosely and end on the dawn positions; the combined fleet's glyphs face its course, about 193° true, not the drawn segment.
7. **Estimated points.** Positions no source fixes are estimates inside the sources' range: the Toulon berth, the Atlas's longitudes for 4–8 April, the 4 p.m. point off Conil, the anchorage of 9 April, the Finisterre smoke, the bend of 15 August, the moorings of 10–19 October and every position of the night of 20–21 October.
8. **Signals by the book.** The day relay holds each hoist until the Victory's answer runs back, and the night wears show two lights at the ensign staff and one at the bowsprit end. Both follow the 1799 rule books; no log of those days records them.
9. **The Mars's distant signal.** The relay ends at the Mars with three dark shapes at three mastheads, on the word of one eyewitness writing years later (Cumby 1899, p. 719).

### The names on the chart

The brief asks for the names used in 1805. The chart sets the English forms (the site is in English), settled in `research/scene-02/a-h4.md` from `h4-names.md` and its check (`v-h4.md`), and upheld by the check of that ruling (`v-a-h4.md`) and the rulings on it (`a-v-a-h4.md`). They rest on the charts the adjudicators read: Faden's chart of Nelson's track (12 August 1807) first, then Heather's *Coast of Portugal* (1800), Laurie & Whittle's *Atlantic Ocean* (1794) and their *Spain and Portugal* (plate of 12 May 1794, issued in their atlas of 1804), Cary's maps of 1801–1805 and Arrowsmith's of 1802–1803; and on usage in Nelson's letters, the logs, the *Naval Chronicle*, Norie, Malham and Brookes.

- **The rule.** Where the English charts of 1800–1807 agree, the chart letters their form even if it looks odd today (Barbadoes, Bourdeaux, Guadaloupe). Where they disagree, it drops any form a visitor would misread (Martinico, Guadalquiver, Cape Verd). The naval and printed usage of 1805 then decides among the rest (Atlantic Ocean, Canary Islands, Carthagena, Barbary), and Faden's chart of 1807 is the last tie-break. A region no chart of the period letters in its body takes the name of the period's prose and chart titles (West Indies; `research/scene-02/a-v-a-h4.md`, 2). High for the forms, medium for the choices between equal forms.
- **Type.** Ordinary characters only: the engravers' raised letters become the printed abbreviation with a stop (Sᵗ as "St.", Pᵗ as "Pt."), as printed English of the period had it ("Cape St. Vincent" in Malham, Norie and the *Naval Chronicle*). No accents (Cadiz), as on every English chart of the period. Only oceans and seas are in spaced capitals (the brief); period plates also set countries, capitals and island groups in capitals. Double names take a small "or", as the plates set it. High.
- **Capes and the article.** Capes take "C." with a stop, the usual small-scale form (Faden 1807 for every European cape); the period also spelled "Cape" on large-scale plates (Heather 1800 CAPE Sᵀ. VINCENT) and for some capes on small-scale maps (Faden's Cape Geer, Cape Nun and Cape Bojador; Arrowsmith 1802; Laurie & Whittle's *Spain and Portugal*). Leaving out "The" ("Atlantic Ocean", "English Channel") is a choice: Faden letters the ocean and the Channel without it, as Cary 1803 and Arrowsmith 1803 letter the ocean, but Faden has THE NORTH SEA, Heather 1800 THE ATLANTIC OCEAN and THE MEDITERRANEAN SEA, Cary 1804 THE ATLANTIC OCEAN and Laurie & Whittle 1794 THE CHANNEL. High.
- **Four forms changed when the names were settled.** "The Channel" to English Channel (Faden 1807; Heather's chart titles; Norie, Malham); "C. St Vincent" to C. St. Vincent; "Western Islands" to Azores or Western Islands (Laurie & Whittle 1794; Heather c. 1806), so that a visitor finds the Azores; "Martinico" to Martinique. The other thirty names on the chart stand, and the check of the ruling refuted none of the 34. West Indies, a region rather than a water, is set in roman like a land and fades out close up. High; medium for Martinique.

The chase of Collingwood on 20 August was led by Magon (Desbrière) or Gravina (Corbett); Collingwood was back off the port that evening with four (Desbrière) or before daylight on the 21st (James). The 10 a.m. position that day: about 36°07′ N 6°32′ W if "Cádiz NE nine leagues" is by compass, 36°13′ N 6°42′ W if true. The arrival: 20 August (James, Desbrière, Corbett, Escaño's relation, Collingwood's "yesterday"), 21 August (the Précis; Escaño's later letter to MacDonnell) or 17 August (Troude, impossible). Desbrière's staff map "Situation au 15 août" (leaf n126) prints "19 Août" beside the Spanish ships at Cádiz; read as the arrival, it is a day before his own text (p. 107) and James (III 375). The count in port: 35 of the line (James) against Collingwood's "36 sail of men-of-war", which counts frigates.
- **The British off Cádiz, August to October.** At the end of August: 26 (James III 379; Allemand's intelligence of 11 September, Desbrière p. 108), 29 (Desbrière's own lists without his double count, with the Dragon and Goliath "ralliés en route", p. 109; Hallowell's letter of 17 August, p. 103 n.; Corbett p. 247, Cornwallis's orders) or 32 (Desbrière's printed total); the Dragon was off Vigo on 10 October (Desbrière p. 108 n.). Ajax and Thunderer joined Nelson off the Lizard (Nelson to Marsden) or off Plymouth (James, Beatty). On his arrival on 28 September: 29 under his command (the Victory's log and signal log; Nelson to Acton; Mahan; the ship-by-ship tally) or 27 (James III 379: 22 offshore and 5 inshore; Beatty p. 2: 27, with five or six inshore); all 29 were in company only if the Leviathan had not yet gone to Gibraltar. Louis's inshore squadron then: 6 (the Victory's log; Nelson to Acton), 5 (James III 379; Corbett) or "five or six" (Beatty, p. 2 in the 1807 and 1825 editions). His detachment of 2 October: Nelson wrote of "six Sail of the Line" (to Marsden, VII 63); five are named, and no source names a sixth. He sailed on the evening of 2 October (Austen; Nelson; the log's nautical "October 3rd") or on 3 October (Desbrière p. 148), and rejoined on 4 October (Beatty) or the morning of the 5th (Austen). The October peak: 28 by the ship-by-ship tally, or 29 (James III 383; Clowes V 128), which needs the Africa to have joined before the Prince of Wales sailed on the evening of 13 October; the Africa joined on the 13th or the 14th. The Prince of Wales: 98 guns (James, Clowes) or 90 (Desbrière, Mahan). The fleet's station: 16–18 leagues west of Cádiz (orders of 4 October), "15 lgs. to twenty" (diary, 14 October) or 15–18 (a letter to Ball). The link squadron's distance east of the fleet: "from three to four leagues" in the order to Duff, four leagues in the letter to Blackwood (`research/scene-02/a-h2a.md`, R1). On 18 October coast signals told Villeneuve that four of the line had left Gibraltar with a convoy (Desbrière p. 141); Louis had five (Austen).
- **Boulogne.** The flotilla's counts are right for their own dates: 1,301 craft built in 1803–04 (Desbrière), 2,054 in the spring of 1805 (the embarkation return), 2,293 in seven ports and 1,104 at Boulogne on 20 July 1805 (James; Clowes). Its layout in August 1805 is not documented: the lines in the road are of 1804 (James III 217, 227, 232) and of Napoleon's intentions after he left (III 332). Wimereux and Ambleteuse: both "new ports" Napoleon had built (Corr. IX No. 7832, 2 July 1804), or Wimereux made a port and Ambleteuse's existing harbour deepened and enlarged (James III 217). The left camp at Outreau, and the right camp's extent toward Terlincthun, rest on an uncited Wikipedia page. The two embarkations rest on James alone (III 326). The modern figures of about 60,000 men at Boulogne and over 175,000 in the camps have no source.
- **The turn east and Ulm.** When Napoleon knew Villeneuve was in Cádiz: by 31 August at the latest (the postscript of 13 fructidor, Corr. XI 157), 1 September (Corbett p. 278, the same postscript), 1–2 September (Desbrière pp. 115–116, the full dispatches), or about four days after the 22nd (James III 332, with the Daru story, which does not fit the dates). The force to stay at Boulogne: an army of 60,000–80,000 men on the heights and 500 craft able to carry 40,000–50,000 (No. 9209, art. 4; dated 13 September by the editors), 80,000 or 100,000 men encamped as a pretext (art. 5), 500 craft and 80,000 men (Desbrière 1907, p. 120), or 30,000–40,000 men encamped on the heights with the gun-vessels in the basin and road (James III 332). The second divisions marched on 30 August (Berthier's order) or 29 August ("demain" in Napoleon's letter of the 28th); Napoleon left Boulogne on 2 September (Corr. XI No. 9177; Desbrière) or 4 September (James III 332). The Rhine was crossed on 25–30 September by corps (Alombert and Colin) or on 25–26 September in Napoleon's 1st Bulletin. Napoleon was at Donauwörth on 7 October in the afternoon (Schuermans) or the morning (Maude). The coalition's dates rest on Maude alone: Russia approached Austria in October 1803, the courier with Austria's adherence left Vienna on 7 July 1805 and the two signed a treaty on 9 August (pp. 76, 90), while on 23 August Napoleon still expected Austria's act to be signed "cet hiver" (Corr. XI No. 9117); the treaties and Britain's part were not researched. Ulm: the capitulation signed at noon (Alombert and Colin) or with Berthier coming at 2 p.m. (the Austrian eyewitness); about 23,000 (the eyewitness), 25,365 (Alombert and Colin; Maude), 27,000 (7th Bulletin) or 30,000 (8th Bulletin) laid down their arms, Napoleon's own figures on the 17th having been 15,000 and 20,000; the muskets 13,600 on the page image, 43,600 in the OCR. Some modern summaries give 19 October for Ulm, without period support.
- **Cádiz: orders, Rosily and the fleet in port.** The orders of 14 September reached Cádiz on 27 September (Decrès's report) or the 28th (Desbrière p. 136; James; Clowes; Corbett, as Nelson appeared off Cape St Vincent). Rosily's instructions: 17 September (No. 9230) or the 18th (Desbrière's narrative). Rosily reached Madrid in the night of 10–11 October (Beurnonville), on the 10th (Desbrière p. 128) or the 12th (Corbett, the date of Rosily's own letter), and Cádiz on 23 October (expected, Beurnonville) or by the 25th (his first letter from Cádiz); the date line shows 23–25 October 1805. Villeneuve heard Rosily was in Madrid on 18 October (his own letter; Desbrière; Corbett) or about 15–16 October (Fraser p. 63). Magon's planned night sortie of 18 October: four French and three Spanish of the line and the Hermione (Villeneuve's letter, Desbrière annexes 101; Corbett) or three ships (Desbrière p. 141). Where the fleet lay in late September and early October: in the inner harbour, the Argonauta between the Puntal and Matagorda forts (Escaño); its fastest ships brought down to just within the harbour mouth within ten days of arrival (Fraser p. 29); "crowded up in the harbour" about 29 September (Corbett p. 318); at anchor in the outer harbour, apparently ready for sea, on 1 October (the Euryalus, James III 380). British counts of the fleet in port from the offing ran from 34 to 36 (Nelson 35 or 36 on 28 September; the Euryalus and Blackwood 34).
- **The anchorage, 10–19 October.** No source fixes the ships' places. The layout rests on Escaño ("fuera del tiro de la plaza"), the French "entre les Porques et le Diamant", Nelson's "harbour's mouth", the modern depths of `research/scene-02/a-h2c.md` and Faden's plan of 1797, which shows the Spanish moorings of 1797, not those of 1805; the sheet does not say whether its soundings are fathoms or Spanish brazas. The number of ships in the narrows is not recorded: the Fougueux was there by her own report, and the Thémis mentions "the innermost ships" still at anchor on the 19th; Faden's plan draws two Spanish ships between the forts in 1797. The outermost ships are drawn 0.25 nm north-east of the point `a-h2c.md` (H2-36d) gives them, which lies on the modern buoy line; its midpoint between the forts falls on Natural Earth's coastline, which has no Matagorda shore, so the chart draws no narrows.
- **The sortie.** The signal to weigh on the 19th went up at first light, 5–6 a.m. (the French), or at 7 (James). Ships of the line out that day: seven (Philibert, the Montañés, Desbrière) against "six à sept" (the Rhin), "7 ou 8" (the Fougueux), eight French (the Observateur), four at 4.30 p.m. (the Thémis), nine (Prigny, from memory) and twelve (James and Nicolas, from the Euryalus's count of all sail). The Algésiras at 4 p.m.: 36°33.0′ N 6°22.0′ W from Philibert's bearings; the 36°33.8′ N of `research/scene-02/a-h2c.md` (H2-38b) is a computing slip. On the 20th the signal to weigh came at 6 a.m. (the Montañés, the Observateur) or 6.30 (Desbrière), and the whole fleet was out at about 8–8.30 a.m. (the Observateur, the Montañés, the Fougueux), about 11 (Desbrière p. 163) or nearly noon (Corbett p. 337), the last two describing the fleet formed. The afternoon wear: between 1 and 3 p.m. (Desbrière p. 164, many reports saying 3), the van on the starboard tack after 1 (the Sirius's master's log, NRS II 313), 3.30 (the Sirius's log as Nicolas prints it, VII 134), about 4 (Corbett; Prigny), or still on the larboard tack until a tack toward the Strait at about 5 (James III 388); in three columns (Prigny, the Fougueux, Desbrière) or five (James; the Spanish official report, per Nicolas). The Santísima Trinidad: 130 guns (Nicolas VII 130), 136 (`research/dawn-setting.md`) or 140 (Desbrière p. 134). On the 20th Nelson was told of "near forty Sail" outside, an overcount.
- **The combined fleet's night, 20–21 October.** It wore and stood north-west after the Aigle signalled 18 British ships in line to the south at 7.30 p.m. (James III 388); it wore twice (Beatty, p. 10; Nicolas VII 136, after Beatty); or the two reports of its going about that reached Nelson were wrong and it stayed on the starboard tack, standing S to SSW (Desbrière p. 178; the French reports); Corbett makes the mean course about south (p. 338). At nightfall it was at most 8–10 miles south-west of Cádiz (Desbrière p. 164): about 36°25′ N 6°27′ W if south-west is true, 36°23.5′ N 6°23.5′ W if by compass. Its midnight position is interpolated, and with the dawn position implies a track of about 147° true against the signalled 193°.
- **The British, 19–21 October.** At noon on the 20th Cádiz bore NE 9 leagues (Nelson's diary): 36°07′ N 6°33′ W if by compass, 36°13′ N 6°43′ W if true; James has 8–9 leagues, Corbett about 20 miles, Desbrière about 25 (p. 177), Clowes about 28 (V 130). The turn north at 4 p.m. was a wear (James III 390) or a tack at 4.40 (Desbrière p. 178). The evening wear to the south-west came at 8 (Corbett p. 340 n.) or 8.40 (James III 390). The morning wear came at 4 a.m. (the diary; the logs) or 2 a.m. (Beatty's narrative, p. 11, against his own appendix); its course was N by E (the Victory's log) or "to the N.E." (Nelson's diary). Desbrière's midnight estimate, about 20 miles south-west of Cádiz (p. 178), cannot be joined to the dawn positions to better than 5–15 nm (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-32i), and no position of the night is better fixed than that.
- **The relay of 19 October.** Which ships of the line passed No. 370: the Defence and Colossus are inferred; the Agamemnon was stationed in the cordon (Beatty, p. 6; James III 389), but nothing shows she relayed it (`research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R5–R6). How long it took: about 2½ hours on the logged clocks, "within two hours" (Blackwood), about 3 by Browne's 10 a.m., or about 3¼ with Corbett's 45-minute clock offset (R10). The arrival at the Victory: 9.30 (the diary; Beatty), 10 (Browne) or "about nine" (Walker). The Euryalus's repeat to the Phoebe: 7.20 by the logs; Corbett's "in ten minutes" (7.10) has no support. The Phoebe's course: "to the northward" by the Sirius's log at 7, against her bearing WNW of the Euryalus at 8. No. 370's wording: "enemy's ships are coming out of port" (the NRS editor, from the 1799 book), "or getting under weigh" (Cumby) or "or getting under sail" (Corbett). Where the Victory lay at 9.30: about 36°33′ N 7°18′ W by Nelson's 16 leagues ESE by compass, against the noon log position, 36°36′ N 7°30′ W, 19 leagues off, although the fleet had chased east in light airs since 9.30. The distance: 16 leagues (the diary, the noon letter, Blackwood, Browne), "fifty miles" (Corbett and Mahan, rounding) or "twenty leagues" (Robinson, 1858, a late memory); the bearing ESE (the diary's manuscript; the noon letter) against "E.N.E." (a copying slip in Nicolas VII 133) and the "W.S.W." of James (III 389) and Nicolas's narrative. Where the Defence lay at 8 a.m.: about 10 nm south of Cádiz's parallel by the Euryalus's masthead bearing ("west"; `research/scene-02/a-h2b.md`, H2-37f), or on it by Nelson's "in the latitude" order and her usual station (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-40c). The Colossus lay with the Mars by the diary of 14 October; only the memorandum of 20 October puts her between the Defence and the Mars, for the night chain. The Sirius's first-light position is not logged: the nearest entry is the 4 a.m. bearing, "Cadiz NE by E, 4 miles" (NRS II 312), and she telegraphed the topsail yards at 6.04 by the Euryalus's signal log. The Mars's distant signal of a flag, a ball and a pendant rests on one eyewitness writing years later (Cumby); no printed description of the Distant Signals of 1805 was found. The second signal down the chain, that the enemy's fleet was at sea: about 2 p.m. (Beatty), 3 p.m. (the diary; Nicolas VII 131) or "by three" (Corbett). If the Victory's clock ran about 45 minutes slow on the 19th (an inference, R10), her 9.30 was about 10.15 local time, with the sun about 37° up rather than 32°.
- **The signals of the night of 20–21 October.** The 5 p.m. signal: 5 by the Victory, 5.40 by the Euryalus (NRS II 149), "a little before sunset" (Beatty 1807, p. 9); the Naiad's report of 31 sail at 5 (the original diary; Beatty's appendix), "shortly afterwards" (James) or 6 p.m. (Nicolas VII 137, from a facsimile). Whether the frigates also used the 1799 book's tack signals (Nos. 28–29) besides Nelson's code, and whether the Sirius's three guns at 9.35 p.m. meant "standing west" (`research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R18); the six guns she heard then may be No. 31 repeated by two flagships, an inference only (R16, R25). The Euryalus's midnight bearing of the enemy: SE by S (NRS II 149) or SE by E (Nicolas VII 134 n.). The Sirius was 4 miles off the enemy at midnight (NRS II 313) or 6 at 11.30 (Nicolas VII 134 n.). Whether the Victory burned blue lights at the 4 a.m. wear: no log says, and Corbett calls the blue lights Nelson's custom; the Africa saw gun flashes WSW at 4 (NRS II 297).
- **Signals: colours, books and ranges.** The 1804 values of the numeral flags (Perrin's Plate XIII) were not transcribed. Which half of Popham's telegraph flag was red: Popham names only "a diagonal red and white flag" (1803, p. 6); Perrin has white and red, Wilson red over white, Kent white over red, and Barraclough found both in contemporary books (all via FOTW). The hoists held along the chain until the Victory's answer ran back, the wear lights (two at the ensign staff, one at the bowsprit end) and the form of No. 31 (three guns and two lights, or a square of four lanterns) come from the 1799 rule books; no log of those days records them (R16, R25). All ranges are estimates: flag colours readable at about 4–11 nm, lanterns at 1–3 nm, blue lights at 5–14 nm (R21–R22). The blue light's burn is inferred from the false fire's, the only one defined (Smyth 1867). The colour of the British fires, which French observers remarked on (the Cornélie, the Argus; Lucas), is read as bluish blue lights against their own yellow lanterns and gold rockets.
- **Dawn, 21 October.** The British lay west, slightly south of west, of Cape Trafalgar, 20–21 nm off (Collingwood's "E by S about seven leagues"; the logs; `research/dawn-setting.md`, 1.2), or about 21 miles north-west of it (Desbrière p. 179). The combined fleet lay about 8–12 nm off the coast by the British bearings, its northern end about 7–9 nm, or 18–24 nm off by the French (Dumanoir; the Scipion's "8 à 9 lieues" at noon); Villeneuve's own reckoning (Cape Trafalgar ESE about four leagues; Fraser 1906, pp. 110–111) puts his centre about 2½ nm north-west of the scene 1 position. At first light and at sunrise the British steered N by E by compass, about 350° true (the Victory's log); they bore up after signal No. 76 at 6.50 by her clock (about 06:40–07:00 in scene 1's notes) to ENE, about 047° true, then E by N (058°) from 8 a.m. The Africa's position, Cape Trafalgar SE 8–9 leagues at 6 a.m., about 36°21′ N 6°30′ W, is low.
- **The chart's dawn licences (the owner's call).** The combined line: about 16 nm long on the chart (36°00′–36°16′ N), its northern glyph 4.8 nm off the chart's coast near Conil; in the sources nearly 5 miles long (James III 389), its northern end about 7–9 nm off the coast by the British reckoning and 18–24 nm by the French (Dumanoir, the Scipion). The British spread: about 11 nm north to south on the chart, the southern pair 6–8 nm from the Victory; in the logs single ships 3–4 nm from her at 4 a.m. (the Mars, the Ajax), and nothing on how far apart the two groups were. The British heading: 047° on the chart, against N by E (about 350° true) at first light and sunrise in the Victory's log. See "Assumptions and licences".
- **Not consulted first-hand.** Tofiño's own plan of Cádiz (only Faden's English copy of 1797); the Victory's master's log for 1–21 October (ADM 52); the Spanish official report in full; the treaties of the Third Coalition and Britain's part in it.
- **The 1805 names.** Each disputed form, with who used it, is under "The names on the chart".
