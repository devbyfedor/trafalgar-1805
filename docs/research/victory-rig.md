# HMS Victory, 21 October 1805: masts, spars, sails, rigging and flags

Research note for scene 1 ("Dawn") of *Trafalgar 1805*. It covers starboard-side elevation, full sail, at first light. Every number is in feet (ft) and metres (m). Each fact carries a source and a confidence rating:

- **high**: primary source or museum artefact.
- **medium**: good secondary source, or a primary rule applied to Victory.
- **low**: my own estimate, measurement or inference.

Doubts are collected in §10, and all URLs are in §11.

---

## 0. Key findings

1. **Spars.** The best public-domain spar list is David Steel's 1794 Royal Navy table for a **100-gun ship of 2,164 tons**. Victory was 2,142 tons. Main mast 117 ft, topmast 70 ft, topgallant 35 ft; main yard 102 ft 4 in; bowsprit 74 ft (§1.1, §4).
   - It is **confirmed against a surviving Victory artefact**. Her 1803 fore topsail, now in the National Museum of the Royal Navy (NMRN), measures 80 ft at the foot, 54 ft at the head and 54 ft deep. That fits Steel's fore yard (89 ft 1 in), fore topsail yard (64 ft 6 in) and fore topmast (62 ft 10 in) to within a foot or two.
   - McKay's Victory-specific table, from *The 100-Gun Ship Victory*, is not online. Treat Steel as a close proxy, not a certainty.
2. **The main truck is about 195–200 ft (59.5–61 m) above the load waterline** by reconstruction. The commonly quoted 205 ft (62.5 m) is about 3–5 % generous. Recommended drawing value: **about 60–61 m**. Ratios of truck heights: fore ≈ 0.90 × main, mizzen ≈ 0.83 × main (§1.3).
3. **Sails at dawn: primary evidence.** Victory's master's log for 21 October (Thomas Atkinson): at 6 the enemy was seen E by S, 10 or 12 miles off, and she bore up to the eastward; then "Out all reefs [of] topsails. Set steering sails and royals." She cleared for quarters, and at 8 the log has light breezes and cloud.

   Wind NW to NW by W; course ENE, then E by N; speed 2–3 knots.
   - So the brief's royals are right.
   - The brief **omits the studding sails ("steering sails")**, which were set at dawn. They went out on the **weather (larboard) side**, the far side from the viewer (§5.1).
4. **Topsails had 4 reef bands in 1805.** Victory's own log, 24 October 1805: "out fourth reef of main topsail". Courses: 1 reef band plus a middle band (Steel's 1794 table for 100 guns). Topsail feet were cut **straight in the RN** (no roach). Course feet were cut slightly hollow (§5.3).
5. **Flags.**
   - Vice-admiral's flag (plain St George's cross) at the **fore topgallant masthead**: confirmed by the 1808 *Regulations*, by an RMG catalogue note, and by Pocock's 1805 drawing.
   - White Ensign with the post-1801 Union canton: confirmed.
   - No pennant while a flag officer's flag flies: supported.
   - **The Union flags on the stays and the fleet-wide white ensigns were hoisted shortly before noon, not at dawn** (Beatty 1807; James, quoted by Nicolas). The sources disagree on which stays (§7).
   - "England expects" went up at **about 11:45** (Pasco: "about a quarter to noon").
6. **The drawing problem has a historical way out.** By the master's log, the wind was **2–3 points abaft the larboard beam**, not right aft. At 3 knots the apparent wind was roughly on the beam.
   - So the yards were braced well round, probably 30–40° from square, and the **forward (lit) faces of the sails were turned towards a viewer on the starboard side**.
   - In strict broadside the sails then show about 50–64 % of their width (§8).

**Royal "masts".** The 1794 table lists **no royal masts** but does list royal yards (main 36 ft, fore 32 ft, mizzen 24 ft). The royals were therefore set on the **long pole heads** of the topgallant masts.

- Steel's rule (p. 40): "Long pole-heads to topgallant-masts 2/3 of the length of the stop."
- Lever (1808): royals "set flying… the Halliards… reeved through the Sheave-hole in the Pole Head of the Top-gallant Mast". Royal masts proper are "seldom" used.
- The flag truck sits on top of the pole.
- Steel describes a "flagstaff-stay [that] goes round the topgallant or royal mast-head… close under the truck".
- The McKay-based JoTiKa kit for Victory 1805 rigs fore and main "topgallant flagstaff stays" and "royal backstays".

**Medium.**

**Masthead (doubling) lengths.** Steel (p. 42, "Lengths of…"):

- Main and fore masts: **5 in per yard** of mast length.
- Mizzen mast, and main and fore topmasts: **4 in per yard**.
- Mizzen topmast and all topgallant masts: **3½ in per yard**.
- The hounds of all lower masts are 7/15 of the head; of topmasts, ¾ of the head.

Applied, these give:

- Lower mastheads: main 16.25 ft (4.95 m), fore 14.4 ft (4.38 m), mizzen 11.3 ft (3.43 m).
- Topmast heads: main 7.8 ft (2.37 m), fore 7.0 ft (2.13 m), mizzen 5.1 ft (1.54 m).

How the topmast sits in the doubling (Steel p. 16): the main and fore topmasts have a "block" of 1.5 × diameter (2.6 ft) below the square heeling. The **lower cap stands one lower-masthead length above the lower part of the heeling**. The mizzen topmast and the topgallants have no block. **High.**

### 1.3 Heights above the load waterline (reconstruction)

**Method.**

1. I measured deck positions on Steel's **Plate 1, "Mast construction of a 100 Gunship"**. The masts are drawn to scale at about 32.3 px/ft on the archived scan, so the heels can be placed against the decks.
   - Main mast heel 19.15 ft below the gun (lower) deck; middle deck +26.9 ft, upper deck +34.2 ft above the heel.
   - Fore heel 18.55 ft below the gun deck.
   - Mizzen heel 17.0 ft below the gun deck; quarterdeck +40.1 ft, roundhouse (poop) +46.8 ft above its heel.
2. I took the gun deck at the main as **2.5 ft above the load waterline**, from the documented 4 ft 6 in lower-port sill height less about 2 ft of sill. The ends were set higher for sheer and trim: fore +6 ft, mizzen +3.5 ft.
3. I stacked the spars with Steel's masthead and doubling rules: a long pole on each topgallant, plus a truck of about 1 ft.

**Confidence: low-medium (±3–4 ft).**

**Checking the "205 ft / 62.5 m" figure.**

- Wikipedia (citing Goodwin) gives 205 ft (62.5 m) from the waterline to the top of the mainmast.
- My stack puts the main truck about **214 ft above the heel**. A model-builder's page (dnelson.ca, unsourced) gives 216 ft from the keel to the top of the main royal mast, which agrees.
- Above the load waterline that becomes **about 195–200 ft (59.5–61 m)**. The range depends on where the heel sits: 16.6 ft below the waterline from the port-sill evidence, or about 19 ft from a 24 ft 3 in / 25 ft 6 in sea-going draught.
- Longridge's 1:48 Trafalgar model (Science Museum Group 1947-175) is 1,450 mm "ship" height, which is 69.6 m including the keel and possibly the base. That implies 59–62 m above the waterline.

**Verdict: 205 ft is at the upper end of the plausible range.** Draw the main truck at **about 60–61 m (≈1.06 × the 186 ft gun deck; ≈0.87 × the 227 ft hull length)**. 62.5 m is defensible but slightly tall.

Use any larger figure (the "220 ft above sea level" on sailingtexas.com) with caution.

### 1.4 Positions of the masts

Steel (1794, p. 1), the Royal Navy rule:

The centre of each mast is placed abaft the stem rabbet (inside the lower-deck planking) by a fraction of the lower-deck length: one ninth for the foremast, five ninths for the main, seventeen twentieths for the mizzen.

| Mast | Fraction of gun-deck length | From the stem rabbet at gun-deck level (186 ft deck) |
|---|---|---|
| Fore | 1/9 = 0.111 | **20.7 ft (6.30 m)** |
| Main | 5/9 = 0.556 | **103.3 ft (31.50 m)** |
| Mizzen | 17/20 = 0.85 | **158.1 ft (48.19 m)** |

**Confidence: medium.** It is a rule, and RMG notes that her masts were repositioned during her Large Repair at Portsmouth (plan ZAZ0122, c.1788), so the real positions may differ by a few feet.

As a cross-check, the companion hull note (hist-hull.md) measured the channels on the contemporary model SLR0513. Converted to stem-rabbet coordinates, they run:

- fore channel about 20–50 ft;
- main channel about 103–153 ft;
- mizzen channel about 156–184 ft.

Each channel starts at its mast, as it should. In a side view the foremast stands about 45 ft abaft the figurehead tip, because the stem rabbet at gun-deck level is about 25 ft abaft that tip.

### 1.5 Rake

- Steel gives no number for ships: "no strict rule can be laid down; but in general they rake aft". His only numeric rule is for brigs: main ¾ in per yard (≈1.2°), fore ⅛ in per yard (≈0.2°).
- A Model Ship World builder reports for Victory: foremast vertical, main 1.5–2° aft, mizzen 3.5–4° aft (unsourced).
- My visual check of photographs of the restored ship (2013) is consistent, but perspective defeats any precise measurement.

**Recommendation (low-medium):** fore 0–1°, **main ≈ 2°**, **mizzen ≈ 3.5°**, all raked aft relative to the waterline. Over the stack the main truck then sits about 7 ft aft of the mast's deck position, and the mizzen truck about 10 ft aft.

Steel's proportions (p. 39–40):

- Main mast = (lower-deck length + extreme breadth) ÷ 2. For Victory, (186 + 52) ÷ 2 = 119 ft, against 117 ft in the table.
- Fore mast = 8/9 of the main; mizzen = 6/7 of the main.
- Main topmast = 3/5 of the main mast; fore topmast = 8/9 of the main topmast; mizzen topmast = ¾ of the main topmast.
- Topgallant = ½ of its topmast.
- Bowsprit (80 guns and up) = 7/11 of the main mast.

### 4.2 Taper and yardarms

- **Taper** (Steel's "fractional table", "Yards in general"): diameter at the 1st quarter is 30/31, at the 2nd quarter 7/8, at the 3rd quarter 7/10, and at the **arms 3/7 of the diameter at the slings**. The quarters are measured from the slings to each end. **High.**
  - Example, main yard: 24 in at the slings, then 23.2, 21, 16.8, and about 10.3 in at the ends.
  - Draw it as a gentle double taper that steepens over the outer quarter.
- **Section** (Steel): all yards are round except the lower and topsail yards, which are eight-square in the middle and taper like a cone to the ends. The lower and topsail yards are octagonal in the middle third. **High.**
- **Yardarm**: the part outside the stop cleats. Measured on the surviving fore topsail:
  - The topsail clews reach within about 4.5 ft of each end of the fore yard (89.1 ft yard, 80 ft foot), so lower yardarms are about 5 % of the length each.
  - The topsail head stops about 5.25 ft from each end of its yard (64.5 ft yard, 54 ft head), so topsail yardarms are about 8 %, including the 18 in clear of the cleats that Steel requires.

  **Medium.**
- **Studding-sail booms** lie on top of the lower and topsail yards in boom irons: an outer iron at the yardarm and an inner iron about ⅓ of the boom's length in. When run out they extend beyond the yardarm. **High** (Steel pp. 33–34).

### 4.3 Mizzen gaff and boom (spanker/driver): what changed after 1794

- **Steel's 1794 table gives the 100-gun ship a long mizen yard (87 ft) and *no* driver boom.** His glossary says a gaff is fitted "from the mizenmast of ships, except those of the line". **High** (for 1794).
- **By 1805 the gaff and boom were in use in ships of the line.**
  - The Trafalgar logs refer to the "spanker boom" (Collingwood's journal, 25 Oct; by then possibly describing Euryalus) and to "the spanker was split" (Prince, 98, Logs II p. 231).
  - Lever (1808): "The DRIVER or SPANKER is now cut Mainsail Fashion: and… spread by a Boom".
  - The McKay-based JoTiKa kit gives Victory a "driver gaff" and a "driver boom". The kit angles the gaff so its outer end is directly over the transom, and sets the boom off centre to port so the ensign staff can be shipped.

  **Medium-high** that Victory had a gaff and a driver boom in 1805.
- **Lengths: not found for Victory.**
  - Steel's general rules (p. 40): the driver boom equals the main topsail yard (**73 ft**); gaffs are **5/8 of their booms (≈45 ft)**.
  - For a boom outboard of about 22–30 ft past the taffrail (mizzen to taffrail ≈ 41 ft), **use boom ≈ 65–70 ft (20–21 m) and gaff ≈ 42–46 ft (13–14 m)**.

  **Low-medium.**
- **Angles.** The gaff was peaked so the peak lies over the stern. With a 45 ft gaff that means **≈25–35° above horizontal; use 30°**. The boom was nearly horizontal, topped up a few degrees.
  - With the wind on the larboard quarter or beam, both gaff and boom are **eased off to starboard (towards the viewer)**. In elevation they appear shortened, by about cos 25° ≈ 0.9 if eased 25°.

  **Low-medium.**

### 5.5 How the sails fill on this point of sailing

From the master's log, the wind was NW and the course ENE to E by N. The wind was therefore **2–3 points (22–34°) abaft the larboard beam**. At 2–3 knots in light airs the apparent wind sits near the beam, perhaps 85–100° from the bow. (My calculation from the log; **medium**.)

- **Yards.** The yards are braced with the **lee (starboard) yardarms aft and the weather (larboard) yardarms forward**, the lee braces hauled. On a beam-to-broad reach expect **≈30–40° from square** (low-medium). In a starboard view the near yardarm of every yard is aft (left) and the far yardarm forward (right).
- **Square sails.** Each sail presents its **forward (convex, leeward) face** towards forward and starboard, which is the viewer's side. The rising sun (azimuth ≈103°) lights that face almost frontally. In elevation:
  - the leeches bow towards the bow (right);
  - the head stays straight along the yard;
  - the foot is held flat at the clews by the sheets but bellies forward between them;
  - topsails show the most belly low down, in the "bag" above the foot;
  - royals and topgallants are flatter.
- **Fore-and-aft sails** (headsails, spanker) are sheeted to **starboard (lee)**, so the viewer sees their convex lee faces. The clews of the jib and flying jib are drawn aft and to starboard; the spanker boom is eased towards the viewer.
- **Heel.** Negligible in light airs (≤1–2° to starboard).

## 8. The drawing-convention problem

### 8.1 The physics in one paragraph

In exact broadside elevation, a square sail braced β degrees from square shows **W·sin β** of its true width W.

| Bracing β | Projected width |
|---|---|
| 0° (dead run) | edge-on |
| 20° | 34 % |
| 30° | 50 % |
| 40° | 64 % |

Projected yard lengths at β = 20°/30°/40°:

| Yard | Projected length (ft) |
|---|---|
| Main yard | 35/51/66 |
| Fore yard | 30/45/57 |
| Crossjack | 22/32/41 |
| Main topsail | 25/36/47 |
| Fore topsail | 22/32/41 |
| Mizzen topsail | 17/24/31 |
| Main topgallant | 17/24/31 |
| Fore topgallant | 15/21/28 |
| Main royal | 12/18/23 |
| Fore royal | 11/16/21 |

The belly of each sail (camber ≈10–15 % of the width, along the sail's forward normal) shows in elevation as **leeches bowed towards the bow**, not as a curved head or foot.

### 8.2 What the history says about β at dawn

- **Master's log** (wind NW, course ENE to E by N, 2–3 knots): wind 2–3 points abaft the larboard beam; apparent wind ≈ on the beam.
  - **β ≈ 30–40°**: sails at about 50–64 % width, forward faces lit by the sunrise.
  - Flags stream mostly towards the viewer, a problem for flags only.
- **Brief** (wind WNW, heading due east): wind 6 points abaft the beam, apparent wind ≈140° from the bow.
  - **β ≈ 15–25°**: sails at 26–42 % width.
  - Flags stream forward-right at ≈75–80 % visible length.
- **Compass versus true.** Log directions are compass bearings. Magnetic variation off Cadiz in 1805 was roughly **20° W (≈2 points)**: my estimate, not verified. So the log's "NW" is about WNW true, which matches the brief's wind, and "ENE" is about NE by E true.
  - Relative angles are unaffected, so the bracing argument above stands whichever frame is used.

### 8.3 How period painters handled it (public-domain examples)

- **Ship portrait convention.** The hull is near broadside, or a slight bow or quarter three-quarter view. The **yards are braced up and the sails drawn wider and fuller than strict geometry allows**, so they read as big lit shapes; flags are spread in the picture plane. Painters also used **several positions in one picture**.
  - Turner's ***The 'Victory' Returning from Trafalgar, in Three Positions*** (c. 1806, Yale Center for British Art, Paul Mellon Collection) is the textbook case. It is Victory herself, jury-rigged after the battle, shown broadside heading right (starboard side) with yards braced sharply: the sails are tall narrow bellied shapes with leeches curved towards the bow, beside a stern view and a quarter view.
- **Nicholas Pocock** worked from officers' sketches and, RMG says, set great store by accuracy. Relevant works:
  - *The 'Victory' in Naples Bay, 25 June 1804* and *The 'Victory' with the fleet off Stromboli, January 1805*: 1810 drawings, RMG PAF5880 and PAF5885. Victory in her 1804–05 rig, three-quarter views, yards braced, sails showing their faces; Stromboli shows the St George flag at the fore truck.
  - *The Battle of Trafalgar: Beginning of the Action* (c. 1808, BHC0548) and *End of the Action* (BHC0549): high viewpoint, columns running down under full sail.
  - *The 'Victory' cutting through the French line* (1810 drawing, PAF5881).
  - *Nelson's Flagships at Anchor* (1807, BHC1096).
- **J. M. W. Turner.**
  - *The Battle of Trafalgar, as Seen from the Mizen Starboard Shrouds of the Victory* (1806–08, Tate N00480). Turner drew Victory at Sheerness and Chatham in December 1805 (Tate "Nelson" sketchbook).
  - *The Battle of Trafalgar* (1822–24, RMG BHC0565, Greenwich Hospital Collection). It compresses events from different times of day. The RMG/Flag Institute caption notes that it shows Victory still flying the flags of Nelson's signal, and a Union flag by the mainmast. The contemporary naval criticism of its accuracy is well known but was not re-checked here.

### 8.4 Recommendation for the SVG

- **Hull**: strict starboard elevation, per the brief.
- **Yards**: **braced ≈30°** (historically supported by the log). Draw sails at **≈50–60 % of true width**. Exaggerating to about 60 % follows marine-painting convention.
  - Near (starboard) yardarms are aft (left), far yardarms forward (right).
  - Upper yards are braced the same, or a touch squarer.
- **Sails**: show the forward faces, with buntline cloths and reef bands on the visible side and leeches bowed right. The courses' hollow foot sits higher in the middle.
- **Studding sails** (optional, historically right): on the larboard side, so they extend each fore and main topsail and topgallant **to the right (forward)** beyond its far leech. The fore lower studding sail sits outside the fore course on the far side.
- **Fore-and-aft sails**: sheeted towards the viewer; the spanker boom foreshortened by about 10 %.
- **Flags**: streaming right at ≈60–80 % of length. This is mild licence, justified if the true wind is a little further aft than the log's dawn bearing, which is what the brief assumes.

## 10. Doubts and conflicts

1. **Main truck height.** The brief's 205 ft (62.5 m; Wikipedia, citing Goodwin) against my reconstruction of ≈195–200 ft (59.5–61 m). The reconstruction depends on the heel depth and on Steel's long-pole rule; Longridge's model suggests 59–62 m. **Use about 60–61 m.** The brief's claim is close but slightly generous.
2. **Steel 1794 versus Victory 1805.** The spar list is the 1794 establishment for a generic 100-gun ship, not Victory's own 1803 rig. McKay's *The 100-Gun Ship Victory* (1987) has Victory-specific tables but is not online; I could not check it. The surviving fore topsail matches Steel's fore spars well. Known changes since 1794:
   - the gaff and boom replaced the 87 ft mizen yard;
   - a flying jib-boom was added;
   - a 4th topsail reef was added.
3. **Gaff versus mizen yard.** Steel's 1794 table gives a 100-gun ship a lateen-type mizen yard and no driver boom. 1805 logs and Lever (1808) attest spanker booms. The McKay-based kit gives Victory a "driver gaff/boom". I found no Victory-specific primary statement. **Medium-high confidence in the gaff.**
4. **Mast positions.** Steel's rule (fore 20.7, main 103.3, mizzen 158.1 ft from the stem rabbet) against Victory's c. 1788 repositioning (RMG ZAZ0122). The channel positions measured in hist-hull.md fit the rule, but the true values could differ by a few feet.
5. **Rake.** No primary figures. Fore ≈0–1°, main ≈2°, mizzen ≈3.5° are estimates from forum reports and photographs.
6. **Shroud counts.** Fore 10, main 11, mizzen 7 lower shrouds a side come from the present restoration and a McKay-based kit, not a 1805 document. Topmast shrouds (≈5/5/4) are unverified.
7. **Bowsprit steeve.** Steel says "nearly 36°"; the restored ship looks ≈33–35°. I recommend 34°.
8. **Flying jib-boom, gaff, boom and dolphin-striker lengths** are rule- or estimate-based (low).
9. **Reef counts.** Steel's 1794 table gives 3 topsail reefs and 1 course reef (100 guns), and a 2-reef mizzen topsail against 3 in his own text. Victory's 1805 log proves **4** on the main topsail. I assume the fore matches and keep 1 reef on the courses.
10. **Studding sails at dawn.** The brief omits them, but they were set by about 6:30–7:00, on the weather (larboard) side. Showing them is optional and correct; omitting them is a simplification.
11. **Mizzen royal.** In the establishment, but not specifically logged. The brief omits it; either choice is defensible.
12. **Wind and course.** The brief has WNW and due east. Victory's log has NW (compass), ENE then E by N, 2–3 knots. Moorsom: "light from the NW"; Beatty: "from the west". Magnetic variation (≈20° W, my estimate) reconciles NW (compass) with WNW (true). The consequences are bracing (§8.2) and flag foreshortening.
13. **Draught and waterline.** Wikipedia's 28 ft 9 in conflicts with Goodwin's 24 ft 3 in / 25 ft 6 in (via hist-hull.md) and with the 4 ft 6 in lower-port sill. My heights use the sill evidence; a deeper heel would lower all heights by up to about 2 ft.
14. **Union jacks.** Three versions of the position (Nelson: fore topgallant stay; Beatty: fore topmast and topgallant stays; James: main topmast and fore topgallant stays), with funeral evidence for two jacks. **Timing is clear: late morning, not dawn.** The brief implies they might be shown; I recommend not showing them at dawn.
15. **White ensign at dawn.** Plausible (Victory's own squadron colour), but the documented fleet-wide hoisting of white ensigns was ≈noon. Showing the ensign at the gaff peak at dawn is a small licence.
16. **Admiral's flag at dawn.** Normally always flying, but James says the admirals "hoisted their respective flags" at noon. Showing it at dawn is defensible.
17. **Flag sizes and proportions.** Only the Brunswick ensign (1794) and the Trafalgar jacks are measured. I found nothing for 1805 RN admirals' flags.
18. **Steel's table misprint.** The jib-boom diameter is printed "45¼" in; it must be 15¼ (it cannot exceed the bowsprit's 37 in).
19. **Course depths and lower-yard hang** (≈5 ft below the tops) are estimates; the fore topsail calibration suggests 3–7 ft.
20. **George Chambers' Trafalgar painting** was not located; it is listed in the brief but unverified here. Similarly, Constable's 1806 watercolour is only cited from Commons; I did not check the holding institution.
21. **Sunrise against the enemy bearing.** The sun rose at ≈103° true. The enemy bore "E by S" by compass, ≈80° true if the variation is ≈21° W. So the sun rose behind the southern part of the enemy line, not its centre: consistent with "behind the enemy fleet".
