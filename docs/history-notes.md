# History notes

Facts the scene uses, with their sources, confidence and doubts. This file is the summary; the full research notes, with measurements, tables, short quotations from period sources and every URL, are in `docs/research/` (what may and may not go there: `docs/research/README.md`):

- `research/victory-hull.md`: Victory's hull as of October 1805 (dimensions, ports, paint, details, what not to copy from the present ship).
- `research/victory-rig.md`: masts, spars, sails, rigging and flags, with an elevation coordinate table.
- `research/dawn-setting.md`: positions, sun, moon, weather, both fleets, and a fact-check of 54 claims against independent sources (its last section).

Confidence: **high** means several independent primary sources agree, a museum artefact, or a computation confirmed by a second ephemeris; **medium** means one good source or a consistent inference; **low** means an estimate, a measurement from a photograph, or conflicting sources.

Times are local apparent time (ship's time). Bearings in 1805 logs are magnetic; the variation off Cádiz was about 21° W, so true = magnetic − 21°.

## The setting

- **Place and layout.** The British fleet was about 20 nm west of Cape Trafalgar (the Cape "bore E by S about seven leagues", Collingwood). The Combined Fleet lay between it and the Spanish coast, 9–12 nm east of Victory, strung out north–south over about 5 nm, heading south on the starboard tack. Layout high; distances medium.
- **Sunrise.** Upper limb at 06:27 local apparent time (06:36 UT), azimuth 102.5° true; the whole disc clear by about 06:30. Civil dawn 06:01, nautical dawn 05:31; the logs' "daylight" falls at 05:30–06:10. The sun climbs at about 53° to the horizon, up and to the right. High (computed; matches USNO and JPL Horizons DE441).
- **Moon.** A waning crescent about 2 % lit, rising at 05:19 in the east and about 12° up at sunrise; new moon on 22 October. The night had been moonless. No account mentions seeing it. High (computed). The scene shows it faintly at p = 0 and lets it fade.
- **Stars.** The scene's sparse stars fade in the first third of the scroll, before civil dawn would have washed them out. Mercury (magnitude −1.1) was about 5° up in the east at civil dawn, deep in the glow and haze; nobody records it, and the scene leaves it out.
- **Sky and cloud.** The night's squalls had passed. Gravina's flagship logged squall cloud in the north-east and south-east quadrants at dawn, so cloud banks on the eastern horizon are supported; several British logs say "cloudy". The scene has low, broken strands near the sunrise. High.
- **Wind.** Very light (Beaufort 1–2), westerly, dying toward calm by noon. Victory's log at 08:00: "light breezes and cloudy". High for "light westerly"; the exact direction is disputed (NW, WNW, W and WSW in different logs, all magnetic).
- **Swell.** "A great swell from the westward" (Euryalus), "the sea smooth with a great ground swell setting from the westward" (James), "la houle de l'Ouest très forte" (French logs). Few or no whitecaps. High. Period and height were not recorded; the scene uses a long, slow swell.
- **Visibility.** At least 10–12 nm: the enemy's 33 ships were counted at that range. Their hulls were partly below the horizon (3–12 m hidden from an eye 5–10 m up), so sails and upper works show and waterlines do not. Each rig subtends about 0.3 of the sun's diameter. High (computed).

## The fleets

- **British.** 27 ships of the line. At dawn they were in loose cruising order, not yet two tidy columns ("sans ordre", Dumanoir and Scipion), and the columns formed over about two hours. Once formed, the first two ships astern of Victory in the weather column were the three-deckers Téméraire (98) and Neptune (98). Collingwood's lee column was to the south, out of a north-facing frame. High. The scene shows Téméraire, Neptune and a 74 astern, faint in the haze.
- **Combined Fleet.** 33 ships of the line (18 French, 15 Spanish), plus 5 frigates and 2 brigs; easy sail at dawn (mostly topsails), close-hauled on the starboard tack, heading south, a ragged line of about 5 nm. The crescent shape came only after Villeneuve's order to wear (about 08:00–08:30), finished around 10:00, so the dawn line is a ragged straight line, not a crescent. High. Seen from the west, ships heading south show their bows to the right.
- **The enemy's paint** (one eyewitness, Midshipman Badcock): some black and yellow like the British, some with a single red or yellow band, some all black; the Santísima Trinidad had four red bands with white between. Irrelevant at silhouette scale; against the sunrise they read as soft mid-tone silhouettes.

## HMS Victory as of October 1805

After the 1800–1803 great repair. The most valuable source is the contemporary 1:48 model made during that repair (Royal Museums Greenwich SLR0513), photographed broadside-on; several heights and positions are measured from those photographs and from RMG plans (ZAZ0120–0122), scaled from the 186 ft gun deck.

### Hull
- Gun deck 186 ft (56.7 m); figurehead to taffrail about 226 ft 6 in (69.0 m); extreme breadth 51 ft 10 in. High.
- Armament at Trafalgar: 104 guns. Guns per side 15 lower, 14 middle, 15 upper, 6 quarterdeck, 2 forecastle (one a carronade), so some ports carried no gun. High.
- Ports (see the table above). Port spacing about 11.5 ft; the middle-deck ports are staggered between the others. The eight central upper-deck ports (3rd–10th from forward) and the forward quarterdeck openings had no lids, so they show as dark openings even with the ports closed. Medium.
- Heights above the waterline (measured, about ±1 ft): lower port sills about 4 ft 6 in; bands at about 1.5–2.7, 3.7–5.0 and 5.9–7.0 m; channels about 7 m (above the upper-deck ports, moved there in 1800–03); waist rail about 8.8 m, quarterdeck 10.1, forecastle 10.3–10.9, poop at the taffrail about 12.5. Medium-low.
- Sheer: gentle, lowest a little abaft amidships, rising about 1.5 m at the stern and 1.7 m at the bow. Medium-low.
- Paint: black (near-neutral) hull and lids, pale ochre bands: the Nelson chequer, correct for Trafalgar (when she left the repair in 1803 the lids were still ochre). Port linings and inner lid faces red. Medium-high. The whole of the British lower masts, hoops included, were painted yellow; the enemy's mast hoops were black, and Nelson had Belleisle and Polyphemus paint theirs to avoid confusion (Nicolas VII 131). High.
- Copper: only a thin strip shows at the waterline. Low (inferred; no documented figure).
- Bow: an open beakhead with a bulkhead at upper-deck level, three curved headrails, catheads, and a figurehead of the crowned royal arms held by two cupids (blue sash to starboard). The round bow of pre-1922 photographs is the 1814 bow and wrong for 1805. Medium-high.
- Stern: closed in 1803, three tiers of windows; the quarter gallery has three tiers from the middle deck to the quarterdeck; no stern davits (removed in 1800–03). Medium-high.

### Rig and sails
- Spars from David Steel's 1794 Royal Navy table for a 100-gun ship of 2,164 tons (Victory: 2,142). It matches her surviving 1803 fore topsail (80 ft foot, 54 ft head, 54 ft deep) within a foot or two. Main mast 117 ft, topmast 70, topgallant 35; main yard 102 ft 4 in; bowsprit 74 ft at "nearly thirty-six degrees" (drawn at 34°), jib-boom 53 ft. Medium (Steel as a close proxy; McKay's Victory-specific tables were not available).
- Mast positions by the Royal Navy rule (fore 1/9, main 5/9, mizzen 17/20 of the gun deck from the stem); rake about 0.5°, 2° and 3.5°. Low-medium.
- Sails set: fore and main course, topsail, topgallant, royal; mizzen topsail and topgallant; the spanker on gaff and boom (by 1805 a gaff and boom had replaced the old mizzen yard; logs of 1805 say "spanker"); fore topmast staysail, jib, flying jib. Medium-high.
- Topsails had four reef bands (Victory's log, 24 October: "out fourth reef of main topsail") and straight feet; courses one reef band plus a middle band, feet cut slightly hollow. High (main topsail), medium (others).
- The wind was 2–3 points abaft the beam, so the yards were braced about 30–40° from square, turning the sails' forward, sunlit faces toward a viewer on the starboard side. In strict broadside they show about 50–64 % of their width. The ship is drawn with yards braced about 30° and sails at 64 % width, as period ship portraitists (Pocock, Turner) did. Medium.
- Rigging: shrouds about 10 fore, 11 main, 7 mizzen a side (from the restored ship and a kit manual, not an 1805 source); topmast shrouds about 5/5/4. Low-medium.

### Flags
- Vice-admiral's flag of the White (white, plain red St George's cross) at the fore topgallant masthead: confirmed by the 1808 Regulations, an RMG catalogue note and Pocock's 1805 drawing. High for the position.
- White Ensign with the post-1801 Union canton, at the peak of the spanker gaff when flown under sail (Perrin 1922). High for design and position. **Not flown at dawn** (owner's decision, see below); the spec keeps it, with its position and size, for the approach scene.
- No commission pennant while an admiral's flag flies. Medium-high.
- Ensigns were about twice as long as deep (a surviving 1794 ensign is 20 × 40 ft). The flags are drawn about 1:2 and foreshortened, since they stream toward the viewer as well as to the right.
- The Union Jacks on the stays and "England expects" (about 11:45) belong to late morning, not dawn, and are not shown.

## Assumptions and licences

1. **Ports closed at dawn.** An assumption, as the brief says. The lidless ports still show as openings.
2. **No White Ensign at dawn** (owner's decision, session 3). The Euryalus log and James have colours hoisted at about 12:15, as the action began; no source has ensigns flying at dawn. The brief asked for one; the owner removed it. It will be hoisted in the approach scene, before battle, as a deliberate moment.
3. **The vice-admiral's flag at dawn.** Kept (owner's decision, session 3): as he understands it, a flag officer's flag flew for as long as he was aboard in command. The sources do not clearly say otherwise. For: Basil Hall (via Perrin) has the commission pendant never hauled down, day or night, and in a flagship the admiral's flag takes its place; the 1808 Regulations keep even a fallen commander-in-chief's flag flying until the battle ends. Against, at most: the Euryalus log and James have the admirals "hoisting their respective flags" at about 12:16, which can also be read as battle flags added to the ones already flying. No source says Nelson's flag was down at dawn.
4. **No studding sails in scene 1** (owner's decision, session 3). The log has them set on bearing up, about 20–30 minutes after sunrise, and later accounts have them on both sides; at the moment of sunrise Victory was most likely still steering N by E under reefed topsails and foresail. They belong to the approach scene, where the fleet sets every scrap of canvas in the light wind (`docs/backlog.md`).
5. **The British in a column.** At dawn they were in loose order; the scene shows the weather column already forming astern of Victory, which is how it looked within the next two hours.
6. **Scale of the distant ships.** At true angular size the enemy rigs would be a few pixels tall. They are drawn larger, still about a third of the sun's diameter as the research advises, so they read as ships.
7. **The sun in frame.** Artistic licence, accepted by the owner: the real sun rose behind a north-facing viewer's right shoulder (see 8), but the scene shows it rising on the right of the frame.
8. **Viewpoint.** A side view looking north with the sun and the enemy on the right is a stylised panorama: the real enemy line ran north–south across 90° of the eastern horizon, and the sun rose at 102.5° true, behind a north-facing viewer's right shoulder.

## Open doubts

- Lower-deck ports: 16 a side (a Trafalgar midshipman, the 2012 official site, the present ship) against 15 on the contemporary model. Settled by the owner for the scene: 15, as on the model, matching her 15 lower-deck guns a side.
- The main truck height (59.5–61 m by reconstruction against the quoted 62.5 m) depends on the assumed heel depth and Steel's long-pole rule. The owner accepted the 60.2 m reconstruction from Steel's 1794 tables.
- Gaff, spanker boom, flying jib-boom and dolphin striker lengths are rule-based estimates.
- Quarterdeck (7 or 8) and forecastle (2 or 3) openings; the poop bulwark (open rail on the model, solid in one reconstruction).
- Red inside the ports: the museum's specifications use red, but a summary of Goodwin's 2013 paint article says the 1805 accounts list no red. Deferred by the owner until a scene shows the ports open (`docs/backlog.md`); in scene 1 the colour shows only through the lidless upper-deck, quarterdeck and forecastle openings.
- The exact wind direction at dawn; the distances to the enemy (6–12 nm across sources) and to the coast.
- Whether the Spanish coast was visible from deck at first light (certainly seen later in the morning).
- Not consulted first-hand: McKay's *Anatomy of the Ship* drawings and spar tables, McGowan (2003), Goodwin's *Pocket Manual* (2015) and his 2013 *Mariner's Mirror* article.

## Scene 2: the strengths lettered at the tracks' heads (sessions 20 and 21)

On the far views of scene 2 each squadron is lettered at its track's head with its commander and its strength ("Villeneuve, 20 sail"), and the October fleets off Cádiz among their ships ("Nelson, 27 sail"). This section records what the label counts, day by day, and where the sources part. It joins the Scene 2 section when that is merged (`docs/research/scene-02/session-17/history-scene-02-wip.md`). The research behind it, with every page and link: `docs/research/scene-02/session-21/strengths-by-date.md`.

**The rule (the owner's answers of 12 November 2026).** The label counts ships of the line only, as the dispatches counted a fleet's strength and as the finale's 27 against 33 does. Frigates, small craft and prizes are left out. One rule holds everywhere: the figure is the ships of the line actually sailing together at the head of the track on that date. A straggler is added on the day she caught up. A ship that left is taken off on the day she left. Villeneuve's crossing begins with the 14 who went out with him from Cádiz. The figure and the ships that come in at the head as the camera nears always agree, and the figure changes smoothly. Where no source gives the day, the most probable is taken and recorded here. High.

**What "sail" counted then.** Where a dispatch gave a fleet's strength it counted ships of the line apart from the rest. Calder's own dispatch has the enemy at "20 Sail of the Line", with ships armed en flûte, frigates and brigs listed after them, and his own as 15 of the line, two frigates, a cutter and a lugger (Naval Chronicle XIV, p. 163). Nelson's frigates reported the Toulon fleet as eleven of the line, seven frigates and two brigs (Nicolas VI, p. 396). Where strength was the subject, bare "sail" meant ships of the line too: Nelson's memorandum of 9 October gives the enemy as "thirty-four sail" (Nicolas VII, pp. 89–92). In a sighting the same word took in every vessel in view (the Defiance's "24, then 27 sail" when 20 of the line were there; Corbett, p. 196).

**The British fleet off Cádiz, by date.**
- **29 from 6 p.m. on 28 September.** Nelson's three joined Collingwood's 26: 23 in the main body and 6 inshore under Rear-Admiral Louis (the Victory's log and signal log, Nicolas VII 53 n.; Nelson to Acton and to Elliot, 30 September, VII 54, 59; the 26 named in Desbrière 1907, p. 143).
- **23 from 6 p.m. on 2 October.** Louis sailed for Gibraltar and Tetuan with the Queen, Canopus, Spencer, Tigre and Zealous. The log enters this under the nautical "October 3rd. P.M. at 6" (VII 63 n.; Nelson's diary, Hudson 1917, pp. 19–20; Austen, Hubback 1906, p. 148). The Leviathan was also away at Gibraltar: Nelson wrote on the 5th that he had sent "six Sail of the Line" and had "twenty-three Sail with me" (VII 75).
- **24 from the morning of 7 October.** The Defiance joined (VII 84).
- **26 on 8 October.** The Leviathan came back from Gibraltar, and the Royal Sovereign joined at about 4 p.m. (VII 88 and n., 107; Nelson's diary, Hudson 1917, the transcript facing p. 20).
- **27 from the afternoon of 10 October.** The Belleisle joined (VII 101).
- **28 from the forenoon of 13 October.** The Agamemnon joined (VII 119).
- **28 through the night of 13–14 October.** The Prince of Wales left on the evening of the 13th (VII 119–120; Beatty 1825, pp. 5–6; Corbett 1910, p. 330), and the Africa joined by the forenoon of the 14th (the Victory's log under the nautical "October 14th", VII 121 n.; VII 94 n., 121). Strictly the fleet was 27 for those hours; the label takes the two as one exchange at 6 p.m. on the 13th, so the figure stays at 28 (its ships, one for the other).
- **27 from 17 October to the battle.** The Donegal was sent to Gibraltar for a ground tier of casks (the diary, Beatty 1825, p. 97; James III 383); no hour is recorded, 3 p.m. is taken.

The peak was 28. Sources that differ: James (III 379) and Beatty (1825, p. 3) give 27 on Nelson's arrival; James and Clowes give a peak of 29, which needs the Africa in before the Prince of Wales sailed, which no source shows; James and Clowes put the Royal Sovereign "between the 9th and 13th" and the Prince of Wales on the 14th, the log's nautical date; Beatty's narrative has the Donegal leaving on the 18th, probably the log's date; Desbrière (p. 148) has Louis sailing on 3 October, the log's date read literally. High for 29, 23 by the 5th and 27; medium-high for the steps between.

**Louis's brief return, 4–5 October.** On the morning of the 3rd the Euryalus told Louis by signal that the enemy had embarked their troops. He sent the Zealous and the Endymion on with the convoy and turned back with the other four (Austen, Hubback 1906, pp. 148–149; James III 380). On the 4th they were in sight to the southward (the diary, Hudson 1917; Codrington, *Memoir* I 53). They were with the Commander-in-Chief on the morning of the 5th and sent off again that day (Austen; Beatty, p. 4, has them rejoin on the 4th). Nelson counted 23 that day. The label does not count those hours. Medium-high.

**The Leviathan.** She was at Gibraltar from early October and came back on the 8th "loaded" (Nelson to Collingwood, 8 October, VII 88; James III 380). No source records the day she went. The 29 of 28 September include her, and Nelson's 23 of 5 October leave her out. The Victory's log names only Louis's five and the Endymion parting on 2 October, and Austen makes Louis's group five of the line, so she did not sail with them. Nelson's letters of 2 and 5 October count the ships sent to Gibraltar and Tetuan as six (VII 63, 75). She had been in Louis's inshore squadron in August (Austen's log, Hubback 1906, p. 142), and on 1 October Louis came out to the fleet with only part of his squadron (Beatty, p. 4), so she may have gone straight from the inshore station on 30 September or 1 October. The label takes her off with Louis's five at 6 p.m. on 2 October. Low for the day. Her own log (Bedfordshire Archives, X170/1/2) or the Victory's for those days would settle it; neither is online.

**The combined fleet in Cádiz.** 32 ready on 28 September: 18 French and 14 Spanish. Gravina told Villeneuve that day that his 14 were entirely ready and the Santa Ana, a three-decker just out of dock, would follow in four or five days (Gravina to Decrès, 28 September, Desbrière 1907, annexes p. 91). 33 from early October: Desbrière has the Santa Ana ready only in the first days of October (p. 134; his p. 136 paraphrases the same letter as nine days), and on 8 October the council of war counted her with the fleet, "à peine sortis de l'arsenal" (annexes p. 97). The label takes noon on 3 October. Medium; low for the day. Nothing else came or went until the sortie: the Terrible and the San Fulgencio lay disarmed or in dock, and British counts from outside of 34–36 take in ships in harbour that were not with the fleet. Seven of the line got out on the 19th and the rest on the morning of the 20th; the label stays at 33 through the sortie, one fleet under one flag.

**The crossing's 14.** Villeneuve went out from Cádiz at about 2 a.m. on 10 April with 14 by the owner's choice: the 11 from Toulon, the Aigle, the Argonauta and the América (Desbrière 1907, pp. 9–11). The record has two days that differ: at dawn on the 10th only the Argonauta was with the 12 French, and the América rejoined on the 12th (Reille, p. 11), or "next morning" by Villeneuve's report (p. 10); strictly that gives 13 for two days. James (III 342) has 17 getting under way together, with the San Rafael following. The Firme, Terrible and España were found off Fort-de-France on 14 May (17; the hour is not recorded, 4 p.m. is taken), and the San Rafael anchored with the fleet in the afternoon of the 16th after Diamond Rock had fired on her in the morning (18; James III 343–344; 3 p.m. is taken, where session 20 had noon). High.

**Off Finisterre.** The Firme struck soon after 8 p.m. on 22 July and the San Rafael a few minutes later (James III 361; the label takes 8.30): 20 to 18. Lauriston, writing from Cádiz on 2 September with news from the two Spanish captains, has the San Rafael striking at 9 p.m. (Desbrière, annexes p. 111); Desbrière's narrative has both fighting on through part of the night and striking at daybreak on the 23rd (pp. 63–64). Medium for the hour.

**Vigo, A Coruña and Ferrol.**
- **15 out of Vigo on 31 July:** the Atlas, España and América left there (Desbrière pp. 85–86; James: the 30th).
- **13 from 2 August.** On entering the bay the two Spaniards, with Gravina already in the channel, had to go into Ferrol under the Emperor's order, and the 13 French anchored at A Coruña (Desbrière 1907, p. 86; James, Mahan and Corbett's narrative have him in on 1 August). New under the rule; session 20 kept 15 until the 13th.
- **26 from the afternoon of 10 August.** The French worked out of A Coruña on the 8th to 10th. On the 10th, at about 12.30, Gourdon's five and eight Spaniards came out of the ría of Ferrol; the wind died before the last three cleared the narrows, and all anchored in the bay of Ares (p. 97; Escaño's journal, annexes p. 7, has nine Spaniards, which would make 27). The label takes 2 p.m.
- **29 from the forenoon of 13 August.** The last three joined, and all were under sail by 2 p.m. (pp. 97–98; Escaño has the fleet sailing from the ría of Ares at 9 a.m.; James: out on the 11th). The label takes 9 a.m.

High for the split, medium for 26.

**Smaller dates.**
- **Nelson.** The Royal Sovereign left him between 4 p.m. and 6.50 p.m. on 11 May, while Knight's Queen and Dragon were in company, not under Nelson's flag and not counted (Nicolas VI 433); 5 p.m. is taken. His flag was rehoisted in the Victory at St Helens at 11.30 a.m. on 14 September (VII 36), so "Victory" is lettered from then (session 20 had 8 a.m. on the 15th, when she weighed).
- **Collingwood.** The Mars rejoined at midnight on 20–21 August (James III 375): 3 to 4. Bickerton's four joined on the 22nd, hour not recorded, and made 8 (James III 379; Desbrière p. 143); his label closes at noon on the 22nd, as they join: from there he is the fleet off Cádiz, which the scene letters only with Nelson's arrival.
- **Calder.** He became 14 at 8 p.m. on 26 July, when the Windsor Castle went home with the prizes (James III 371), after his window closes at noon on the 26th; the label stays 15.

**What the chart draws for these figures.** A squadron at a track's head is a block of its ships of the line only, the ships its label counts: the frigate Euryalus is no longer drawn with the Victory in September, nor Calder's two prizes with his line. Each block's places are its ships, one each or in turn: off Finisterre the two that struck are the Firme and the San Rafael, Spanish glyphs. The October fleets draw the 27 and 33 of Trafalgar, each ship from the day she was with the fleet (the Defiance, Royal Sovereign, Belleisle, Agamemnon and Africa joining, the Leviathan away from 2 to 8 October, the Santa Ana from 3 October). The seven British ships of the line there in those weeks but not at Trafalgar (Queen, Canopus, Spencer, Tigre, Zealous, Prince of Wales, Donegal) are counted in the label on their days and not drawn: a fleet's ships of the line come in only where every one of them reads one by one, and off Cádiz the British fleet's do not before the Donegal has gone (`scripts/dev/fleet-gates.mjs` shows it).

**Drawn with other types, not counted wrong** (they show only in the close views): Nelson's Canopus and Tigre, 80-gun two-deckers, and his Royal Sovereign, a first rate, are drawn as 74s and a 98; Spanish 80s and 64s, Calder's Malta (80) and two 64s, and four of Ferrol's six French 80s are drawn as 74s.

**What changed from session 20's figures.** The British October label goes by date (29, 23, 24, 26, 27, 28, 27) instead of a constant 27; the combined fleet's in Cádiz is 32 until the Santa Ana joined, then 33; Villeneuve is 13 at A Coruña from 2 August and 26 from 10 August, where 15 was lettered until the 13th; the San Rafael joins at Martinique in the afternoon of 16 May; "Victory" is lettered from 11.30 a.m. on 14 September; Collingwood's label closes as Bickerton's four join.

**Doubts.**
- The day the Leviathan went to Gibraltar. Low.
- Whether the Africa joined in the night of 13–14 October or in the morning. Medium.
- The hour the Donegal left on the 17th. Low.
- The day the Santa Ana joined the fleet in the roads. Low.
- Whether Louis's four were in company on the 4th (Beatty) or the 5th (Austen); either way, for hours only.
- The six inshore on 28 September, of whom only the Canopus, Spencer and Tigre are certain.
- James's 27 on arrival, which leaves out two ships no source names as absent.
- The hour the Firme and the San Rafael struck.
- Whether the strict reading should letter the British 26 for the night of 20–21 October, while the Africa was out of touch to the north. The label keeps 27: she was under Nelson's flag and fought.
- Whether the America's two days astern after Cádiz (13, not 14) should be lettered. The owner chose 14 from Cádiz.

## Scene 2: Boulogne from the sea (beat 1)

What beat 1's painting rests on: the roads of Boulogne, the flotilla, the town and the camps on an August evening in 1805, seen from a boat in the roads looking east at the shore. The research is in `docs/research/scene-02/boulogne-sea/`, and its `README.md` lists the files and their status: four topic notes (`light-and-watch.md`, `flotilla.md`, `camps.md`, `town-and-defences.md`), an independent check of each (`*-check.md`) and the computation of the evening (`evening.md`). Below, files are named without the folder. Where a check corrects its note, this section follows the check. The liberties the painting takes are decisions, recorded in `decisions.md` ("Boulogne from the sea").

**The painting's eye**: 50.7300 N 1.5780 E, 1.1 km due west of the 1805 jetty heads (taken as 50.730 N 1.594 E, uncertain by 200–300 m), 6 m above the water, looking along 98° true. Left is north-north-east, right south-south-east, straight behind 278°. Times are local apparent time (LAT), which French clocks showed in 1805; on 10 August 1805 at Boulogne LAT = UT + 1.3 min. Tide heights are on Desbrière's zero (the tide scale of the ponts et chaussées).

The scene 2 history draft (`docs/research/scene-02/session-17/history-scene-02-wip.md` and `history-scene-02-merged.md`, "Boulogne and the invasion army") is superseded by this section where they differ: the Étaples huts were thatched (tiles and glazing were materials the dig expected to find, not finds), and the 748 huts of 1804 were Legrand's division's at Ambleteuse, not a Boulogne camp's (`camps-check.md`, (a)).

### The evening and its light

- **The date: 10 August 1805**, a Saturday (22 thermidor an XIII). The full moon fell at 11:57 UT that day, and at sunset the moon was 99.9 % lit. Of the owner's evenings (9–11 August), it is the one on which the moon's disc clears the hills over the town at sunset, seen from the eye. High (computed; `evening.md`, 1 and 3).
- **The sun.** Sunset (upper limb on the horizon) at 19:26, azimuth 296.2°, 18° left of straight behind. The golden light, the sun 12° to 5° up, lasts from 18:01 to 18:47. Civil dusk ends at 20:03, nautical dusk at 20:52. High (NASA/JPL Horizons; the check's own computation and the US Naval Observatory agree within a minute; `light-and-watch-check.md`, 2).
- **The painting's light is that of 18:33**, 52 minutes before sunset: the sun 7.0° up at azimuth 286.2°, 8° left of straight behind. All that faces the sea is lit square-on and nearly without shadow as seen from the boat: the cliffs north of the harbour face about west, the shore towards Le Portel north-west. Shadows fall away from the viewer and a little to the right. The sea ahead mirrors the cool eastern sky; the warm sky and the sun's glitter are behind the camera. High for the geometry; Medium for the look (general optics; `light-and-watch.md`, 2).
- **The moon.** It rose on the sea horizon at 19:09 (azimuth 108.8°), but from the eye the hills hid it. Its upper limb showed over them at 19:22. At 19:26, the minute of sunset, the whole disc was clear: azimuth 111.95°, centre 1.96° up, the lower limb just on the skyline. That skyline is the southern shoulder of Mont Lambert (ground 154 m, 5.1 km off), with the southern part of the lower town in front of it. So the moon came up 9.3° right of the belfry and 6.9° right of Mont Lambert's summit: over the right-hand part of the town, not over the walled upper town. It then climbed to the right at about 36° to the horizon, 1.9° right and 1.4° up every ten minutes. High for the positions; Medium for the minutes it shows over the hills (modern bare ground, about ±1 minute; `evening.md`, 3).
- **The moon's size and look.** 30.0′ across (0.50°): the full moon came near apogee, so it was about 10 % smaller than one at perigee. At 2° up refraction flattens it to about 0.92 of its width. High. At sunset, 2° up through summer haze, it is a dim, warm disc, apricot to pink, hardly brighter than the sky, and haze can hide it; it whitens and reads plainly from about 5° up (about 19:45). It stands in the pink band of the anti-twilight, and the blue-grey shadow of the earth rises under it within ten minutes. Medium (general optics).
- **The neighbouring evenings**, from the same eye. On 11 August (98 % lit) the disc clears the hills at 19:44, 20 minutes after sunset, straight above the west corner of the walls, 1.4° right of the belfry. On 9 August it clears at 19:06, before sunset, over the Liane valley right of the town; on 12 August at 19:57, left of the upper town. High (computed). The light note's moonrises are for the sea horizon; behind the hills the moon shows later and further right (`light-and-watch-check.md`, item 5).
- **The weather.** Boulogne's weather on 10 August 1805 is not recorded in anything read. Napoleon arrived on 3 August in bad weather, with no craft in the roads, and wrote on 20 August that it had rained a great deal. In London, 150 km away, the Royal Society's journal has light airs on 10 August from the south-west, then the west, cloud in the morning and fair weather at 2 p.m., 16–23 °C, and the barometer at the month's highest. No rain fell there from the morning of 9 August to 14 August, and the afternoons of 11–13 August had light airs from south to west-north-west. So a warm, settled evening with a light westerly air, some cloud and haze (which such weather commonly brings in the Strait) is well founded. High for London; Medium for Boulogne, as inference; Low for any detail of the evening. The British logs kept off Boulogne (The National Archives, ADM 51/52) would settle it; they were not read (`evening.md`, 5; `light-and-watch-check.md`, 5).
- **The sea.** In summer, a low swell from the west or south-west and small waves, usually well under a metre; whitecaps only above force 4. Medium (modern climate statistics). A French report of 25 August 1804 describes a good day in the roads as fine, with "la mer peu houleuse" (Dumas XI 45). High as a state of the sea in which the line was kept.
- **England is out of sight.** From a boat (an eye 3–6 m up) the cliffs of Dover and Folkestone, 46–48 km away, lie below the sea horizon. At most a hair-line 0.03–0.06° tall could show on a rare clear day, and it would lie behind the viewer's left shoulder (Dover at 332–338°). High (geometry; `light-and-watch-check.md`, 8).
