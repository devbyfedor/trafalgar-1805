# Small craft with the fleets, 19–21 October 1805 (checked)

Research note for scene 2, checked against the sources. It covers the vessels below frigate size at the chart's two moments: Cádiz bay on 19 October, about 7 to 8.15 a.m., and first light on 21 October, about 6 a.m. It replaces the first draft (small-craft.md). What the check changed is listed at the end.

Conventions. Log bearings are by compass unless marked true. The variation was about 20–21° W, so true = compass − 21°. Positions are worked by plane sailing from San Sebastián light, 36°31.7′ N 6°19.0′ W, as the project's notes do. Distances are in nautical miles (nm). "Annexes" means the separately paged second part of Desbrière 1907 (letters, then the French and Spanish reports). Page numbers were checked on the archive.org scans; where the scan and its OCR text differ, the scan is followed. Every finding carries a confidence.

## Summary for the chart

- **First light, 21 October.** Four naval vessels below the frigate were with the fleets: the British schooner Pickle and cutter Entreprenante, and the French brigs Argus and Furet. The chart should draw all four. No source names any other warship of that size with either fleet. High.
- **A possible fifth small sail, with the British (contested).** The Orion's master listed "a brig" with the British fleet besides the schooner and the cutter (NRS II 278). Two French frigates counted three small craft among the British: the Hermione at first light (annexes p. 243) and the Hortense at 6.45 (annexes p. 189). Every other British or French count gives two, or fewer. The likeliest candidate is the laden merchant brig that the Agamemnon detained on the 19th and towed on the 20th; whether she was still with the fleet on the 21st is not recorded (section 1). Low–Medium that a third small sail was in sight; Low that it was this brig. Whether to draw her is the owner's decision.
- **Where.** No log fixes any of the four at dawn. Estimates: the Pickle between the fleets, last recorded in company with the Sirius at midnight; the Entreprenante with the British, near the Victory; the Furet to leeward (east) of Villeneuve's own squadron, about the Bucentaure and the Santísima Trinidad; the Argus on the lee side of Gravina's Príncipe de Asturias. Low.
- **Cádiz bay, 19 October, 7–8.15 a.m.** Three French brigs lay in the bay with the fleet: the Argus, the Furet and the Observateur. None sailed that day. High for their presence; High that the Argus and the Observateur stayed in, Medium-High for the Furet. Their berths are not recorded. Low for any berth.
- **Off the port at that hour.** The British brig-sloop Weazle and the schooner Pickle were in company with the Euryalus and Sirius, a few miles south-west of San Sebastián, until 9 a.m. French observers also saw a brig and a schooner with the British frigates in those days. If the harbour view shows those frigates, it should show these two as well. Medium.
- **Not there.** The Observateur stayed at anchor in Cádiz by Villeneuve's order (High). The Weazle left on the 19th with the news for Gibraltar and Tetuan, to bring out Louis's ships, and missed the battle (High). No French schooner (Medium) and no Spanish small craft (High) sailed with the combined fleet.
- **Not settled for the harbour view.** Two kinds of small craft may have been in the bay on the 19th and are not in this note's lists. (a) Spanish gunboats: James has a strong force of them drawn up across the harbour mouth on the evening of the 18th (James III 385); two Spanish sources have the gunboat stations withdrawn and the small craft taken in that day (annexes pp. 100, 383). Contested. (b) The Spanish frigate Flora and the corvettes Mercurio and Indagadora came from Ferrol with the combined fleet in August (Desbrière p. 99), and so did the French schooner Téméraire (p. 98). None sailed on 19–20 October. Where they lay on the 19th was not checked. Low.
- **Counts.** Dawn: British 27 of the line, 4 frigates, 1 schooner, 1 cutter (33 hulls; 34 if the brig is drawn); allied 33 of the line, 5 frigates, 2 brigs (40 hulls). Cádiz bay on the 19th: the combined fleet's 33 of the line, 5 frigates and 3 brigs (41 hulls), of which the Euryalus counted 19 under way by 8 a.m. (NRS II 146). That count leaves out any Spanish small craft, gunboats and merchant ships.

## 1. Who was with each fleet at first light on 21 October

### British

- **Pickle and Entreprenante.** Collingwood's printed order of attack names the four frigates, the schooner Pickle and the cutter Entreprenante after the ships of the line; all six stand in the van column (London Gazette, 6 November 1805; Nicolas VII 215, scan checked). His journal names the same at noon (NRS II 202). James lists both (James III 384, 1902 ed.), and so does the NRS list (NRS II 144). Nicolas's summary gives the force off Cádiz on the 19th and 21st as 27 of the line, four frigates, a schooner and a cutter (VII 130). At daylight the Conqueror logged the fleet as 27 of the line, 4 frigates, 1 schooner and 1 cutter (NRS II 257). The Leviathan agrees (NRS II 267). The Dreadnought and the Swiftsure count 26 of the line with the same small craft at noon (NRS II 235, 282); probably they left out the Africa, which had parted in the night and came in from the north about 11 (Collingwood's journal, NRS II 202). Lieutenant George Browne of the Victory gives the same make-up for the fleet on the 19th, in a letter home (NRS II 194). High.
- **Commanders and logs.**
  - Pickle: Lieutenant John Richards Lapenotière (Nicolas VII 142, 346; the NRS list prints "John Richard", II 144). Her printed log is that of the acting second master, whose name NRS prints as "G. Along" (NRS II 317, scan checked). A modern reading of the manuscript log (ADM 52/3669, maritimeviews.co.uk) gives George Almy. Medium for the name.
  - Entreprenante: Lieutenant Robert Benjamin Young (Nicolas VII 142). James and the NRS list print "John Puver" (James III 384; NRS II 144); Nicolas notes that James is wrong on this (VII 346 n.). Her log was kept by William Adeane, second master and pilot (NRS II 319). High.
- **No other British warship below frigate size.** No list or log names one. The Weazle was sent to Gibraltar and Tetuan with the news on the morning of the 19th (Euryalus, NRS II 146; Sirius, NRS II 312). Blackwood wrote to his wife that day that he had sent a vessel to Gibraltar to bring out Louis's ships (Nicolas VII 131 n.); Dallas (1816, as cited by Wikipedia) has the same. High.
- **Stray counts, and what they mean.**
  - **The third small sail.** The Orion's master logged the British fleet with frigates, "a brig", a schooner and a cutter (NRS II 278). The French Hermione counted at first light 33 sail to windward: 26 of the line, 4 frigates and 3 "corvettes ou avisos" (annexes p. 243, scan checked). The Hortense at 6.45 counted 27 of the line, 4 frigates and three small avisos (annexes p. 189, scan checked). Against them: Prigny counted 27 of the line, 4 frigates and 2 avisos (annexes p. 141); the Aigle 29 ships, 4 frigates and 2 corvettes (annexes p. 224; the scan has 2, the archive.org text 3); the Redoutable 27 of the line, 4 frigates and a schooner (annexes p. 198); the French Swiftsure 3 frigates and a cutter (annexes p. 247); the Fougueux 27 of the line and 6 frigates or corvettes, 33 sail in all (annexes p. 213); and every British list. Two readings:
    - The first draft's: the Hermione's total, 33, is the British naval total (27 + 4 + 2), so one of her three small craft was probably a ship of the line misjudged at a distance, perhaps the Africa. This does not explain the Hortense, who counted all 27 of the line.
    - The checker's: a third small sail was in sight, most likely the brig the Agamemnon had taken. The Agamemnon boarded a brig on the morning of the 19th and put a party into her (NRS II 299). She took her in tow early on the 20th, and by her own log cast off the tow when the enemy was sighted about 8 (NRS II 300). The Euryalus saw her at 8.20 with a heavy brig in tow, and still towing when lost to sight after 9.10 (NRS II 147–148). James calls the brig a deeply laden merchant prize, kept in tow (James III 387). A prize with a prize crew could follow the fleet on her own after the tow was cast off; British fleet counts would leave a merchant prize out.
    - Nothing places the brig on the 21st. Desbrière identifies her with the American the Héros took into Cádiz on the morning of the 20th (pp. 162–163). That vessel was the one the Sirius stopped and boarded, which the Sirius and the Euryalus call an American ship, from Belfast for Gibraltar (NRS II 147, 313); the French Rhin, Thémis and Hermione describe an American merchant ship taken from a British frigate, and none links her to the Agamemnon (annexes pp. 208, 242, 274). Desbrière's identification is therefore doubtful. Not a warship. Low–Medium for a third small sail; Low for its identity.
  - The Britannia's journal (Lieutenant John Barclay) calls the two small craft "2 cutters" (NRS II 213). A matter of wording. High.
  - The Polyphemus's log, as Nicolas prints it, has the Pickle and a "Guernsey Schooner" picking up the Achille's men (Nicolas VII 156 n.). The Spartiate's log names the Pickle and the Entreprenante there (NRS II 251); the Prince's log saw a schooner and a cutter saving men (NRS II 229); the Entreprenante's log has her boats saving upwards of 169 men (NRS II 320); the Achille's surviving officers saw a cutter and a schooner come up to help (annexes p. 265); James agrees (III 441). The "Guernsey schooner" is almost certainly the Entreprenante, misnamed. Medium-High.

### Combined fleet

- **Argus and Furet.** At 8 a.m. the Euryalus counted 33 of the line, five frigates and two brigs (NRS II 149). James names the brigs Argus and Furet (James III 386). Nicolas's summary: the French had five frigates and two brigs, the Spaniards none (VII 130). Both commanders wrote reports (annexes pp. 173–176, 268–270). High.
- **Their stations in the line of battle.** The line sent to all ships on 6 October puts the Furet abreast the Santísima Trinidad, next to the Hortense abreast the Bucentaure, in Villeneuve's own squadron. It puts the Argus abreast the French Achille, next to the Thémis abreast the Príncipe de Asturias, in Gravina's own division of the squadron of observation; the Hermione stands with Magon's division, abreast the Algésiras (annexes p. 124, scan checked). High.
- **Commanders.** Furet: lieutenant de vaisseau Demay. Argus: lieutenant de vaisseau Taillard (annexes pp. 86, 173, 268). High.
- **The third brig stayed in port.** The Observateur (lieutenant de vaisseau Croizé) was ordered by Villeneuve to stay at anchor and not follow the fleet; her seamen were taken out of her (her captain's letter from Cádiz, 20 October, 7 p.m., annexes pp. 158–159). Villeneuve's own letter of 18 October gives the reason: he was leaving the brig because she sailed badly, and putting her seamen into the Indomptable and her gunners into the Algésiras (annexes p. 102). The line of 6 October had placed her with Dumanoir's squadron, abreast the Rayo, and Desbrière marks her as left in Cádiz (annexes p. 124; Desbrière p. 174). High.
- **No schooner, no Spanish small craft.** The French force that left Ferrol on 13 August listed the three brigs and a schooner, the Téméraire, which had joined at Ferrol, though Desbrière's tally under the list counts only three corvettes (Desbrière p. 98). Villeneuve's state of the French squadron at Cádiz on 16 September lists only the three brigs (annexes pp. 85–86). No source read puts a schooner with the fleet in October. Medium. The Spanish ships had no frigates or small craft with them in the sortie (Nicolas VII 130; James III 386); the brigs in the Spanish table of the fleet are the two French brigs (annexes pp. 100–101). High.
- **Stray counts.** British logs give the enemy's small craft as two brigs (Euryalus, Britannia, Neptune, Dreadnought, Colossus, Leviathan, Spartiate, Achille; the Polyphemus and Sirius as Nicolas prints them), one brig (Belleisle, Defiance, Orion, Revenge), or "brigs 2" at noon and "corvettes 2" in the evening (Swiftsure) (NRS II 149, 213, 232, 235, 238, 241, 249, 253, 265, 267, 271, 278, 282–283; Nicolas VII 145 n., 155 n.). The Pickle saw two brigs on the afternoon of the 20th (NRS II 317). Two is right. High.

## 2. Where each stood at first light (about 6 a.m.)

No log fixes any of the four at dawn. Below is what the records give, then the most defensible estimate.

### Pickle

- **Evidence.**
  - From 5.40 p.m. on the 20th she repeated the Sirius's signals to the fleet. From 8 p.m. to midnight she tacked about with the Sirius in company, the enemy in sight to the east (NRS II 317; Nicolas VII 135 n.). Her log does not name the Sirius after midnight.
  - At 1.30 a.m. on the 21st she took in her foresail and hove to. The enemy then bore ENE and the Commander-in-Chief SW by W (NRS II 317), so she lay roughly on the line between the two fleets.
  - She made sail at 3. At 5 she wore towards the enemy, swayed up her fore and fore topsail yards and set the fore topsail and topgallant sail (NRS II 317).
  - At 8 the enemy bore E, 4 or 5 leagues, and the Commander-in-Chief WSW (NRS II 317). Nicolas's printing of the same log gives the Commander-in-Chief at "2 or 3 leagues" (Nicolas VII 145 n.); the two printings differ in other details too (for the 20th, four frigates in NRS, five in Nicolas). Her distances are high: the Euryalus put the British van 8 or 9 miles from the enemy's centre at 8 (NRS II 149). The bearings are more useful than the distances.
  - The Sirius bore up at 5.10, wore and hove to at 6.30, and by 8 lay a mile from the Victory, her captain on board the flagship (NRS II 313; Nicolas VII 145 n.). So by 8 the two were some miles apart.
  - A modern summary of the manuscript log (ADM 52/3669) gives her noon reckoning as Cádiz N 30° E, 28 miles (maritimeviews.co.uk). That would put her west or south-west of the battle at noon. It does not fit the morning bearings and is taken as a rough reckoning. Low.
- **Estimate.** Between the fleets, about 6–8 nm NE by E (true) of the Victory and 4–6 nm west of the allied line, near the Sirius. With the chart's Victory at 36°08.0′ N 6°28.0′ W and Sirius at 36°12.0′ N 6°20.4′ W, about 36°11.5′ N 6°21.0′ W: 6.7 nm at 058° true from the Victory, within about a point of her own 8 a.m. bearing of the Victory (WSW by compass, about 226° true). Low.
- **Heading and sail.** Standing east towards the enemy, under fore topsail and topgallant over her fore-and-aft sails (NRS II 317). Medium.

### Entreprenante

- **Evidence.** Her printed log starts on the 21st. At daylight she saw the enemy to the east and counted 35 sail, all taken for ships of the line. The next entry, that the fleet wore and she stood towards them, may mean the British fleet or the enemy, who wore about 8 (NRS II 319–320). Either way she was with the British fleet, too far off to tell the enemy's frigates from his ships of the line. On the evening of the 19th Nelson sent her to the Belleisle and Polyphemus to repeat an order, so she was then close to the Victory (Beatty 1807, pp. 7–8, checked; Nicolas VII 131). After the battle Collingwood used "the schooner and cutter" to carry his orders (NRS II 204). Nothing places her at dawn.
- **Later stations, for what they suggest.** All three refer to the approach, late in the morning, not to dawn. Low.
  - Collingwood's order of attack prints the frigates and both small craft in the van column (Nicolas VII 215). It is a list, not a plan.
  - Pocock's painting of the opening of the battle (c. 1808, made for Clarke and M'Arthur's Life of Nelson) shows the Pickle and the Entreprenante among the British vessels attending Nelson's line, on the side nearer the viewer, who looks from the north-east (RMG BHC0548 catalogue). A modern summary also puts the Pickle north-west of the weather line (Wikipedia, HMS Pickle).
  - The Harbin plan, a coloured manuscript plan said to have belonged to a petty officer present at the battle, numbers the Pickle 5 and the Entreprenante 6 and draws them between the two British columns, with the Euryalus, Phoebe and Sirius north of Nelson's column and the Naiad astern of it (Corbett 1910, frontispiece and p. 452). Corbett trusts it for Collingwood's line, not for Nelson's.
  - The first draft cited an unnamed modern account placing the cutter with the lee division. No source for it was found, and the van column of the printed list and Pocock's picture both point the other way. Dropped.
- **Estimate.** With Nelson's body, within about 1 nm of the Victory on her lee (east) side, as the admiral's tender: about 36°07.6′ N 6°26.9′ W. An equally defensible alternative is between the two British bodies, 1–2 nm south of the Victory. Heading with the British, on the chart's 047°. Low.

### Furet

- **Evidence.**
  - At 8.30 p.m. on the 20th Villeneuve sent her along the whole line to tell the captains to close the line and keep their wind. At 10.30 p.m. Magon gave her a message for Villeneuve: the Achille had seen 18 ships in line of battle (her report, annexes p. 174, scan checked).
  - At 6 a.m. she saw the British in the west, 26 sail, running down before the wind with studding sails set (annexes p. 174; the scan has 26, the archive.org text 36).
  - Her station in the line of 6 October was with Villeneuve's squadron, abreast the Santísima Trinidad, with the Hortense by the Bucentaure (annexes p. 124). From about 8.30 she and the Hortense were to leeward of that squadron (Desbrière p. 174).
  - Her noon reckoning put the fleet 10 leagues S ¼ SW of Cádiz, in 36° N (annexes p. 174). Her longitude, 10°55′ W, is printed so both in the report and in Desbrière's footnote (p. 174); it fits no meridian tried (Paris, Greenwich, Cádiz) and is not used.
- **Estimate.** Near the Bucentaure and the Santísima Trinidad in the middle of the allied main body, 0.5–1 nm on their lee (east) side. With the main body centred near 36°09.7′ N 6°13.7′ W, about 36°09.5′ N 6°12.7′ W. Heading with her fleet, 180–195° true. Low.

### Argus

- **Evidence.**
  - At 3 p.m. on the 20th Villeneuve ordered her back to her post near Gravina. From 5 p.m. she sailed near Gravina, under his orders (her report, annexes p. 269).
  - At 7.15 p.m. Gravina sent her to tell Villeneuve that the Achille had seen 18 enemy ships forming a line of battle ahead of the fleet. By 9.15 she was back with Villeneuve's answer: form the line on the ship furthest to leeward, whatever the stations. Gravina then sent her to tell the ships astern to form on him; he hoisted a light at his mizzen, and she wore and went down the line to pass the word (annexes p. 269). Prigny and the Bucentaure's captain record her passing under the flagship's stern, at 10.30–11 p.m. and 8.30 p.m. respectively (annexes pp. 139, 178). The Príncipe de Asturias's journals record a French brig alongside at 9.15 with Villeneuve's order (annexes pp. 379, 384).
  - At daybreak she saw the British, 29 sail, bearing from NNW to NE, running large on the larboard tack (annexes p. 269; the scan reads so). Gravina's flagship had them from W to WNW (W to NNW in her second journal), 4 or 5 miles off (annexes pp. 379, 384). The Cornélie, close to leeward of the van, had them in the NW (annexes p. 159). The Argus's bearing does not fit the others and is set aside.
  - Desbrière has Gravina take the head of the line in the night, with the Thémis and the Argus near him (p. 165). At daybreak both of the Príncipe's journals put her at the head of a badly formed line (annexes pp. 379, 384), and Gravina ordered his squadron to take the head (Desbrière p. 166). By the opening of the battle the Argus was to leeward of the fleet; the Thémis joined her there, under the lee of the Príncipe de Asturias, at about 10 (annexes p. 269; Desbrière p. 174).
- **Estimate.** Within about 1 nm of the Príncipe de Asturias, on her lee (east) side. She may as well have been anywhere along the line after carrying Gravina's word down it in the night. Heading with her fleet. Low.

### Out of the dawn frame

- The Observateur at anchor in Cádiz bay. If the last frame takes in the bay, she lies there. High.
- The Weazle in the Strait or at Gibraltar. High that she was not with the fleet; her exact place is unknown.

## 3. Movements, 1 to 21 October

### Pickle

- **14 September.** Nelson, at St Helens, sent orders for ten vessels whose whereabouts he did not know, the Entreprenante and the Pickle among them (Nelson to Marsden, Nicolas VII 36–37). High.
- **About 22 September.** Left Plymouth: she was seven days out when off Cape St Vincent (Nicolas VII 57). Medium.
- **29 September.** Off Cape St Vincent on that Sunday, looking for a ship on that station (Nelson to Collingwood, 30 September, Nicolas VII 57). A modern summary of the London Gazette has her take the American brig Indefatigable that day (Wikipedia). High; Low for the capture.
- **1 October.** Joined the fleet from Plymouth (Nelson to Marsden, 2 October, Nicolas VII 64). The fleet then kept 13–19 leagues west of Cádiz (Nelson's letters, Nicolas VII 96, 110). High.
- **5 October.** Nelson told Collingwood he had sent her to Blackwood off Cádiz until he had more frigates (Nicolas VII 77). High.
- **8 October.** Nelson hoped to keep her with Blackwood, to protect his boats at night (Nicolas VII 88). High.
- **9 October.** She was then with Collingwood's division and went off to Cádiz from him (Nelson to Collingwood, postscript, VII 95). Blackwood was told to keep her for night work close inshore (VII 96, the note identifying her). Nelson's diary: sent to Blackwood with orders to keep a good look-out (VII 97). High.
- **10–19 October.** In Blackwood's inshore squadron: five frigates, the Weazle and the Pickle (Nicolas VII 110). The French saw five frigates, a brig and a schooner close off the port (Desbrière pp. 141, 150). By day the squadron kept 2–8 nm west and south-west of San Sebastián; at night the Pickle worked close inshore (the project's notes; Nicolas VII 96). Marshall's Royal Naval Biography (Supplement, 1828) says that a few days before the battle she took a Portuguese settee carrying bullocks from Tangier; Nelson's letter to Blackwood of 8 October speaks of a Portuguese and his bullocks without naming the captor (VII 88). Medium for the settee. A modern statement that she came close enough to count the ships in the harbour has no period source found. Low.
- **19 October, 7–9 a.m.** With the Euryalus and Sirius off the port. At 9 Blackwood ordered her to Cape Spartel, to tell all ships the enemy was out, cruise there three days and then come back (Euryalus, NRS II 146). The Sirius logged the Weazle and Pickle parting company at 9 (NRS II 312). At noon both bore SSE of the Euryalus, which was then about 36°28′ N 6°27′ W, in a calm (NRS II 146). High.
- **Night of 19–20 October.** Making for Cape Spartel (35°47′ N 5°55′ W), about 45–48 nm from the port. No log is printed for it. Medium.
- **20 October.** Back with the fleet by the early afternoon, long before her three days were up. At daybreak the fleet lay between Trafalgar and Spartel (Browne, NRS II 194; the project's notes), so she probably met it there in the morning. At 2 p.m. she answered a signal to reconnoitre to the north-west; at 3 she sighted the enemy N by E, standing south-west; at 4 the Commander-in-Chief bore SSW; from 5.40 she repeated the Sirius's signals; from 8 p.m. to midnight she kept company with the Sirius (NRS II 317; Nicolas VII 135 n.). Desbrière counts her among the British vessels keeping south and south-west of the allied track that evening (p. 164). High for the afternoon; Low for where she met the fleet.
- **21 October.** See section 2.

### Entreprenante

- **4 October, morning.** Joined the fleet from Portsmouth with the Admiralty's letter of 16 September; Nelson took her under his command (Nelson to Marsden, 4 October, Nicolas VII 74–75; VII 66 n.). High.
- **5–18 October.** Not recorded. On 8 October Nelson feared that "the Cutter" would never beat up to Blackwood (VII 88); if that is she, the main fleet kept her. Inference: with the main fleet as a tender. Low.
- **19 October, evening.** With Nelson in the chase to the south-east; sent to the Belleisle and Polyphemus to repeat an order (Beatty 1807, pp. 7–8; Nicolas VII 131). High.
- **20 October.** Not recorded. Low.
- **21 October.** With the fleet at daylight (NRS II 319–320). High.

### Weazle (for the harbour view of the 19th)

- Cruizer-class brig-sloop of 18 guns, Commander Peter Parker (Winfield 2008, via Wikipedia). Nelson meant to send her to Blackwood on 8 October and hoped she had joined by the 9th (Nicolas VII 87, 95–96); she was with him by the 10th (VII 110). On 16 October Nelson had the enemy's state "by signal from Weazel" (VII 126). On the 19th, between about 8.10 and 9 a.m., Blackwood sent her by telegraph with the news to Gibraltar and Tetuan; she parted company at 9 and at noon bore SSE of the Euryalus with the Pickle (NRS II 146, 312). The French frigate Hermione, coming out that morning, saw a ship and a brig-corvette make off WSW firing guns and making signals (annexes p. 242); the brig was probably the Weazle. High for the 19th; Low for the Hermione's identification.

### Argus, Furet and Observateur

- **In Cádiz from 20 August.** All three came in with Villeneuve from Ferrol (Desbrière p. 98; annexes pp. 85–86). The Argus had been with Villeneuve since the night of 8–9 April 1805, when she came out of Cádiz with the Aigle and the corvette Torche to join him (James III 342). No source read sends any of the three out of the bay before the sortie. Medium.
- **9–10 October.** The fleet, all but the San Fulgencio, moved to the harbour entrance (James III 385; the project's notes). That the brigs moved with it is an inference. Low.
- **18 October.** At 5–5.15 p.m. the signal to unmoor and hoist in boats (Argus and Furet reports, annexes pp. 173, 268). When the signal to heave short came at 10.30 p.m., the Argus was already at a long stay, held by a small kedge (annexes p. 268). That evening Villeneuve wrote that he was leaving the Observateur behind (annexes p. 102). High.
- **19 October.** At 6 a.m. the Argus was ready to sail, topsails hoisted, when the signal to weigh went up (annexes p. 268). At 8.30 the Observateur's captain brought her an order to sail only with the last ship of the combined fleet (annexes p. 268). Neither brig came out that day. The Observateur says only eight French ships of the line and two frigates got out (annexes p. 158); the Furet says 14 sail, ships of the line and frigates (annexes pp. 173–174); at 4 p.m. Villeneuve signalled the Swiftsure and all the French frigates, not the brigs, to get under way (annexes p. 174). No report names a brig outside. James gives the whole force as 33 of the line, five frigates and two brigs in describing the sortie of the 20th, without saying on which day the brigs sailed (James III 385). High for the Argus; Medium-High for the Furet.
- **20 October.** The signal to weigh came at 5.45–6 a.m.; by 8 the whole fleet but the Rayo was under sail (annexes pp. 158, 174, 268). The Furet gives no hour for herself; she came out with the main body. Medium. The Argus weighed at 8.30, stood off and on under topsails to wait for the Rayo, sent a boat to her at 9, then made all sail after the fleet once the Rayo was under way. At 11 a.m. San Sebastián tower bore SE 5° E, 8 miles; she was running W ¼ NW at 5½ knots, with two reefs in her topsails, in squalls (annexes pp. 268–269). Worked by compass, that is about 36°34′ N 6°28′ W; 36°37′ N 6°27′ W if the bearing is true. She reported to Villeneuve at 3 p.m. and was near Gravina by 5 (annexes p. 269). High for the facts; Medium for the 11 a.m. position.
- **Night of 20–21 October.** As in section 2. High.
- **Observateur.** At anchor in the bay throughout (annexes pp. 102, 158). High.

### The harbour view, 19 October, 7–8.15 a.m.

- **In the bay.** Argus, Furet and Observateur at anchor. No source gives their berths. The brigs served the admirals as repeaters and messengers. A defensible layout puts each near the flagship she served, inside the lines with the frigates and clear of the fairway: the Furet near the Bucentaure, the Argus near the Príncipe de Asturias. For the Observateur there are two readings: near the Bucentaure, since her captain carried an order from the admiral that morning (annexes p. 268); or with Dumanoir's squadron, near the Formidable and the Rayo, her station in the line of 6 October (annexes p. 124). Low.
- **Sail state.** The Argus had her topsails hoisted from 6 a.m. (annexes p. 268). The others are not recorded. Medium.
- **Off the port.** The Weazle and the Pickle in company with the Euryalus (about 36°28.0′ N 6°21.0′ W at 5.30 a.m.) and the Sirius (Cádiz NE by E, 4 miles, at 4 a.m.) until 9 (NRS II 145–146, 312). Within 1–2 nm of the frigates. Medium.
- **Possibly in the bay, not drawn yet.** Spanish gunboats, and the Spanish frigate and corvettes from Ferrol; see the summary. Contested or unchecked. Low.

## 4. Build and rig, for the glyphs

### Pickle, topsail schooner

- **Origin.** Built in Bermuda about 1799 as the merchant schooner Sting; bought into the Navy at Jamaica in December 1800 for £2,500 (Winfield 2008, via threedecks.org and Wikipedia). The renaming is dated February 1801 by threedecks.org and by Hore (via Wikipedia), 1802 in Wikipedia's infobox. Medium-High.
- **Size.** 73 ft 0 in (22.25 m), on the gun deck by Winfield (via threedecks.org and Wikipedia), overall by Lyon (via maritimeviews.co.uk); keel 56 ft 3¾ in (17.16 m); breadth 20 ft 7½ in (6.29 m) by threedecks.org and Lyon, 20 ft 7¼ in by Wikipedia; depth in hold 9 ft 6 in (2.90 m); 127 tons burthen. The tonnage fits the keel and breadth by the builder's rule. High for the hull; Medium for whether 73 ft is the deck or the overall length.
- **Guns and men.** Winfield gives 8 × 12-pdr carronades. Ten guns in Marshall's Royal Naval Biography (for 1802), in the key to Pocock's painting (RMG BHC0548), in the key to the Harbin plan (Corbett 1910) and in a French table of the British line, which makes them carronades (annexes p. 184). A modern summary gives six carronades (maritimeviews.co.uk). Men: an establishment of 30 (Winfield), 35 (Marshall, for 1802), and 40 at Trafalgar by the crew lists cited on threedecks.org. Medium.
- **Rig.** Two masts with gaff foresail and gaff mainsail, the main on a long boom; square sails on the foremast only. Her log names a fore yard, a fore topsail yard, the fore topsail and topgallant sail, the mainsail, the foresail, a middle jib, and the jib set on the middle jib boom (NRS II 317–319). A model made before 1858 by William Haines for the Greenwich Trafalgar scene shows her as a topsail schooner square-rigged on the foremast only, at 1.40 p.m. on the day (RMG SLR2745.19). Steel's merchant schooner of 110 tons crosses topsail and topgallant yards on both masts (Steel 1794, I, "Mastmaking", p. 52), so square sails on the main were not unknown; nothing in the Pickle's log points to them. Bermuda-built schooners carried strongly raked masts; Steel says only that a schooner's masts rake aft and her bowsprit lies nearly level; no figure for her rake was found. Medium for the sails; Low for the rake.
- **Spars, estimated.** Steel's table for a merchant schooner of 110 tons gives: main mast 75 ft, main topmast (his "topgallant mast") 32 ft, fore mast 66 ft, fore topmast 26 ft, bowsprit 50 ft, main boom 47 ft, main gaff 28 ft, fore gaff 22 ft, square-sail yard 38 ft (Steel 1794, I, "Mastmaking", p. 52, scan checked). For 127 tons add about 5 %. Low.
- **Height.** Main truck about 25–30 m (80–100 ft) above the water; the fore truck about nine-tenths of that. Steel's schooner gives about 28–30 m. A modern Baltimore-schooner replica, Pride of Baltimore II (about 96 ft on deck, rig height 107 ft), scaled to her deck gives about 24–25 m. Low.
- **Length with spars.** About 35–38 m from jib-boom end to boom end. Low.

### Entreprenante, cutter

- **Origin.** French-built privateer cutter, probably of 1797, taken in 1798 and bought in November 1798 (Winfield 2008 and Demerliac, via Wikipedia and threedecks.org). Registered on 10 January 1799 by threedecks.org, 1 June 1799 by Wikipedia; in commission from February 1799 (Wikipedia), and in the Mediterranean by May 1799 (Nelson to St Vincent, 8–9 May 1799, Nicolas VII, addenda p. clxxxi). Medium.
- **Size.** 67 ft 0 in (20.42 m), given as gun deck by threedecks.org and as overall by Wikipedia; keel 51 ft 6 in (15.70 m); breadth 21 ft 6 in (6.55 m); draught 9 ft and 11 ft (2.74 and 3.35 m), read as forward and aft by threedecks.org and as light and laden by Wikipedia; 126 59/94 tons burthen, which fits the keel and breadth (Winfield 2008). High for the hull.
- **Guns and men.** 10 × 12-pdr carronades from December 1803, at first 10 × 4-pdrs; 40 men (Winfield 2008, via threedecks.org). Ten in the key to the Harbin plan (Corbett 1910); eight in the key to Pocock's painting (RMG BHC0548); twelve carronades in the French table of the British line (annexes p. 184). In the gale of 25 October she threw five guns overboard (NRS II 321). Medium.
- **Rig.** One mast with a fidded topmast; a large gaff mainsail on a long boom reaching well past the stern; a foresail (staysail) and a jib on a long running bowsprit lying nearly level; a square topsail, and a square sail set flying when running. Her log names the mainsail, trysail, foresail and storm jib (NRS II 320–321). Steel describes the sails of single-masted vessels as a boom mainsail abaft the mast and, before it, a square sail, a small topsail, a topsail and a topgallant sail (Steel 1794, I, sail-making, description of sails). He gives a single-masted vessel's mast a rake aft of 1½ in to the yard, about 2½°, and a bowsprit lying nearly level (Steel 1794, I, "Mastmaking", p. 2). Thomas Buttersworth's painting of her after the battle (public domain; undated) shows one tall mast with a topmast and a crossed topsail yard, gaff mainsail, staysail and jib. Medium.
- **Height.** Steel's rule for sloops, smacks and hoys makes mast and topmast in one three and three-quarter times the breadth (Steel 1794, I, "Mastmaking", p. 41, scan checked): about 81 ft for her. His merchant sloop of 70 tons carries a topgallant mast (a cutter's fidded topmast) of about half the mast (p. 52), about 38 ft for her. That puts the truck near 31 m. The 1991 replica of the Saint-Malo privateer cutter Renard, hull 19 m, has an air draught of 26 m (French Wikipedia); scaled to her hull, about 28 m, though the original Renard was a much lighter vessel. Estimate 27–31 m (90–100 ft) with the topmast up. Low.
- **Length with spars.** The Renard replica is 30 m overall on a 19 m hull. For the Entreprenante about 32 m. Low.

### Furet, brig-corvette

- **Origin.** Built at Toulon to Pestel's Abeille design (Roche, via Wikipedia; threedecks.org calls the class Sylphe). Laid down September 1801. Launched 24 December 1801 by Wikipedia (Roche), 25 February 1802 by threedecks.org; Roche has her commissioned on 25 February 1802, so the second date is probably her commissioning. Taken by HMS Hydra off Cádiz on 27 February 1806. Medium.
- **Size.** 95 ft 8 in on the gun deck, moulded breadth 27 ft 7 in (threedecks.org). Read as English feet, 29.2 × 8.4 m; if they are French feet, as their source may be, 31.1 × 9.0 m, close to the class figures in Roche: 32 m long, 8.7 m broad, 3.5 m draught, 350 tonnes, about 750 m² of sail (via Wikipedia). Low-Medium.
- **Guns and men.** 16 French 6-pdrs as built and 18 French 8-pdrs in 1806 (threedecks.org); 16 × 8-pdrs in Wikipedia's infobox; an 18-gun brig-corvette in James (III 238); 18 guns and 130 men when taken, given by the British as 9-pdrs (London Gazette, 1806, via Wikipedia). Villeneuve rated her a good little vessel, fit for any mission (annexes p. 86). Medium.
- **Rig.** Brig: two masts. Foremast: course, topsail and topgallant (royals possible). Mainmast: a large gaff-and-boom mainsail as the lower sail, with square topsail and topgallant above; Steel describes a brig's mainsail so (Steel 1794, I, sail-making, description of sails), and his merchant brig crosses a cross-jack yard on the main (p. 52). Whether French naval brigs also set a square main course is not settled here. Staysails between the masts; jibs on bowsprit and jib boom. Pocock's painting of the opening of the battle shows her among three French cruisers in the left foreground, named in the museum's catalogue with the Cornélie and Hortense (RMG BHC0548). Medium for the rig; Low-Medium for a square main course.

### Argus, brig

- **Origin.** Vigilant class, to Forfait's design; built at Le Havre by Fouache and Thibaudier; laid down July 1799, launched 20 July 1800, in commission 1802 (Winfield and Roberts 2015, via Wikipedia and threedecks.org). With Villeneuve since 8–9 April 1805 (James III 342; Desbrière p. 27). High.
- **Size.** 29.2 m long, 8.4 m broad, 3.5 m draught (Wikipedia's infobox). Her sister the Observateur, measured by the British after her capture in 1806: 90 ft 6 in (27.6 m), keel 72 ft 8 in (22.1 m), breadth 28 ft 0 in (8.5 m), depth in hold 7 ft 6 in, 303 tons burthen (Winfield 2008, via Wikipedia; Wikipedia labels the 90 ft 6 in as overall). The tonnage fits the keel and breadth. Medium.
- **Guns and men.** Nominally 16. Wikipedia's infobox gives 14 bronze 8-pdrs (citing Winfield and Roberts) and a complement of 84; threedecks.org gives 16 × 4-pdrs as built and about 110 men at Trafalgar; the British reported 14 brass 8-pdrs and 120 men in 1807 (London Gazette, via Wikipedia). James calls her a 16-gun brig-corvette (III 349). Villeneuve: sails fairly well, except in heavy weather (annexes p. 86). Medium.
- **Rig.** As the Furet. Medium.

### Brig heights and mast placing

- Steel's rule for Royal Navy ships makes the main mast half the sum of the lower-deck length and the breadth, with the topmast and topgallant mast in fixed proportions to it (Steel 1794, I, "Mastmaking", pp. 39–40). Steel gives it for ships; applied to the brigs, it makes their main masts about 59–62 ft. His merchant brig of 150 tons has a 60 ft main mast, 30 ft topmast and 16½ ft topgallant mast (p. 52), so the figure is not too high. With the deck about 2 m above the water and the usual doublings, the main truck stands about 29–33 m (95–108 ft) above the water. The foremast is a little lower. Low-Medium.
- A brig's main mast stands about two-thirds of her length abaft the stem head and her foremast three-twentieths; the main rakes aft about 1°, the fore barely at all (Steel 1794, I, "Mastmaking", p. 2, checked). Medium.

### Weazle (if drawn on the 19th)

- Cruizer-class brig-sloop: 100 ft 0 in (30.5 m), keel 77 ft 3½ in, breadth 30 ft 6 in (9.3 m), depth in hold 12 ft 9 in, 382 tons burthen; 16 × 32-pdr carronades and 2 × 6-pdrs; 121 men (Winfield 2008, via Wikipedia; Wikipedia labels the 100 ft as overall). Main truck by Steel's rule about 33–35 m. Medium for the size; Low for the height.

### Relative sizes for the glyph types

Against the chart's Victory (gun deck 56.7 m, main truck 60.2 m above the waterline, `victory-1805.ts`):

- Brigs: hull about 0.49–0.52; main truck about 0.48–0.55.
- Pickle: hull about 0.39; main truck about 0.42–0.50.
- Entreprenante: hull about 0.36; main truck about 0.45–0.52.
- Weazle: hull about 0.54; main truck about 0.55–0.58.
- The chart's frigate type is 0.74 in both.

The small craft carry rigs tall for their hulls. At glyph size: a brig reads as a ship without a mizzen, square sails stacked on two masts with a big boom sail aft; the schooner as a low hull under two raked fore-and-aft sails with small square sails at the fore; the cutter as one tall mast with a huge mainsail, a long bowsprit and a long boom. Medium for the hull ratios; Low for the heights.

## 5. Side notes outside the brief

- **Gravina's place at dawn.** The Príncipe de Asturias's journals put her at the head of the allied line at daybreak (annexes pp. 379, 384); Desbrière has her take the head in the night (p. 165), and Prigny saw the squadron of observation manoeuvre to take the head at dawn (annexes p. 139). On the starboard tack, heading south, that is the line's south end. Medium.
- **French frigates at dawn.** By their reports the Cornélie took post close to leeward of the van at 11.30 p.m. (annexes p. 159), while the Hermione and Thémis were to windward of the line, reconnoitring, from Villeneuve's signal at 6.15 a.m. until near 10 (annexes p. 243; Desbrière pp. 166, 173–174, 184). The frigates went to leeward later in the morning. Medium.
- **Ships under way in the bay on the 19th.** The Euryalus saw 19 of the enemy under way at 8 a.m. (NRS II 146); the Argus saw 17 ships of the line under sail at 9, several of them anchoring again in the calm with their sails clewed up at the mastheads (annexes p. 268). How many of the line got out that day is contested: seven by Desbrière, who names Magon's Algésiras, Achille, Argonaute, Neptune, Héros, Duguay-Trouin and Bahama (p. 161); eight, one of them Spanish, by Villeneuve's postscript (annexes p. 102); eight French and two frigates by the Observateur (annexes p. 158); six or seven and three frigates by the Rhin (annexes p. 208); six and a frigate by the Príncipe de Asturias (annexes p. 383); twelve ships of all kinds by James (III 385) and Nicolas (VII 130); nine at 4 p.m. and twelve at 6 p.m. by the Euryalus (NRS II 146). Medium for "seven or eight".
- **Topsails at anchor.** At daylight on the 19th eight allied ships had their topsails hoisted to the mastheads, and by 8 a.m. all but two (NRS II 145–146). The chart's anchored types have furled sails. Medium.

## What the check changed

- The noon count of 26 of the line with the schooner and cutter is the Dreadnought's (NRS II 235) and the Swiftsure's (II 282), not the Neptune's.
- The Pickle's printed log is headed "G. Along", not George Almy; Almy is a modern reading of the manuscript.
- The Hortense also counted three small British craft; the dismissal of a third small sail is withdrawn and the question left to the owner.
- The Aigle's count is two corvettes on the scan, not three as the OCR text has it.
- Collingwood's printed list puts the small craft in the van column; the unnamed "lee division" reading is dropped; Pocock and the Harbin plan added.
- The settee with bullocks has a 19th-century source (Marshall); the ship count in the harbour has none found.
- The Weazle's errand to bring out Louis's ships rests on Blackwood's own letter of the 19th; raised to High.
- Villeneuve's letter of 18 October (Observateur left behind as a poor sailer) and the line of battle of 6 October (her station with Dumanoir) added.
- The brigs' and frigates' stations in the line of 6 October given exactly.
- The Argus was at a long stay, not hove short, at 10.30 p.m. on the 18th.
- James does not say on which day the brigs sailed.
- "Only seven of the line got out" on the 19th is contested; versions given.
- The Pickle is attested in company with the Sirius only until midnight; the Sirius was by the Victory at 8.
- Gun counts widened for the Pickle and the Entreprenante; the Entreprenante's lowered to Medium.
- The Furet's threedecks dimensions may be in French feet; confidence lowered.
- Steel's brig has a boom mainsail as her main lower sail; a square main course on the French brigs is left open.
- Steel's 110-ton schooner crosses square yards on both masts; noted against the Pickle's fore-only rig.
- Gaps for the harbour view: Spanish gunboats (contested) and the Spanish frigate and corvettes from Ferrol (unchecked).
