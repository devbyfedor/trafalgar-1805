# V-H3b: fact-check of the H3 signal notes, sections 3–7 (claims H3-15 to H3-32)

Notes checked: `C:/Users/User/AppData/Local/Temp/claude/d--dev-trafalgar-1805/81d46a93-68b9-4541-8365-f913ef045b3d/scratchpad/research/h3-signals.md`, lines 273–506 (sections 3–7). Nelson's night code of 20 October (H3-14, section 2.7) is also covered here, as the task asked.

**Method.**
- I re-read every period source the notes cite, and opened page scans to check page numbers and wording.
- I added sources the notes did not use:
  - the **Euryalus signal log** printed in NRS *Logs* II, pp. 160–168;
  - Lt **William Pryce Cumby**'s letter (Bellerophon), printed in *The Nineteenth Century*, November 1899;
  - Gilbert **Hudson**'s edition of Nelson's *Last Diary* (1917), transcribed from the original manuscript at the Probate Registry;
  - **Mahan**, *Life of Nelson* (1897), and **Clowes**, *The Royal Navy* vol. V (1900);
  - the DNB, FOTW and navigation-light sources for the anachronism list.

**Dates.**
- Jackson's NRS volume prints every log under civil-day headings, with a.m. before p.m. under each date.
- The contents fit the civil chronology. For example, the Euryalus has the topsails hoisted on the morning of the 19th, 12 ships out by that evening, and the rest out on the morning of the 20th.
- The log extracts in Nicolas VII 134–135 n. are also headed by the civil day (for example "Sunday 20th October … P.M." for the afternoon of the 20th).
- So the notes' dates are civil dates, and I found **no nautical-day error** in sections 3–7.
- Republican dates in Desbrière convert correctly: the Cornélie report of "28, 29, 30 vendémiaire et 1er brumaire an XIV" is 20–23 October 1805.

**Arithmetic.** Cádiz is at about 36° 32′ N, 6° 17′ W. The Victory at noon, 36° 36′ N 7° 30′ W, was 1° 13′ of longitude west, about 58 nm, and 4′ north: a true bearing from Cádiz of about 274°. With a westerly variation of about 20–21°, Cádiz from the Victory bears about 94° true, which is about 115° magnetic, which is E.S.E. by compass. So the original diary, Browne and the log agree. E.N.E. by compass would put Cádiz about 47° true, which is impossible from that latitude.

### H3-26 Seen from the Combined Fleet

- Desbrière, *Trafalgar* (1907), annexes:
  - Prigny 139: the French lit stern and top lights and fired rockets to mark their position. ## Important points the notes missed

1. **The Euryalus signal log (NRS II 160–168)** is the only timed record of the first links. It gives:
   - 6.04 telegraph and 7.00 No. 370 from the Sirius;
   - 7.20 No. 370 and No. 163 "West" to the Phoebe;
   - the Naiad as a repeater only from about 8.10;
   - the telegraph traffic later that morning;
   - the gun counts that went with day signals on the 20th (1, 2, 3 and 7 guns).
2. **Cumby's eyewitness account of the last link** (Nineteenth Century, Nov 1899, p. 719). The Mars was hull-down from the fleet ("topgallant masts alone"), so her numeral flags could not be read. She then made the **distant signal**: a flag, a ball and a pendant at different mastheads, independent of colour. The Victory answered her directly. This also answers the notes' open question 1 on the form of the 1805 "Distant Signals".
3. **The original diary** (Hudson 1917) settles two disputes. It reads E.S.E. on 19 Oct and "5 o'clock" for the Naiad's 31 sail on 20 Oct.
4. Corbett's own figure of **about 19 leagues** from the Victory's logged noon position, and Browne's "**Cadiz bearing east**, 16 leagues", are both missing from the distance and bearing table.
5. Nelson's order of 4 Oct to keep the connecting ships "as near as possible in the latitude" of Cádiz, so the chain lay on an east–west line.
6. Beatty p. 6 names the 19 Oct cordon as Colossus, Mars, Defence and **Agamemnon**. Beatty p. 11 has the fleet's second wear "about two o'clock in the morning".
7. The night chain did not work only by lights. Between about 0.20 and 2 a.m. the Phoebe hailed the Mars and the Naiad spoke the Colossus. On the evening of the 20th the look-out captains had been briefed aboard the Victory (Browne, NRS II 195).
8. The Combined Fleet also fired rockets, burned flares ("amorces") and made gun and lantern signals that night (Prigny; Lucas, Fraser 92). Nelson noted in 1804 that the French used rockets and blue lights freely.
9. Counts for the glyphs. In my count the British fleet on the 19th was 27 of the line, with 4 frigates, a schooner and a cutter (Browne; Cumby; Blackwood's letter; Leviathan and Conqueror on the 21st); Walker's "26 … under all sail" is the low count. The Combined Fleet had 12 ships out on the 19th (Euryalus and Sirius logs, evening). On the 20th the Euryalus saw "thirty-four ships of the enemy" at 8.50. On the 21st it had 33 of the line, 5 frigates and 2 brigs (Euryalus).
10. Minor:
    - The Euryalus's midnight bearing of the enemy differs between the transcriptions: NRS "SE by S", Nicolas "S.E. b. E.".
    - The Sirius log as printed has "At 9, tacked" before "At 7".
    - The 7.10 entry that Corbett read as "in ten minutes" is a general signal.

## What changes for the scene

1. **Line direction.** Run the 19 October chain **about due west (true) from Cádiz**, roughly along 36½° N. Start just SW–WSW of the town: the Sirius about 4 nm SW, the Euryalus 4–8 nm W–WSW of San Sebastián. End at the fleet **48–57 nm (16–19 leagues)** out, not WSW. Nelson ordered the connecting ships kept in Cádiz's latitude.
2. **Order and timing of the first pulse.**
   - The Sirius makes a telegraph hoist at 6.04, then No. 370 at 7.00.
   - The Euryalus repeats at 7.20 and sends the Phoebe west under all sail, firing three-minute guns. The Phoebe is a moving link.
   - Then the Defence and the Colossus (inferred), then the Mars, then the Victory at **9.30**.
   - Total **about 2½–3 h** of story time.
   - The Naiad lights up only after about 8.10, with telegraph hoists between the Euryalus and the Phoebe.
   - Date line: 19 October, morning.
3. **The last link looks different.** From the fleet the Mars is hull-down, so only the tops of her masts show above the horizon. Her coloured numeral flags are unreadable at that range. She switches to the **distant signal**: dark shapes, a flag, a **ball** and a pendant at different mastheads. The Victory answers, then hoists the general chase (one flag and the S.E. compass flag) with **two guns**.
4. **Gunsmoke counts.** Guns go with day signals in counted numbers (1, 2, 3; 7 to the Agamemnon on the 20th) and as three-minute guns from the ship carrying the news.
5. **Night of 20–21 October.**
   - Hourly **paired blue lights** are the dominant motif. The enemy stood south or SSW most of the night, which was the "two blue lights" case.
   - "Three guns, quick" appears only if the enemy stands west; the Sirius's three guns at 9.35 are a possible instance.
   - The British fleet wears at about 8 p.m. (No. 31, Victory burning blue lights) and at about 4 a.m. (gun flashes seen by the Africa). Beatty's 2 a.m. is a variant.
   - The British fleet is dark except the Admiral's light ("the light of the English fleet") and blue lights.
   - The Combined Fleet is a long string of stern and top lanterns, about six miles long. It shows its own rockets, flares and gun and lantern signals, especially around 9 p.m. and midnight.
   - Some links (Phoebe–Mars, Naiad–Colossus) worked by hailing, with no visible signal.
6. **On-screen times.** Naiad's 31 sail at **5 p.m.**, not 6. The Victory's "I rely on your keeping sight of the enemy" at 5 p.m. by Victory time (5.40 by the Euryalus).
7. **Caption.** Keep "a chain of frigates and ships of the line". Do not say the signal ran in minutes or "within two hours"; "about two and a half hours" or "by half-past nine" is safe.
