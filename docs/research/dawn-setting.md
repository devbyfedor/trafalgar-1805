# The setting at dawn, 21 October 1805, off Cape Trafalgar

Research notes for Scene 1 ("Dawn"). They cover positions, the sun, moon and sky, the weather, both fleets, and the details a knowledgeable viewer might check. Every fact gives its source and a confidence level. Conflicts are collected in section 7.

## Conventions used in these notes

- **Times.** A ship's log of 1805 records ship's time, which is local apparent (sundial) time, reset at each noon sight. All times below are local apparent time (LAT) unless marked otherwise. Off Cape Trafalgar on this date, LMT = LAT − 15 min and UT (GMT) = LAT + 9 min. Different ships' logs disagree by up to about an hour for the same event (section 4.3).
- **Directions.** Bearings, courses and wind directions in 1805 logs are compass (magnetic) readings. Magnetic variation off Cádiz in 1805 was about 20–21° W, so true = magnetic − 21°. "E by S" (magnetic) therefore means about E by N (true). Both values are given where it matters.
- **Distances.** A mile is a nautical mile (1,852 m), and a league is 3 nautical miles. James uses a cable of about 200 yards.
- **Confidence levels:**
  - **High:** several independent primary sources agree, or a computation agrees with USNO.
  - **Medium:** one good source, or a consistent inference.
  - **Low:** an estimate, or the sources conflict.

## 0. What matters for the scene (summary)

1. **Geometry at first light.**
   - The British fleet was about 20–21 nm west of Cape Trafalgar by the dawn log estimates. The Cape bore "E by S about seven leagues". The logs' noon distances do not fit well with this (fact-check F-12), so the real distance may have been greater.
   - The Combined Fleet lay between them and the coast, 9–12 nm east of Victory. It was strung out north–south over about 5 nm and heading south.
   - The Spanish coast lay about 10–12 nm beyond the Combined Fleet by the British bearings and Villeneuve's "four leagues" to the Cape. Dumanoir put the land 6–8 leagues (18–24 nm) away (fact-check F-13).
   - Confidence: High for the layout (British to the west, the enemy between them and the coast). Medium for the distances. *(Downgraded from High by the fact-check.)*
2. **Sunrise.**
   - Times: the sun's upper limb appeared at 06:27 LAT (06:36 UT), and the whole disc was clear by about 06:30.
   - Twilight: civil dawn was at 06:01 and nautical dawn at 05:31. The logs' "daylight" and first sightings fall at 05:30–06:10, between the two (fact-check F-5).
   - Direction: the sunrise azimuth was 102.5° true. That is E by S, 12.5° south of east, and not quite ESE (112.5°).
   - Confidence: High (computed; matches USNO and, in the fact-check, JPL Horizons DE441).
3. **The sun and the enemy line.** After correcting the log bearings for variation, the sun rose about 10–15° south of the southern end of the enemy line, which was their van at dawn. Place the disc at or just beyond the right-hand end of the enemy silhouettes, not in the middle of them. Confidence: Medium.
4. **Moon.**
   - It was a waning crescent, about 2 % lit, 31 h before new moon.
   - It rose about 05:19 LAT at azimuth 101° and stood about 12° high (azimuth 112°) at sunrise.
   - The night had been moonless and cloudy ("the absence of the moon").
   - No bright planet stood high in the morning sky. *Corrected:* Mercury (magnitude −1.1) was a morning object, about 11° west of the Sun. It rose about 05:47 UT and was about 5° up in the east at civil dawn, deep in the twilight glow where the logs place haze and cloud. Nobody records seeing it. Venus, Mars and Jupiter were evening objects (fact-check F-4).
   - Confidence: High.
5. **Wind.**
   - Strength: very light, from "light airs" to "light breeze" (Beaufort 1–2), with a few ships logging "moderate". It died towards calm by noon.
   - Direction: logged as NW, WNW, W and WSW, all magnetic. True direction was about WSW to WNW.
   - Confidence: High for "light westerly". The exact direction is uncertain.
6. **Swell.**
   - Logs: "a great swell from the westward", "a heavy swell from the westward", "a long heavy swell".
   - James: "the sea smooth with a great ground swell setting from the westward".
   - French logs: "la houle de l'Ouest très forte".
   - There were no whitecaps. *Fact-check (F-20): better "few or no whitecaps at dawn". Three logs' a.m. summaries say "moderate", which covers the night.*
   - Confidence: High for the swell. Medium–High for the whitecaps.
7. **Haze is documented, fog is not.**
   - British logs:
     - Euryalus: "Light breezes and hazy" at 04:00, and "Light winds and hazy with a great swell from the westward" at 08:00.
     - Britannia: "At 8, light airs and hazy".
     - Pickle: "At 4, light airs and hazy".
     - Defiance: "hazy weather".
   - Spanish log (Montañés): "Amaneció con celajería, horizontes calimosos" (day broke with broken cloud and hazy horizons).
   - Limit: the enemy was seen and counted at 9–12 nm, so visibility stayed at least 10–12 nm. A light haze that lifts is supported by the sources. A fog bank is not.
   - Confidence: High.
8. **Sky.**
   - The night had squalls, rain and lightning, which had passed by daybreak.
   - Dawn had broken cloud. Gravina's staff log: "Amaneció el cielo claro y aturbonado por el 1° y 2° cuadrante" (clear sky, squall clouds in the north-east and south-east quadrants). That puts cloud banks on the eastern horizon, where the sun rose.
   - Several British logs say "cloudy". By noon the sun was "shining in a beautiful manner".
   - Confidence: High.
9. **How the enemy looked from the British fleet.**
   - From an eye height of 5–10 m at 10–12 nm, the lowest 3–12 m of each hull is below the horizon. Sails and upper works show, but waterlines do not ("hull partly down").
   - Each rig subtends 8.5–10 arc-minutes, about 0.3 of the sun's diameter.
   - At dawn they carried easy sail (three topsails, mizzen or spanker, a headsail), close-hauled on the starboard tack and heading south.
   - Confidence: High for the computation, Medium for the sail plan.
10. **The British fleet at dawn.**
    - It was not yet in two tidy columns. It was in loose cruising order, scattered over several miles. The French described it as "sans ordre" (Dumanoir; Scipion). The Algésiras and the Montañés saw lines instead (fact-check F-25).
    - The columns formed over about two hours after the bear-up.
    - Once the weather column formed, the first two ships astern of Victory were the three-deckers Téméraire (98) and Neptune (98).
    - Confidence: High.
11. **Victory's sail.**
    - Until about 06:40–06:50 (James's and Nicolas's times), she steered N by E under reefed topsails and foresail. Her master's log is consistent with this: the course column has N by E at 05:00 and 06:00 and ENE at 07:00 (NRS II 183).
    - On bearing up she "shook the reefs out of her topsails, set steering-sails, and royals" (her log, as given by Nicolas). Later accounts have her studding sails set "on both sides".
    - The brief's rig, which has no studding sails, under-dresses her for the morning approach. This is a decision for the owner (section 7, item 9).
    - Confidence: High.
12. **Course and speed.**
    - The fleet bore up first ENE, then E (both magnetic; true about NE, then about ENE). Victory's own master's log has ENE at 07:00 and E by N from 08:00, which is about 047° and then about 058° true (fact-check F-27).
    - Speed was about 2–3 kn. Victory's master's log gives 3 kn every hour from 05:00 to 11:00 and 2.5 kn at noon (NRS II 183–184). James has it falling to "scarcely a knot and a half" at about 12:20.
    - Confidence: High.
13. **Colours.** Ensigns, and by the Euryalus log and James also the admirals' flags, were hoisted at about 12:15, as the action began. A White Ensign at the gaff at dawn is a small licence. When the ensign is flown under sail, the peak of the spanker gaff is the correct place (Perrin 1922). Whether the vice-admiral's flag was already flying at dawn is disputed (fact-check F-36). Confidence: Medium.

### 1.2 Reconstructed positions (true bearings, variation 21° W)

- **Cape Trafalgar:** 36°10.3′N 6°01.5′W (Purdy, *Sailing Directory*, 1840). The modern position is about 36°10.9′N 6°02.1′W.
- **British fleet at first light:** the Cape bore E by S (magnetic), about 080° true, 21 nm off. That puts the fleet about **36°08′N 6°28′W (±6′)**, roughly 20–21 nm west, slightly south of west, of the Cape (true). Prince observed latitude 36°05′N at noon in the rear of the lee column, which is consistent. Confidence: Medium (the method is sound; the logs disagree by a point or two).
  - *Fact-check (F-12).* Recomputing from the 1840 Cape position gives 36°06.6′N 6°27.1′W, and from the modern position 36°07.3′N 6°27.7′W. Both fall inside the ±6′.
  - The noon log entries do not fit a 21 nm dawn distance. The same Prince entry gives "Cape Trafalgar East 9 leagues" (NRS II 228). Other noon figures:
    - Britannia: "SE by E 7 leagues".
    - Euryalus: "SE by E, about 5 leagues" (NRS II 150).
    - Victory: "E. b. S. eighteen or twenty miles" (Nicolas VII 151).
  - Yet Victory logged 3 kn for every hour from 06:00 to 11:00. Nicolas warns: "Scarcely any two Ships' Logs agree in the Bearings and Distance at Noon on the 21st, and no Observation was taken" (VII 151n).
  - Treat the absolute positions as uncertain by several miles. The dawn distance to the Cape may have been more than 21 nm.
- **Combined Fleet centre:** 9–12 nm at about 080° true from Victory, about **36°09′N 6°14′W**. That is roughly 10–12 nm west of the Cape, and 8–10 nm off the coast between Conil and the Cape.
  - This agrees with Villeneuve's "Cape Trafalgar ESE about four leagues", since ESE magnetic is about E true.
  - Confidence: Medium.
  - *Fact-check (F-13): French sources put the coast further off.*
    - Dumanoir: "la terre à l'E.-S.-E., distante de 6 à 8 lieues" (18–24 nm) on the morning of the 21st (Desbrière, Rapports particuliers p. 149).
    - Scipion at noon: "la terre à l'E.-S.-E., distance de 8 à 9 lieues" (p. 155).
    - The Combined Fleet's distance from the coast therefore ranges from about 10 to 24 nm in the sources. Confidence for that distance: Low.
- **Correction to Wikipedia.** Wikipedia's *Battle of Trafalgar* places the British about 21 miles north-west of Cape Trafalgar at 5:40. The primary bearings put them about west of the Cape (W by S true; W by N magnetic), not north-west.
- **Battle site.** Wikipedia gives 36°15′N 6°12′W, about 8–9 nm west-north-west of the Cape. That is consistent with an approach of about 12 nm on an east-north-east true course from the dawn position. *Fact-check (F-14): the coordinate is confirmed as Wikipedia's, sourced to threedecks. The logs' noon distances (F-12) would put the action further from the Cape, so treat it as approximate.*

### 1.3 Was the Spanish coast visible?

- **From the Combined Fleet:**
  - Achille (French), report of Ensign Quiot: the fleet was "dans la matinée à vue de terre" (in sight of land during the morning). Source: Desbrière, *Trafalgar* (1907), Rapports particuliers p. 266.
  - Villeneuve: "Cape Trafalgar was made at the same time" as the British were sighted at daylight. Source: Fraser p. 110.
- **From the British:**
  - Euryalus, 11:40: "Saw the land bearing E by N, 5 or 6 leagues."
  - Neptune, noon: "Observed the land about Trafalgar E by S ½ S 5 or 6 leagues."
  - Defiance, p.m.: "Land east 5 or 6 leagues."
  - Leviathan, about noon: "Land east about 10 leagues". At 30 nm this must have been high land inland.
  - Source: NRS II. Confidence: High that the land was visible by mid to late morning.
- **At first light from the British deck it is uncertain.** The daylight bearings of the Cape could be sightings or dead reckoning, and the horizon was hazy.
  - What the geometry allows: Cape Trafalgar itself is low, "It is not high, but may be known by its remarkable figure, being flat ... A round tower stands on the eastern corner" (Purdy 1840). But the Altos (Heights) de Meca just north-east of it reach about 170 m (IGN geodetic vertex "Meca", about 169 m; not fully verified). *Fact-check (F-41): confirmed. The IGN vertex is 169.2 m, and the Junta de Andalucía gives 174 m for the Torre de Meca site.*
  - From a 7 m eye at 21 nm, about 55 m is hidden below the horizon (section 1.5; the original said "1.6", a typo). About 115 m of the Meca heights would therefore show, subtending about 10′. That is about the same angular height as the enemy rigs.
  - So in clear air the coast would read as a low, flat, dark band on the horizon behind and beyond the enemy, about as tall as their masts. Haze could easily hide it.
  - It is optional for the scene. A very faint low land line at the far right is defensible. Confidence: Medium.

### 1.4 The Combined Fleet's heading and formation at daybreak, and the wear

**Heading at daybreak:**
- "The Enemy with their heads to the Southward" (Nelson's private diary, Nicolas VII 139).
- "Formed in a close line of battle ahead on the starboard tack, standing to the south" (Beatty, *Authentic Narrative*, 1807).
- "The Enemy's Line extending about NNE to SSW" (Victory's log). With variation applied, the line ran nearly north–south true.
- Corbett: "with Gravina's squadron to windward of the van they appeared to stretch north-north-east and south-south-west, with their heads to the southward as though making for the Straits" (*Campaign of Trafalgar*, 1910, p. 359).

**Formation at daybreak:**
- The line was badly formed. Villeneuve was trying to change from the night's "order of battle as convenient" to the ordre naturel (Corbett p. 359).
- Algésiras, as transcribed in Desbrière: "à 7 heures, l'amiral Villeneuve fit signal à l'armée de former la ligne de bataille tribord amures, ordre naturel" (Desbrière p. 233).
- The Montañés: "la escuadra mal formada en línea" (Desbrière p. 375).
- Spartiate: "lying to on the starboard tack and forming their line of battle" (NRS II).
- Lucas of Redoutable: "spread out from south-east to north-west; the ships being much scattered, and not forming any apparent order" (Fraser p. 112). This orientation conflicts with the British "NNE to SSW".

**Order to wear:**
- Signal times:
  - 08:00: Fraser p. 99, and the log of Gravina's squadron ("a las 8, la señal de virar en redondo, a un tiempo"), which says it was carried out at 08:15 (Desbrière p. 379).
  - 08:30: James III 390, and Algésiras ("A 8 h ½ ... virer lof pour lof tous à la fois", Desbrière p. 233).
  - *Added in fact-check (F-18):*
    - 08:15: Scipion's journal, "aussitôt" after the British bore down at 8 h 15 (Desbrière, Rapports particuliers p. 155).
    - 09:30: Captain Le Tourneur of the Algésiras, in his own report (p. 227).
- British observations of the turn:
  - 07:00, Nelson's diary: "at seven the Enemy wearing in succession". Corbett notes they in fact wore together, not in succession.
  - About 07:20, Beatty: they "wore in succession about twenty minutes past seven o'clock" and stood on the larboard tack, heads toward Cádiz, under a good deal of sail, about two points off the wind with topsails shivering (added in fact-check).
  - 08:55, Spartiate.
  - 10:00, Euryalus and Collingwood's journal.
- Purpose: to form the line on the larboard tack heading north, in reversed order, with Cádiz under the lee (James).

**The resulting line and crescent:**
- James: the manoeuvre was not complete until "near 10 a.m."; with the light wind, flaws off the land, the heavy ground swell and some inexperienced captains, the line formed very irregularly, "curved or crescent-like", with the ships two and in places three deep (III 390–391).
- "It formed a Crescent convexing to Leeward" (Collingwood's dispatch). "Every alternate ship was about a cable's length to windward of her second ahead and astern, forming a kind of double line" (same dispatch).
- Fraser: clumps of ships crowded two or three deep in places, wide gaps with one or two stragglers in others, and the whole line sagging to leeward in the centre in a deep crescent (p. 99).
- Corbett attributes the sag to a shift of wind during re-forming (pp. 376–377).
- **The crescent belongs to about 10:00–12:00, not to dawn.** Confidence: High.

### 1.5 How the enemy would look from the British fleet

Formula: the distance to the sea horizon with standard refraction is d (nm) ≈ 2.08 √h, with h in metres. That is 3.86 √h km (Wikipedia, *Horizon*). Geometric, without refraction, it is 1.93 √h. Refraction at dawn over the sea can vary by several per cent.

**What this means:**
- **From a low eye of 5–10 m at 10–12 nm**, 3–12 m of each enemy hull is cut off. Courses, topsails, topgallants and the upper works show; the waterline and lower-deck ports do not. The picture is "hull partly down", not "topsails only". That would need about 14 nm or more.
- **At Collingwood's "6–7 miles"** the hulls would be fully up.
- **From Victory's masthead** they were fully hull-up.
- Beatty says they were "distinctly seen from the Victory's deck".
- Height estimate: the top of a 74's upper works is roughly 6–9 m above the waterline (my estimate, not sourced).
- Confidence: High for the geometry.

**Angular sizes** (the sun's diameter on 21 October was 32.2′):

| Item | 10 nm | 12 nm |
|---|---|---|
| 55 m rig | 10.2′ (about 0.32 sun diameters) | 8.5′ (about 0.26) |
| 60 m hull length | 11.1′ | 9.3′ |
| A 5 nm line seen broadside, at 11 nm | about 26° of horizon (about 48 sun diameters) | |

At true scale, each enemy ship is only about a third of the sun's diameter tall. This matters if the sun disc is drawn at a realistic size.

**Composition check (derived from the brief's own percentages).** The brief puts the horizon at 60–62 % of the viewport height, Victory's waterline at 68–72 % and her main truck at 10–12 %, with the truck 62 m above the waterline.
- These numbers imply a viewer about **7–12 m above the sea**. With the horizon at 61 %, waterline at 70 % and truck at 11 %, h ≈ 62 / (1 + 50/9) ≈ 9.5 m.
- They also imply Victory is about 200 m away for a 30° vertical field of view.
- That is a plausible viewpoint, such as another ship's quarterdeck.
- At that field of view, a physically scaled enemy ship at 11 nm would be about 4 px tall at 720 px viewport height. The silhouettes will have to be enlarged; keep them in proportion to the sun disc.

## 2. Sun, sky and moon

### 2.1 Method

- **Computation:** Meeus, *Astronomical Algorithms* (low-precision Sun, ch. 25; sidereal time and horizontal coordinates). Moon from the Astronomical Almanac low-precision formulae plus parallax.
  - Rise and set criterion: the upper limb on the apparent horizon. That is h0 = −0.833° for the Sun (34′ refraction plus 16′ semi-diameter) and h0 = 0.7275π − 0.567° for the Moon.
  - Date: 21 October 1805 Gregorian (Britain and Spain both used the Gregorian calendar). It was a Monday (computed; Nelson's diary agrees).
  - ΔT (about 13 s) is ignored.
  - Scripts: a small Python implementation of the above, with planets from JPL approximate Keplerian elements (Standish). The scripts were working files and are not kept in the repository.
- **Cross-check:** the US Naval Observatory Astronomical Applications API, which is valid for 1700–2100.
  - Rise, set and twilight: `https://aa.usno.navy.mil/api/rstt/oneday?date=1805-10-21&coords=36.2,-6.1&tz=0`
  - Moon phases: `https://aa.usno.navy.mil/api/moon/phases/date?date=1805-10-01&nump=6`
  - Sun altitude and azimuth: `https://aa.usno.navy.mil/calculated/altaz?body=10&date=1805-10-21&intv_mag=2&lat=36.2&lon=-6.1&tz=0&tz_sign=-1`
  - Moon altitude and azimuth: the same with `body=11`.
  - Celestial navigation (all bodies above the horizon): `https://aa.usno.navy.mil/api/celnav?date=1805-10-21&time=06:10&coords=36.2,-6.1`
- **Result:** the computation and USNO agree to within a minute for every value. Varying the position over 36.0–36.3° N and 6.0–6.3° W changes the LAT times by less than a minute and the azimuths by less than 0.1°.

- **The sun's path after rising:** it climbs at about 53° to the horizon, rising up and to the right (southward) at about 0.2° of altitude per minute. The disc takes about 3 min from first gleam to clear. This comes from the computed table and is consistent with USNO. It matters for animation: move the sun diagonally up and to the right, not straight up.
- **Flattening near the horizon:** refraction compresses the disc vertically. The ratio of vertical to horizontal diameter is about 0.8 with the lower limb on the horizon, about 0.87 at 1° and about 0.92 at 2°, and it is round within about 3 % by 5°. This uses Bennett's refraction formula. The detail is optional.

### 2.3 Ship's time and log dating

- **Ship's time was local apparent time.** Logs were kept in ship's time, the local apparent time, corrected now and then for the change of longitude; by old practice the correction was made at noon, when the officer taking the latitude sight reported twelve o'clock and the captain ordered it so.
  - Hinks concludes that, in the days of sail, the recorded hour can be read as apparent time at the ship's meridian without serious error.
  - Source: A. R. Hinks, editor of *The Geographical Journal*, 1935, reproduced by the Naval Marine Archive. Confidence: High.
- **What the timings show:**
  - The logs' "daylight" sightings fall at 05:30–05:45, which matches nautical dawn (05:31 LAT). *Fact-check (F-5): other entries are later.*
    - Victory's master's log and the log printed by Nicolas: "At 6".
    - Spartiate: "daylight (6.10)" (Nicolas VII 167n).
    - James: "At 6 a.m." (III 390).
    - So "daylight" in the logs spans about 05:30–06:10, from nautical to just after civil dawn. It is not a precise twilight stage.
  - The logged times of Nelson's first signals spread from 05:40 (Belleisle) and 06:00–06:15 (Mars, Ajax, Defiance) to 06:40–06:50 (Victory, as given by James and Nicolas).
  - Victory's own clock may therefore have run about 30 min behind most of the fleet, or the others logged when they answered. Sunrise fell at about 06:27 by apparent time, around the time of the signals.
  - Confidence: Medium. *Fact-check (F-44): the lag is consistent with the evidence but not proven. Downgraded to Low–Medium.*
    - Victory's master's log records the first sighting "At 6", later than most ships.
    - Its course column changes from N by E to ENE between the 06:00 and 07:00 entries, which fits James's 06:50 (NRS II 183).
    - No independent clock comparison exists.
- **Log dating (relevant if anyone quotes logs).** In 1805 the nautical day ran from noon to noon, 12 hours ahead of the civil day. The morning of civil 21 October therefore appears under "21st, a.m." and the battle under "22nd October p.m." in many logs.
  - An Admiralty circular of 11 October 1805 changed logs to the civil day, but it reached the fleet too late for Trafalgar. Nelson's private diary used civil time.
  - Sources: Hinks 1935, quoting the 1913 Admiralty Trafalgar Committee; A. Bond (Pen & Sword blog). Bond also notes a dating error in Victory's log.
  - Confidence: High.

### 2.5 Planets and stars (optional detail)

**Planets:** none was bright in the dawn sky. Venus, Jupiter and Mars were evening objects, 30–42° east of the sun (Venus and Jupiter were close together in the evening sky). Mercury (8° from the sun) and Saturn (5°) were lost in the glare. The USNO celestial-navigation output for 06:10 UT lists only Saturn, 1.5° up in twilight, and no other planet. Confidence: High.

*Corrected in fact-check (F-4), using JPL Horizons DE441 for 36.2° N 6.1° W:*
- **Evening planets (confirmed).**
  - Venus: magnitude −4.0, 37.6° east of the Sun.
  - Jupiter: −1.8, 39.2° east.
  - Mars: +1.5, 27.6° east.
  - All three were 33–49° below the horizon at dawn.
- **Mercury was a morning object, not "lost in the glare".**
  - It was 10.9° west of the Sun, at magnitude −1.1.
  - It rose about 05:47 UT. It was 4.8° up at azimuth 100° at civil dawn (06:10 UT) and about 10° up at sunrise.
- **Saturn** was 7.6° west of the Sun, at magnitude +0.8, 1.5° up at 06:10 UT.
- **Why USNO omits Mercury.** The USNO celestial-navigation service covers only the four navigational planets (Venus, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn). Mercury's absence from its output is therefore no evidence.
- **Practical verdict.** In a perfectly clear sky Mercury would be a faint point low in the east during civil twilight, just left of the sunrise point. The logs put haze and squall cloud on the eastern horizon, and nobody records it. "No morning star" remains a safe choice, but "no planet was above the horizon" would be wrong.
- Confidence: High.

**Stars at about 05:40 LAT (nautical twilight), from the USNO data:**
- **North:** Polaris, 36° up, due north.
- **North-north-east to north-east:** the Plough (Big Dipper), 25–49° up, bowl high and handle hanging down towards the north-east horizon. Dubhe is at 49°, azimuth 33°; Alkaid at 25°, azimuth 45°.
- **North-west:** Capella, high at 62°.
- **East-north-east:** Arcturus, just rising (3° up, azimuth 67°).
- **East-south-east:** Regulus, high (48°).
- Stars near the eastern horizon vanish first. With the brief's framing (horizon at about 61 %) little sky above about 18° is in frame, so keep any stars sparse and faint.
- Confidence: High.

### 2.6 Twilight colours and light (atmospheric optics, general)

- **The western sky.** *Corrected in fact-check (F-6).*
  - During civil twilight (06:01–06:27 LAT; 05:31–06:01 is nautical twilight), the Earth's shadow is a blue-grey band on the western horizon, opposite the sun, with the pinkish "Belt of Venus" above it.
  - At dawn the band **sinks** towards the horizon and fades as the sun rises. It does not rise. Wikipedia, *Earth's shadow*: before sunrise the shadow recedes as the sun comes up; after sunset it rises as the sun goes down.
  - This is the left-hand side of the scene. Animate it downwards as p increases. It is an accurate optional touch.
  - Confidence: High (general physics; Wikipedia, *Belt of Venus* and *Earth's shadow*).
- **Sun glitter.** With light airs the sea is smooth, apart from the long swell and small ripples. The glitter path under a low sun is therefore narrow and broken into long bright bands on swell faces, not a broad sparkle field. This follows Cox and Munk (1954): mean-square surface slope is about 0.003 + 0.005 × wind in m/s, so it is small at 1–3 m/s. Confidence: Medium.

### 3.2 Wind strength

- **Log terms at dawn:**
  - "Light airs": Victory, Britannia (master), Leviathan, Achille, Naiad, Phoebe, Pickle, Polyphemus.
  - "Light breezes": Euryalus, Conqueror, Swiftsure.
  - *Corrected in fact-check (F-20): Victory's morning entry is "At 8, light breezes and cloudy".* This is the wording of both her master's log (NRS II 183) and the log printed by Nicolas (VII 153n). Her "Light airs and cloudy. Standing towards the enemy's van with all sail set" is the p.m. entry, from noon onwards (NRS II 184; the same wording is in Bond's transcription, under the nautical date).
  - "Inclinable to calm": Defence, Sirius, Bellerophon (all at 04:00).
  - "Moderate": Royal Sovereign ("Moderate breezes and fine weather"), Mars, Agamemnon.
  - Spanish logs: "bonancible", which is moderate in later Spanish usage. Gravina's squadron was further south and west.
- **Later in the morning** the wind fell: "inclinable to calm" (Revenge at 09:00; Defiance), and "the wind had gradually died away to a mere breath" (James III 397, at about 12:20).
- **Beaufort equivalents** (the scale was devised by Beaufort in 1805–06; the terms predate it):
  - "Light airs" is force 1 (1–3 kn, "scaly ripples, no foam crests").
  - "Light breeze" is force 2 (4–6 kn, "small wavelets, crests glassy, no breaking").
  - Whitecaps begin only at force 3 (7–10 kn, "scattered whitecaps").
  - Wheeler (1995) maps "fresh" and "strong breezes" to Beaufort 5 and 6.
  - Sources: NOAA SPC Beaufort table; Met Office.
- **Verdict:** force 1–2, locally 3, at dawn, falling to 0–1 by noon. **No whitecaps.** Confidence: High.
  - *Fact-check (F-20): wording and confidence adjusted.*
  - In modern terms a "moderate breeze" is force 4, which NOAA describes as "numerous whitecaps".
  - Three logs (Royal Sovereign, Mars, Agamemnon) give "moderate" in their a.m. summaries. Those summaries cover the whole watch from midnight, including the squally night.
  - By 08:00 nearly every log says light airs or light breezes.
  - Safer wording: "few or no whitecaps at dawn, none by mid-morning". This matches the brief's "little whitecapping". Confidence: Medium–High.

### 3.3 Swell

- **British logs:**
  - Euryalus at 08:00: "Light winds and hazy with a great swell from the westward". At noon: "light winds and a great swell from the westward".
  - Bellerophon at 04:00: "Light airs, inclinable to calm, with a heavy swell from the westward".
  - Swiftsure at 04:00: "light breezes and a heavy swell from the westward".
  - Prince: "Light winds and a swell"; p.m. "westerly swell".
  - Spartiate, p.m.: "Light winds from the westward, with a swell from that quarter".
  - Source: NRS II. Confidence: High.
- **Accounts:**
  - Beatty: "a long heavy swell running".
  - James (quoted by Nicolas VII 151): "the wind very light, the sea smooth with a great ground swell setting from the westward".
  - Desbrière, summarising the French logs: "Suivant tous les journaux, le vent était très faible et la houle de l'Ouest très forte" (p. 296).
  - Magendie (captain of the Bucentaure): the ships "n'avaient point assez d'air pour bien gouverner, la lame les jetait sous le vent" (they had too little wind to steer and the swell threw them to leeward).
  - *Added in fact-check (F-21):*
    - Lucas (Redoutable) at daybreak: "The wind was very light and there was still a heavy sea running" (Fraser p. 112).
    - Montañés at midnight 20–21 October: she opened her lower-deck starboard ports but had to shut them again "por introducirse golpes de mar por las puertas y llenarse de agua la batería" (because seas came in through the ports and flooded the deck) (Desbrière p. 375). This describes the night sea, before the wind fell.
  - Confidence: High.
- **When it set in and what followed:**
  - Captain Lucas of Redoutable noted the threatening westerly swell on the night of the 20th. Most British logs note it a little later (Wheeler 1995, p. 179).
  - It was "the sure forerunner of a gale" (NRS editor, II 142). Nelson's order to prepare to anchor was about the coming gale and the lee shore.
  - Confidence: High.
- **Swell parameters** (derived; not recorded in 1805):
  - A pre-storm swell from a distant low is typically 12–16 s.
  - In deep water: 12 s gives L ≈ 225 m and c ≈ 18.7 m/s (36 kn); 14 s gives L ≈ 306 m and 21.8 m/s (42 kn); 16 s gives L ≈ 400 m and 25 m/s (49 kn). Formulas: L = 1.56 T², c = 1.56 T.
  - That is 4–7 times Victory's 57 m gun-deck length, so she would ride it with slow heave and gentle pitch.
  - Running before it at 2.5 kn, the crests overtake her from astern. The encounter period is about 13–17 s. The stern lifts first, then the bow.
  - In the 50–90 m soundings of the battle area (Euryalus "Sounded in 50 fathoms" at 14:36), a 12–16 s swell begins to feel the bottom, hence "ground swell".
  - Wave height is not recorded. "Great" or "heavy" suggests a few metres; this is an estimate.
  - Confidence: Medium for physics, Low for the exact numbers.
- **Geometry in the side view:**
  - A swell from the west has crests running north–south. A camera looking north looks along the crests.
  - On screen the swell appears as humps moving left to right in every depth band. Their screen wavelength and speed decrease with distance (parallax).
  - They are not horizontal ridges rolling towards the viewer. The brief's wave strips that translate left to right, slower and finer with distance, are consistent with this.
  - Confidence: High.

- What is not documented is fog. No log of the 21st says "fog" or "thick".
- The enemy's 33 ships were counted at 9–12 nm at daylight, and the land was seen at 15–30 nm by late morning. Visibility therefore stayed at least 10–12 nm.
- Conclusion: keep it a thin haze that lifts and never hides the enemy line once it is revealed.

- Confidence: High.

### 3.5 The night before

- **British movements:**
  - On the evening of the 20th "the horizon cleared, and the wind shifted to west-north-west" (James III 387).
  - The fleet stood to the south-west "under their topsails and foresails" overnight (Nicolas VII 136). It wore at 04:00 and steered N by E under easy sail.
- **Weather:** squalls with rain and lightning, then "more moderate" by about 04:30 (Britannia, Minotaur, Bellerophon, Thunderer, Belleisle).
- **Lights:**
  - The enemy showed many lights, "like a well lit up street" six miles long (Fraser p. 95).
  - The British watching ships "concealed every light" (Defence officer, Fraser p. 95).
  - Frigates and look-out ships burnt blue lights and fired rockets and guns to report the enemy's movements. Logs: Mars "At 2, saw a blue light NE. At 3.20, saw another ENE"; Leviathan "Several guns, blue lights and rockets"; Colossus "4. The enemy's lights in sight to the eastward".
- **Suggestion:** a few faint lights on the eastern horizon at p = 0 would be accurate foreshadowing.

### 3.6 What followed: the gale

- Collingwood's dispatch: a gale blew from the day after the action; on the morning of the 22nd a strong southerly wind with squalls, and on the 23rd "the Gale increased, and the Sea ran so high".
- Wheeler (1995): a "cut-off low". The storm arrived most likely about midday on the 22nd and lasted to the 29th, with winds mainly between W and SSW.
- On the 21st itself the weather was calm, as it had been through three weeks of anticyclonic blockade.
- Confidence: High.
- *Fact-check (F-24): too simple for 19–20 October.*
  - The "calm for three weeks" is Wheeler's phrase (1995, p. 179).
  - Nelson's diary for the 20th: "At noon fresh gales and heavy rain" (Nicolas VII 137).
  - Euryalus on the 20th: "strong breezes and thick weather with rain" (NRS II 148).
  - The night of 20–21 October had squalls with lightning (section 3.5).
  - Wheeler also writes that battle was not joined "hasta las tres de la tarde", which is wrong: the action began about 12:00–12:20. Use Wheeler for the synoptic picture, not for narrative details.

## 4. The British fleet at daybreak

### 4.1 Composition

- 27 ships of the line, 4 frigates (Euryalus, Naiad, Phoebe, Sirius), the schooner Pickle and the cutter Entreprenante. Conqueror's log: "fleet in company, consisting of 27 sail of the line, 4 frigates, 1 schooner, and 1 cutter".
- Seven were three-deckers: Victory 104, Royal Sovereign 100, Britannia 100, Téméraire 98, Neptune 98, Dreadnought 98, Prince 98. The French Algésiras counted "27 vaisseaux dont sept à trois ponts".
- Africa (64) had parted company in the night and came in from the north (Collingwood's journal, NRS II 202).
- Sources: NRS II; Desbrière p. 232; Wikipedia, *Battle of Trafalgar order of battle*. Confidence: High.

### 4.2 Formation before the signals

- **Overnight and at dawn:** "under easy sail", in cruising order and dispersed.
- **At 04:00 the ships were miles apart:**
  - Mars: "Victory WSW, 3 or 4 miles distant".
  - Ajax: "Commander-in-Chief NE 3 miles".
  - Minotaur: "Victory N by E".
  - Spartiate: "Admiral W by S".
  - Slow ships "take their stations as most convenient": Prince, Britannia, Dreadnought (James; Prince's log).
- Corbett: Nelson "found himself with his fleet in disorder and a section of it absent" (p. 359).
- **French view:**
  - Argonaute: the British "running before the wind towards us, in line abreast by groups, the ships at great distances from each other and several much nearer to us, in short without order" (Desbrière p. 249).
  - Lucas: "The enemy also were not in any order" (Fraser p. 112).
  - Conflicting French view, Algésiras: "rangée en ligne de bataille et courait au plus près bâbord" (in line of battle, close-hauled on the port tack).
  - *Added in fact-check (F-25):*
    - The "sans ordre" wording is Dumanoir's: "elle était sans ordre, se dirigeant du Nord au Sud" (Desbrière, Rapports particuliers p. 149).
    - Scipion: "Elle était sans ordre, formée sur deux pelotons qui s'étendaient du N. au S." (p. 155).
    - Conflicting Spanish view, Montañés: from first light the British fleet "estaba formada en dos líneas con todo aparejo, siguiendo la vuelta del E." (formed in two lines under all sail, heading east) (Desbrière p. 375). That description fits the scene from about 07:00, not first light.
- **How the columns formed** (Corbett pp. 362–363, from the signal book): signal 76 was "a signal not intended for a formed fleet", so each ship bore up in turn and made for her flagship's wake. The enemy first saw "line-abreast ... or groups", then two "pelotons", and "finally in about two hours ... two columns with the heavy ships leading".
- Confidence: High.

### 4.3 Signals, about 06:00–07:00

James III 390 and Nicolas VII 137, using Victory's times: at 6.40 Victory signalled No. 72 (form the order of sailing in two columns) and No. 13 (prepare for battle), and at 6.50 No. 76 (bear up). She then bore up to the eastward, "shook the reefs out of her topsails, set steering-sails, and royals", and cleared for quarters.

### 4.5 The lee (southern) column

- Collingwood led in the Royal Sovereign (100), with Belleisle, Mars, Tonnant, Bellerophon, Colossus, Achille, then Revenge and Polyphemus, Swiftsure, Dreadnought, Defiance, Thunderer, Defence and Prince. Order after Achille varies between sources.
- **Position:** it lay to leeward, south of the weather column. At noon the Royal Sovereign bore "south-east, distant about two miles" from Victory (James, via Nicolas VII 151).
- In a north-facing view centred on Victory, the lee column would be on the viewer's side or behind the viewer, so leaving it out of frame is correct.
- Sources: Nicolas VII 151; Wikipedia. Confidence: High.

### 4.6 Course and speed

**Course:**
- Before 06:40, Victory steered N by E (magnetic; about N by W true) on the larboard tack. Beatty: "stood on the larboard tack with their heads to the northward, carrying their topsails and foresails".
- After bearing up: ENE (signal 76 compass ENE), soon E. Sources: Bellerophon at 06:42; Collingwood "to bear up east"; Victory log "Bore up to the Eastwd".
- Corbett gives a "mean course about east" (magnetic), with Orion's courses as an example: E by N at 07:00, E at 08:00, E by S at 09:00, ESE at noon.
- True course: about NE at first, then about ENE (069°).
- Confidence: High for magnetic, Medium for true.
- *Fact-check (F-27): Victory's own course column, master's log (NRS II 183).*
  - N by E at 05:00, unchanged at 06:00; ENE at 07:00; E by N from 08:00 to noon (all magnetic).
  - That is about 350°, then about 047°, then about **058° true**.
  - Nicolas's narrative has Nelson later steering "a trifle more to the northward" (VII 150).

**Speed:**
- Victory's log: "speed up to noon as two and a half knots" (NRS II 142). *Fact-check (F-27): that is the NRS editor's summary of the noon entry. The hourly log column (master's log, NRS II 183–184) reads, in knots:*
  - *01:00–02:00: 2.75 (2 knots 6 fathoms).*
  - *03:00: 3.*
  - *04:00: 2.5.*
  - *05:00–11:00: 3 each hour.*
  - *Noon: 2.5.*
  - *Evening: about 1.5.*
- James: "scarcely going, with studding-sails set, three knots an hour" after bearing up; at 12:20 "going scarcely a knot and a half".
- Nicolas: "not ... a greater rate than a mile and a half an hour" (approach).
- Wheeler: "poco más de dos nudos" (a little over two knots).
- Corbett: 2–3 kn, "The fastest ships were doing a little over three knots, the slower ones about two". Neptune did over 3 kn, falling to 1.4 after 09:00; Dreadnought 1 kn until 10:00; "Victory and Royal Sovereign apparently were doing 3 at the finish".
- **For the scene:** about 2–3 kn. A small bow wave is right, with no bone in her teeth.
- Confidence: High.

### 4.7 Victory's sail at dawn

- **Before bearing up:** reefed topsails and foresail. Beatty describes the fleet carrying "topsails and foresails". Other ships record reefing and striking topgallant yards in the night's squalls: Britannia, Belleisle, Thunderer.
- **On bearing up**, Victory "shook the reefs out of her topsails, set steering-sails [studding sails], and royals, and cleared for quarters" (Nicolas VII 137; Bond's transcription of her log: "Bore up to the Eastwd out all Reefs Top Sails set Steering Sails & Royals, cleared for Quarters").
- **During the approach:**
  - "Studding-sails set on both sides" at 12:20 (James III 397).
  - "All her studding-sails and their booms, on both sides were shot away" (Beatty).
  - About 09:40: "the lee or starboard lower studding-sail" (James III 391).
  - So she carried studding sails on both sides, including lower studding sails, and the starboard side was the lee side.
- **Other ships:**
  - Neptune: royals and topgallant studding sails at 06:30; lower and topmast studding sails at 07:00.
  - Téméraire: "lower topmast and topgallant studding sails set on the larboard side".
  - Algésiras at 10:30: the British "sous toutes voiles possibles, cacatois, bonnettes hautes et basses" (under all possible sail, royals, studding sails aloft and alow).
- Confidence: High.

## 5. The Combined Fleet

### 5.1 Composition

- 33 ships of the line, "of which Eighteen were French and Fifteen Spanish" (Collingwood's dispatch). With them were 5 French frigates (Cornélie, Hermione, Hortense, Rhin, Thémis) and 2 brigs (Argus, Furet). Euryalus logged "thirty-three sail of the line, five frigates and two brigs".
- **Big ships:** one four-decker, the Santísima Trinidad (136 guns), and three Spanish three-deckers: Santa Ana (112), Príncipe de Asturias (112) and Rayo (100).
- **The rest were two-deckers.** The French flagship Bucentaure and three other French ships were 80-gun two-deckers.
- Admirals: Villeneuve in Bucentaure; Gravina in Príncipe de Asturias; Álava in Santa Ana; Dumanoir in Formidable; Magon in Algésiras; Cisneros in Santísima Trinidad.
- Sources: Gazette; NRS II; Wikipedia. Confidence: High.

### 5.2 How strung out

- **Length:**
  - James: "extending over a space, admitting a cable's length, or 200 yards, to be betwixt each ship, of nearly five miles" (at daybreak, III 389).
  - Wikipedia: a line nearly 5 miles (8 km) long by 11:00.
  - Overnight their lights stretched about 6 miles (Fraser).
- **At dawn:**
  - "In two irregular divisions" (Lt Browne, Victory). Gravina's squadron was to windward of the van.
  - "Much scattered" (Lucas).
  - "Mal formada" (Montañés).
- **After wearing:**
  - Clumps two or three deep, with wide gaps. Fraser: "Between the 'Neptuno' ... and the 'Héros', there were nine ships crowded together; although five would ... have been quite sufficient".
  - The ships were "doubled on each other, so as to form two irregular lines, extending from about N.N.E. to S.S.W.: the Spaniards and French being mixed together" (Nicolas VII 150).
- **For the silhouettes:** irregular spacing, clumps of 2–3 overlapping hulls, a few gaps, and no national grouping.
- Confidence: High.

### 5.3 Sail and heading at dawn

- Heading south on the starboard tack, close-hauled or lying to. Seen from the west (the British side), their starboard sides faced the viewer. In a view facing east, their bows point right (south). After 08:00–10:00 they turned to head north, bows left.
- **Sail at dawn:**
  - The Montañés kept her night rig, "3 gavias, mesana y contrafoque" (three topsails, mizzen sail and fore-topmast staysail), "a imitación del general" (following the admiral) (Desbrière p. 375).
  - A British officer saw them "in line under easy sail" (Fraser p. 98).
- **Later in the morning:**
  - Montañés at about 11:00: "la escuadra no tenía más aparejo que las gavias" (the fleet carried nothing but topsails).
  - James: "under topsails and topgallant-sails, with the main topsail shivering, and lay a point, or rather more, off the wind" (III 391).
- **For the silhouettes:** courses furled or few, topsails set, yards braced sharp up (seen obliquely, so narrower than a broadside sail). Show no studding sails.
- Confidence: Medium to High.

### 5.4 Appearance (they will be backlit silhouettes)

- **Paint:**
  - Midshipman Badcock (Neptune): some enemy ships were painted like the British, with double yellow sides, some had a single broad red or yellow streak, some were all black, and the Santísima Trinidad had "four distinct lines of red, with a white ribbon between them" (Fraser p. 109). Confidence: High (eyewitness).
  - Mast hoops: "The hoops round the masts of the Enemy's Ships were, he knew, painted black; the whole of the lower masts of all his own Ships were painted yellow" (Nicolas VII 131, on Nelson's order to Belleisle and Polyphemus to paint their hoops). Confidence: High.
- **At 10–12 nm against the sunrise none of this is visible.**
  - Hulls and sails read as one muted silhouette tone.
  - Haze and distance lighten and cool the silhouettes (aerial perspective), so they should be mid-tone blue-grey or violet-grey, not black.
  - Sails seen from their windward side against the sun may show faintly lighter edges; at this range there is no detail.
  - Confidence: Medium (general optics).
- **Flags:**
  - James (via Nicolas VII 152): the Combined Fleet hoisted its ensigns at about the time the firing began, and every Spanish ship hung "a large wooden cross" from the end of the spanker boom.
  - Conflicting: Britannia's Lt Barclay at 08:00: "Eastern horizon beautifully adorned with French and Spanish ensigns".
  - *Added in fact-check (F-33): Scipion's journal supports James.* At "midi 15" the rear was ordered to haul to the wind; "10 minutes après, le feu commença ...; en même temps, l'armée a arboré le pavillon de poupe" (at the same moment the fleet hoisted its stern ensigns) (Desbrière, Rapports particuliers p. 155). The weight of evidence is ensigns hoisted at about 12:15–12:25. Barclay's quote is confirmed as printed (NRS II 213) but stands alone.
  - French ships also carried a stern escutcheon in blue, white and red (Fraser p. 109).
  - At silhouette scale, show no flags or only specks.

## 6. Other things a knowledgeable viewer might check

1. **Sun against the enemy line.** As corrected for variation in section 1.2, the enemy spanned about 066–095° true from the British, with its centre at about 080°. The sun rose at 102.5°, about 10–15° beyond the southern end. "Sun behind the enemy" holds for the glow. For the disc, put it at or just past the right-hand (southern) end of the line. If someone read the log bearings as true, the sun would sit behind the centre of the line, and that reading is wrong. Confidence: Medium.
2. **Side-view convention.** In a true north-facing view, east, the enemy line and the sun at 102.5° would all lie at or beyond the right edge of the frame. They are ahead of Victory, not abeam. Showing them on the right-hand horizon is the standard marine-painting convention, close to a panoramic mapping. The convention is fine, but it should be stated in decisions.md as a deliberate convention.
3. **Light on Victory.** The low sun was ahead of her on the starboard bow: 102.5° true against a course of about 070° true, or 090° in the picture's convention. On the side facing the viewer, the light rakes from the right (forward). It picks out port lids and wales, and shadows fall aft (to the left).
   - *Fact-check (F-43): number corrected, conclusion unchanged.*
   - Victory's master's log gives E by N (magnetic) from 08:00, about 058° true. The sun at 102.5° was therefore about 45° on her starboard bow.
   - At the moment of sunrise (06:27 LAT) she was probably still on N by E (about 350° true) before bearing up. The sun was then just abaft her starboard beam.
   - Either way the starboard side, the one the viewer sees, is the sunlit side.
   - **Sail faces** (derived for the ship researcher; Medium confidence). With the wind on the port quarter, square yards are braced slightly, with the lee (starboard) yardarms a little aft of square. From the starboard side the viewer therefore sees the forward faces of the sails, filled and bellied towards the bow. Those faces are the ones the sun lights.
   - **Headsails and spanker:** headsails are partly blanketed when running. The spanker would be swung out to starboard or brailed up; check with the ship research.
4. **Studding sails** (sections 4.7 and 7). Studding sails on both sides from about 07:00 are well documented. Omitting them is a simplification an expert may notice.
5. **Colours.**
   - Euryalus log: "At 12.16, the English Admirals hoisted their respective flags and ... the British fleet, the British ensign (white)."
   - James (via Nicolas VII 151): "Immediately the three British Admirals hoisted their respective flags, and the ships of both divisions of the Fleet, the white or St.-George's ensign". Union Jacks were also ordered at the main topmast stay and the fore topgallant stay.
     - *Corrected in fact-check (F-34): Nelson's own memorandum of 10 October 1805 orders only one jack.* "When in presence of an Enemy, all the Ships under my command are to bear white Colours, and a Union Jack is to be suspended from the fore top-gallant stay" (Nicolas VII 104; quoted by Perrin 1922).
     - The second jack, at the main topmast stay, comes from James: each ship "carried, or was ordered to carry, a union-jack at her maintopmast-stay, and another at her foretopgallant-stay" (James III 394).
     - Documented for certain: the fore topgallant stay. Per James only: the main topmast stay.
   - Lt Browne: "The Victory had the English colours and flag in five different places", during the approach.
   - So a White Ensign at the gaff at dawn is a mild anachronism. The vice-admiral's flag at the fore may or may not have been flying (section 7, item 11).
   - *Fact-check (F-35): the position is right, only the timing is in question.* After the spanker boom came in, about 1790, the ensign was hoisted at the peak, the outer end of the spanker gaff, and that stayed its place at sea (Perrin, *British Flags*, 1922). Colours were not normally kept flying at sea: the 1731 Regulations told captains "to husband the Ship's Colours, and not to keep them abroad in windy weather, the Pendant being a sufficient mark of distinction" (quoted by Perrin).
6. **Masts.** British lower masts were painted yellow, including the hoops (Nicolas VII 131). The fleet had been painting "a la Nelson": "employed for this week past to paint the Ship a la Nelson, which most of the Fleet are doing" (Captain George Duff of HMS Mars, letter to his wife, 10 October 1805, quoted in Nicolas VII 71n). This feeds the ship spec.
7. **A tidy column at dawn would be wrong** (section 4.2). Scattered ships are right; the two columns formed between about 07:00 and 09:00.
8. **The crescent is a late-morning shape**, about 10:00–12:00. At dawn the enemy line was a ragged, roughly straight, north–south line heading south.
9. **Sea surface.** Long, smooth swell with no whitecaps (force 1–2). The sun glitter is narrow and banded. Swell crests overtake Victory from astern: the stern lifts first. *Fact-check (F-20): "few or no whitecaps" is the safer wording.*
10. **Moon, stars and planets** (section 2.4–2.5). No morning star. A hairline crescent at most. *Fact-check (F-4): Mercury (−1.1) was technically up, about 5° high in the east at civil dawn. Omitting it is defensible because the eastern horizon was hazy and cloudy and nobody records it, but do not claim that no planet was visible.*
11. **Pre-dawn lights.** The enemy's lights were visible all night, along with British blue lights and rockets.
12. **Caption timing.** "At first light the British sighted the enemy" is historically right: about 05:30–05:45, before sunrise. *Fact-check (F-5): 05:30–06:10 across the logs; Victory's own logs say "At 6". Still before sunrise.* In the scene the enemy appears after sunrise, which is a dramatisation; a lifting haze makes it plausible.
13. **Copy check:**
    - Dateline "Off Cape Trafalgar, at dawn on 21 October 1805": correct; the fleet was about 20 nm west of the Cape.
    - Lede "Twenty-seven British ships of the line. Thirty-three French and Spanish.": correct.
    - Closing caption: correct.

## 7. Doubts and conflicts

1. **Distance to the enemy at daylight.**
   - Figures: 6–7 nm (Collingwood); Victory's master's log 10–12 at 06:00 and 9 at 08:00, or 10–11 and 9–10 in her log as printed by Nicolas *(corrected in fact-check F-16: the original note gave 9–10 as the daylight figure)*; 10–12 (James, Nicolas, Browne); about 12 (Beatty); 12 (Neptune, Britannia); 9 (Belleisle, an unnamed officer); 8 (Wheeler, "15 km").
   - From the French: 2.5 leagues, 7.5 nm (Villeneuve); "about three leagues" (Algésiras); 4–5 miles (Gravina's squadron, which was to windward and so nearer); "dos leguas" (Montañés).
   - Euryalus, between the fleets, had them at 5–6 nm.
   - Use 9–12 nm from Victory.
2. **Wind direction** (section 3.1).
   - Victory's log says NW. Collingwood, Beatty and the French logs say W. James and Gravina's log say WNW. Some ships logged WSW to SW.
   - All are magnetic, so true is about 20° further left.
   - Corbett argues that the general testimony is about west, shifting.
3. **Wind strength.** Most logs say light airs or light breezes. Royal Sovereign, Mars and Agamemnon say moderate. The Spanish logs say "bonancible".
4. **Times of Villeneuve's signal to wear** (section 1.4).
   - Recorded times: 07:00 (Nelson's diary, "wearing in succession", which Corbett says was a misreading); 08:00 (Fraser, Gravina's log, Wikipedia); 08:30 (James, Algésiras); 08:55 (Spartiate); 10:00 (Euryalus, Collingwood).
   - Added in fact-check: about 07:20 (Beatty, "wore in succession about twenty minutes past seven"); 08:15 (Scipion, Desbrière p. 155); 09:30 (Le Tourneur of the Algésiras, Desbrière p. 227).
   - Completion: about 10:00 or later (James "near 10", Fraser "long past ten"). Wikipedia says it took nearly an hour and a half.
5. **Clock spread for Nelson's signals.** The same signals are logged from 05:40 to 07:40 across ships (section 4.3). Victory's 06:40 and 06:50 are later than most. Sunrise at 06:27 LAT falls within this spread.
6. **Visibility.** About half the logs say hazy and about half say clear or fine. Both were true at different times and ships. The haze was thin, since visibility stayed at least 10 nm.
7. **The enemy line's orientation at dawn.** British observers give NNE–SSW (Victory's log, Corbett). Lucas of Redoutable gives "south-east to north-west". Captain Epron of Argonaute and the Algésiras report differ on whether the British were in order.
8. **Column order and counts.**
   - Order after Neptune: Leviathan then Conqueror (Wikipedia), or Conqueror then Leviathan (Nicolas, consistent with James's "Leviathan ... fifth ship").
   - Counts: weather 14 and lee 13 (Beatty); 13 and 14 (French); 11 plus Africa, and 15 (modern).
9. **Studding sails at dawn.**
   - Victory was not under studding sails at first light. She set them on bearing up at about 06:40–06:50 by her clock, close to sunrise, and they stayed set until about 12:20.
   - The brief's rig omits them. Owner decision: add them (documented, but costly to draw), or keep the omission and note it.
10. **Which side the studding sails were on.** Victory's were on both sides (James, Beatty). Téméraire's were on the larboard (weather) side only. Victory's starboard (lee) lower studding sail is documented, so the side seen in the scene would show them.
11. **Flags at dawn.**
    - The Euryalus log and James say the admirals "hoisted their respective flags" and the ensigns at about 12:15.
    - Flag officers' flags were normally kept flying while the admiral was aboard (my inference, not verified for this morning). The vice-admiral's flag at the fore is probably right; a White Ensign at the gaff at dawn probably is not.
    - Britannia's Barclay contradicts James for the enemy ("Eastern horizon ... adorned with French and Spanish ensigns" at 08:00).
    - *Fact-check (F-36): still disputed. No source settles whether the vice-admiral's flag was flying at dawn.*
      - **For a flag flying round the clock.** Basil Hall, on practice early in the 19th century, says the commission pendant "is never hauled down day or night" (Perrin 1922, p. 203). Perrin adds that in a ship wearing an admiral's flag the flag stands in for the pendant (p. 202, stated for his own day).
      - **Against.** The Euryalus log reads "At 12.16 the English Admirals hoisted their respective flags" (NRS II 150; Nicolas VII 205n), and James agrees (III 394). Perrin reads the moment as Collingwood hoisting "his blue flag" when the enemy opened fire (pp. 120–121).
      - **A possible reconciliation.** The flags were flying, and extra or larger flags and colours were added for battle: Victory carried "the English colours and flag in five different places" (Browne, NRS II 197). This is not proven.
      - **Decision.** Keep the flag at the fore as a defensible choice and record it as an assumption.
      - **Rank confirmed.** Victory's return of killed and wounded is headed "bearing the flag of ... Vice-Admiral of the White" (NRS II 186).
    - Scipion's journal backs James on the enemy's ensigns: "en même temps, l'armée a arboré le pavillon de poupe" when firing began at about 12:25 (Desbrière p. 155).
12. **Magnetic versus true.**
    - All log directions are treated here as magnetic, with 21° W variation: the Montañés measured 20° NW; gufm1 gives 21.1° W.
    - Some historians, including James, use the log directions as they stand. This shifts the "sun behind the enemy" geometry by about 20° and turns the brief's "sails east" into about ENE true.
    - The picture convention ("left to right = west to east") is unaffected.
13. **"Northwest of Cape Trafalgar" (Wikipedia).** This conflicts with the primary bearing "Cape Trafalgar E by S" (Collingwood). The fleet was about west of the Cape.
14. **Land visible at first light?** Logs give daylight bearings of the Cape. Villeneuve "made" the Cape at daylight, from a position about 12 nm off. Whether the British saw it from deck at dawn is uncertain. They certainly saw land by late morning.
    - *Fact-check (F-12, F-13): the distances themselves are uncertain.*
    - Dumanoir put the land 6–8 leagues ESE in the morning, and Scipion 8–9 leagues at noon (Desbrière pp. 149, 155).
    - The British noon distances to the Cape run from 15 to 27 nm.
    - These do not fit the dawn "seven leagues" plus a morning's sailing at 2–3 kn. Treat all absolute positions as ±5–10 nm.
15. **Swell numbers** (period, height, wavelength) are not recorded. The values in section 3.3 are typical-range estimates.
16. **The moon.** The computation and USNO agree on a 2 % crescent rising at 05:19 LAT. Nobody records seeing it.
17. **Victory's eye height** of about 9–10 m on the quarterdeck is my estimate. Use the ship research for deck heights. *Fact-check (F-38): plausible. Her lower-deck port sills were only 4 ft 6 in above the waterline (Wikipedia, citing Eastland and Ballantyne). The 205 ft truck height is cited for the ship as first rigged, so it is not verified for 1805.*
18. **Attribution.** The "As the day dawned ... the horizon appeared covered with ships" quote is unattributed in Fraser. It may be Lt Paul Nicolas, RM, of Belleisle, but this is not confirmed.
19. **Collingwood's "Gazette issue 15858".** The naval-history.net page gives this issue number. I have not checked it against the Gazette archive. *Fact-check (F-11): confirmed. The Gazette's own "This month in history" article and a Bonhams lot description both give London Gazette Extraordinary no. 15858, Wednesday 6 November 1805.*

## Fact-check

This is an adversarial check of the note above, made on 24 October 2026. Each claim was tested against an independent source where one exists:

- a second ephemeris (JPL Horizons DE441) in place of the original Meeus scripts and USNO;
- the full texts of the cited books, downloaded from archive.org and searched directly (Nicolas VII, NRS *Logs* II, James III, Corbett, Fraser, Desbrière);
- the page images of Victory's master's log;
- sources the original did not use: Perrin 1922, Espenak's phase catalogue, Desbrière's reports for Dumanoir, Scipion and Le Tourneur, and Moorsom's letter.

**Verdicts:**
- **Confirmed.**
- **Corrected:** the note was wrong and has been fixed in place above, marked *Fact-check* or *Corrected*.
- **Disputed:** the sources disagree, so confidence was lowered.
- **Unverifiable:** no source could be checked in this pass.

The working files (Horizons output, the crossing parser and page images of the Victory log) are not kept in the repository.

### What this changes for the scene

1. **Sun.** No change. Sunrise at 06:27 LAT (06:36 UT), azimuth 102.5° true, confirmed by a second, independent ephemeris.
2. **Western sky.** The Earth's-shadow band (blue-grey, with the pink Belt of Venus above it) *sinks* at dawn. Animate it downwards as p increases, not upwards.
3. **Moon and planets.** Leaving the moon out and showing no morning star is still right. But Mercury was technically above the eastern horizon in civil twilight, so do not write that no planet was up.
4. **Flags.**
   - The White Ensign belongs at the gaff peak when it is flown, but no source has ensigns flying at dawn.
   - Nelson's order calls for one Union Jack, at the fore topgallant stay. The second, at the main topmast stay, rests on James alone. Both belong to about noon.
   - Whether the vice-admiral's flag flew at the fore at dawn is disputed. Keep it, as a stated assumption.
5. **Sea.** Write "few or no whitecaps" rather than "none". The long westerly swell is confirmed.
6. **Victory.** Her log shows 3 kn all morning and a course of E by N magnetic (about 058° true). At sunrise she was most likely still steering N by E under reefed topsails and foresail. She made all sail (reefs out, royals, studding sails) about 20 minutes later. The scene compresses this, as the note already says.
7. **Positions.** The layout is confirmed: British to the west, the enemy strung north–south between them and the coast. The absolute distances to the Cape and to the coast are less certain than the note said and have been downgraded.
