# H3: How British ships signalled in 1805, and the signal chain off Cádiz

Research notes for scene 2 ("The road to Trafalgar"), track H3. They decide how the chain of ships between Cádiz harbour and Nelson's fleet should look when it "lights up" as the Combined Fleet sails, and how the frigates' night signals of 20–21 October should look.

Conventions:

- **Confidence.** High: several independent primary or period sources agree, or the primary document itself (a printed signal book, a log). Medium: one good source, or a consistent inference from good sources. Low: an estimate, or sources in conflict.
- **Quotations.** Only short phrases from public-domain period sources, where the exact wording is the evidence. Everything else is paraphrase. Modern works (Kent 1993 via FOTW, Nicolls 1991, Dempsey) are paraphrased only.
- **Times** are ship's time as logged. Clocks differed between ships: Corbett found the Euryalus about three-quarters of an hour ahead of the Victory on 20 October (Corbett 339 n. 2), and the scene-1 notes found Victory's clock about 30 min behind most of the fleet on the 21st. Compare times across ships with that in mind.
- **Bearings** in logs are magnetic; variation off Cádiz was about 20–21° W (Corbett 333 n. 1; `docs/research/dawn-setting.md`).
- **Page links.** archive.org links use the scan index (`/page/nNNN`), checked against the printed page number. For the NRS *Logs* vol. II, scan index = printed page + 17.

## Summary for the scene

1. **By day (19 October), the chain worked with flags and guns, not lights.** Each link hoisted the signal (numeral flags from the 1799 Signal Book, or Popham's telegraph code) and kept it flying until the next ship repeated it. Guns were fired to draw attention. Nelson ordered the ships bringing the news to fire a gun every three minutes, and the Phoebe's log entry for 8 a.m. reads "firing three minute guns". On the chart this reads as **small bright flags at the mastheads and a white puff of gunsmoke repeated at intervals, passing ship by ship along a line about 50 nautical miles long, running west to west-south-west from Cádiz to the Victory**.
2. **The relay took about two to two and a half hours.** Sirius made No. 370 ("the enemy's ships are coming out of port, or getting under sail") at about 7 a.m., and the Mars repeated it to the Victory at 9.30 a.m. Blackwood wrote that he had told Nelson "within two hours". Nelson answered with the general signal to chase in the south-east quarter, two guns with it, and the whole fleet made sail for the Strait.
3. **By night (20–21 October), the code was lanterns, false fires and blue lights, rockets and guns.**
   - The 1799 *Night Signals* encode numbers by guns (tens), lights in set arrangements (1–4) and false fires or rockets (5).
   - In the presence of an enemy, the book replaces the guns with figures of lanterns: rows, triangles, lozenges and squares.
   - For that night Nelson set a private code. Two blue lights burned together every hour meant the enemy was standing south, towards the Strait. Three guns fired quickly every hour meant they were standing west.
   - The logs record exactly this kind of signalling: "Burnt blue lights and rockets and fired guns all night" (Sirius).
4. **Avoid anything later than 1805:**
   - no semaphore arms;
   - no flashing lamps or beams;
   - no red or green navigation lights or pyrotechnics;
   - no flags at night;
   - no continuous glowing line between the ships;
   - no instant transmission.
   The chain was a sequence of discrete events, minutes apart at night and tens of minutes apart by day.

## 1. Day signals

### 1.1 The Signal Book for the Ships of War (1799)

- **What it was.** The Admiralty's printed day signal book of 1799 came with a separate night volume, *Night Signals and Instructions for the Conduct of Ships of War* (section 2).
  - It continued Lord Howe's "numerary" system of 1790, in which each signal has a number and the number is made by hoisting numeral flags, one flag per digit.
  - The 1799 book grew to 167 pages, with an index and the printed instructions. The private ships' signals were renumbered after the Admiral's signals, giving about 340 in all (Perrin, ch. on signals, section "The Vocabulary Signal Book").
  - Confidence: high.
- **Numeral flags.** There were ten numeral flags (1–9 and 0) plus a substitute, which repeats the flag above it.
  - The flags were changed after the schooner Redbridge was captured in 1803 with a private manuscript copy of the book. An Admiralty circular of 4 November 1803 ordered new flag values, and the Mediterranean fleet changed on 16 January 1804.
  - Those new flags were the ones used at Trafalgar and until the end of 1810 (Perrin).
  - FOTW, from the 1799 Madras edition, describes the flags as mostly bold two- or three-colour designs in red, white, blue and yellow.
  - Confidence: high for the system; the exact 1804 colour values were not transcribed here (see open questions).
- **Manuscript additions.** By Trafalgar the 1799 book had more than 80 signals added by hand (Perrin).
  - Corbett identifies No. 413 as one of them: "the leading ship of the enemy's line bears on the point of the compass shown herewith", from Captain Hope's copy (Corbett 339 n. 2).
  - Confidence: high.
- **Addressing a ship.** Each commander-in-chief had a "pendant board" that gave every ship two pendants, so that a signal could be addressed to one ship (Perrin; for example, Nelson's signal 16 to the Africa).

### 1.2 Popham's *Telegraphic Signals or Marine Vocabulary* (1800, 1803)

- **History.**
  - Popham compiled the vocabulary in 1800 to send messages from his ship Romney off Copenhagen to Admiral Dickson off Elsinore (Popham 1803, introduction dated 1 November 1800).
  - The 1803 edition added a second part of about 1,000 words and a third part of sentences (Perrin; Corbett 298 n. 1).
  - The code was issued to ships of the line from 1803. On 9 September 1805 fifty copies were ordered on board the Victory, with a letter to Nelson (Corbett 298–301).
  - Confidence: high.
- **How a message was made** (Popham 1803, pp. 6–9; Perrin):
  - A diagonally divided red and white flag, the **preparative or "telegraph" flag**, was hoisted before any message.
  - Each word or sentence had a number. A single flag stood for units. In a two-flag hoist the upper flag was the tens; in a three-flag hoist the flags read hundreds, tens and units from the top.
  - Thousands were shown by a pendant or a ball: placed above the hoist for 1000s, below it for 2000s.
  - A word not in the vocabulary was spelt with the numeral alphabet 1–25, in which I and J share 9 and V (20) comes before U (21). "Duty" was 4, 21, 19, 24.
  - The end of a message was shown by a diagonal blue and yellow flag, or by hauling down the telegraph flag.
  - In blowing weather the hoists could be split between masts in the order main, fore, mizzen, gaff, ensign staff.
  - Confidence: high (primary text).
- **The telegraph flag's colours.** The order of red and white is disputed.
  - Popham calls it "a diagonal red and white flag"; Perrin, "divided diagonally into white and red".
  - Wilson's *Flags at Sea* shows red over white and Kent's *Signal!* white over red (both as reported by FOTW).
  - Barraclough says contemporary books show both, so the flag actually used is not known (FOTW).
  - Confidence: medium; irrelevant at chart scale.
- **Speed.** Nelson's "England expects" took twelve hoists and about four minutes in the Victory (Kent 1993 via FOTW). The Euryalus log records repeating it at 11.56 (NRS II 149–150).
  - Confidence: medium for the four minutes.

### 1.3 Making, answering and repeating a signal

- **Popham 1803, p. 6:**
  - The signals were to be kept flying "until they are repeated by the ships in succession, and until they reach the Commander in Chief", whenever he could not see them directly. This is the rule that makes a relay chain work.
  - "Message understood" was shown by the affirmative flag hoisted at the last station and repeated back down the line. "Not understood" was the affirmative with a white pendant under it.
  - In case of doubt, a ship was to repeat the signal instead of hoisting the answering pendant.
  - Private ships could answer with the numeral pendant or the affirmative (p. 9).
  - Confidence: high.
- **The 1796 *Signal-Book for the Ships of War*, the predecessor of the 1799 book** (general Directions, arts. III, VI and IX; the scan's pagination is unreliable):
  - A ship acknowledged a signal with the answering signal and kept it flying until the Admiral hauled his signal down.
  - Flag officers repeated the Admiral's general signals.
  - A private ship's signal to the Admiral was to be repeated by intermediate ships until he was seen to observe it.
  - Confidence: medium as evidence for 1805, since the 1799 book revised the 1796 one; consistent with the 1799 night book below.
- **The same rule at night.** The 1799 *Night Signals* (Sailing Instructions by Night, art. I, p. 27) make signals by private ships repeated by the ships between them and the Admiral until he is seen to answer, and his answer repeated back if needed. Confidence: high.
- **Order of reading.** Long messages were sent as successive hoists. Kent (via FOTW) gives the order of hoisting as main, fore, mizzen; starboard before larboard; upper before lower. Confidence: medium.

### 1.4 Guns fired to draw attention to a signal

The evidence is strong that guns accompanied flags. Confidence: high.

- **1796 Signal-Book, Directions art. II.** Signals were "generally made without Guns, when it may be done with the same Effect". The exceptions were signals of which two or more guns formed part, and signals that had not been noticed in time.
- **The chase signal.** In the 1796 book, signal 7 (chase) adds "If for the whole Fleet to Chace, two Guns". Several private-ship signals were to be repeated by intermediate ships "with one or more Guns, if necessary" until answered.
- **Falconer (1780).** Great guns served "in the day or night, or in a fog" to make or confirm signals or to raise attention. Too many guns cause confusion and give away the squadron's track.
- **Nelson to Duff, 4 October 1805.** If the enemy were out or coming out, "fire guns by day or night, in order to draw my attention" (Nicolas VII 71).
- **Nelson to Collingwood, 10 October.** The vessels bringing the news were to fire a gun every three minutes and burn a rocket from the mast-head every half-hour (Nicolas VII 109–110).
- **Nelson to Blackwood, 10 October.** "fire guns every three minutes by the watch, and in the night to fire off rockets, if they have them, from the mast-head" (Nicolas VII 111).
- **Practice on 19 and 20 October:**
  - Euryalus, 8 a.m. on the 19th: "Phoebe WNW, firing three minute guns" (NRS II 146).
  - Sirius: "the Phoebe made all sail to the northward, firing guns" (NRS II 312).
  - On the 20th, Euryalus repeated her signal to the Agamemnon "with many guns before it was noticed", and ordered Berry to fire every 10 minutes with the signal (NRS II 147–148). The Agamemnon "Fired several signal guns ... Kept the signal up" (NRS II 300).
  - Sirius, on the 20th: "Fired minute guns", in thick rainy weather (NRS II 313).
- **From the fleet.** Midshipman Walker of the Bellerophon: about nine on the 19th "a ship was observed firing guns and making signals" (NRS II 322).
- **Nelson's reply.** His general chase signal of 19 October was made "with two guns" (Beatty 7). Corbett describes it as a single flag, two guns and the S.E. compass flag (Corbett 333 n. 1).

### 1.5 "Distant signals" and how far flags could be read

No period source found gives a reading range in miles. What the sources do say:

- **Nelson to Duff, 4 October (Nicolas VII 70–71).**
  - Mars, Defence and Colossus were to keep "from three to four leagues" (9–12 nm) between the fleet and Cádiz.
  - "Distant Signals" were to be used when the state of the weather kept flags from being read by their colours.
  - In thick weather the ships were to close within signal of the Victory, with one ship placed to the eastward "to extend the distance of seeing".
- **Shape signals existed.** The NMM holds a 1782 manuscript of "Signals made with Flags and Pendants of any colour ... to ships upon the Lookout, or at such distance, that colour is not easily discerned" (RMG SGN/B/7). The form of the 1805 "Distant Signals" was not found.
- **Earlier practice.** The Howe–Kempenfelt sailing instructions of 1781–82 keep look-out ships "six miles distant from the Admiral, but in thick weather to close nearer" (NRS XXXV p. 145). Howe's instructions of 1794 keep the advanced squadron about three miles from the fleet, closing in before night and in thick weather (p. 344).
- **Flags lost at dusk.** On the evening of 19 October Nelson sent the cutter Entreprenante to repeat his telegraph order about mast hoops, fearing "from the lateness of the evening, that the signals might not have been distinctly seen" (Nicolas VII 131).
- **Masthead sighting is not reading.** At 8 a.m. on the 19th, from the Euryalus, "The Defence in sight from the mast head west" (NRS II 146). The Defence was 7–10 leagues west of Cádiz and the Euryalus a few miles off it, so the gap was probably 15–25 nm. That is why the Naiad and the Phoebe were placed in between to repeat.
- **Pendants.** An undated memorandum of about the 1790s in the Banks Papers notes that pendants could not be made out in some lights or in smoke where flags could, and that flag-and-pendant combinations were only distinct at a moderate distance (SL NSW, series 89.04).
- **Flag size.** Nicolls (1991) says signal flags were reduced to about 12 × 14 ft for large ships when three-flag hoists came in, from a recommended 15 × 27 ft earlier. One modern source; medium-low.

**Estimate (low confidence; my own calculation, for the scene only).**

| Quantity | Value |
| --- | --- |
| Masthead to masthead geometric range (look-out aloft ~35 m, flags ~45 m up) | ~26 nm |
| Deck (8 m) to flags (45 m) | ~20 nm |
| A 12 ft flag at 8 nm | ~0.85 arcmin to the naked eye, easily resolved with a period telescope |
| Colour still legible (assumed 20 % contrast threshold), very clear air (visual range 50 km) | ~10–11 nm |
| Clear (30 km) | ~6–7 nm |
| Light haze (20 km, like the morning of 21 October) | ~4–5 nm |
| Rain or thick weather (≤10 km) | ~1–2 nm |

The limit was atmospheric contrast, not resolution: by the Koschmieder relation, contrast falls as exp(−3.912 d/V). A flag's mere presence, or a puff of gunsmoke, could be seen further. This fits the spacing actually used on clear 19 October, about 6–10 nm per link (section 3.3), and the use of guns in the rain on the 20th.

## 2. Night signals

Primary source: *Night Signals and Instructions for the Conduct of Ships of War* (Admiralty, 1799), scanned on archive.org from the ECCO series. Scan index = printed page + 13.

### 2.1 The numeral code (Explanation, pp. 3–5)

| Means | Number |
| --- | --- |
| Two lights at equal heights | 1 |
| Two lights, one over the other | 2 |
| Three lights | 3 |
| Four lights | 4 |
| False fires or rockets (any number of them) | 5 |
| One gun / two guns / three guns ... | 10 / 20 / 30 ... |
| Two lights equal + false fires; two vertical + false fires; three + false fires; four + false fires | 6, 7, 8, 9 |
| Guns + lights and/or false fires | 11–19, 21–29, ... |

How the signals were made:

- The order was fixed: guns first, then lights, then false fires or rockets.
- All signal guns were fired from the same side, so that the report sounded the same.
- Lights were shown wherever the fleet would best see them, at least "twelve feet distant from each other".
- Signal numbers ran up to 70; the page tabs read 1–9, 20, 30 ... 70.
- If the enemy were thought to have the book, the meanings of the numbers would be changed by written memorandum, never the means.

Confidence: high (primary). A 1932 USNI *Proceedings* article by Lt A. R. McCracken describes the same scheme (seen only in a search summary; the page is behind a Cloudflare check).

### 2.2 Directions: repeating, answering, timing (pp. 7–11, 27–30)

- **Repeating the Admiral's signals.** Only the commanders of squadrons repeated them, in order of seniority, four minutes apart. If one failed, the next repeated after six minutes from the last gun (Dir. I).
- **Guns.** Signal guns were fired five seconds apart ("saluting time"). All guns within one minute of each other counted as one signal (Dir. I).
- **Answering.** A signal was answered by one light. When a ship reported a number of sail, the Admiral hoisted and lowered his light each time she did (Dir. II–III).
- **Quarters of the compass.** NE: two lights at equal heights. NW: two lights one over the other. SE: three lights. SW: four lights (Dir. IV).
- **Chasing.** The headmost chasing ship carried a constant light in her stern lantern while she had the chase in sight. She took it in while making a signal and showed it again once the signal was answered (Sailing Instr. V).
- **Avoiding signals.** The Admiral avoided guns and lights where he could: on clear nights the fleet simply followed his motions (Sailing Instr. VII). Nelson ordered exactly this on 19 October (diary, Nicolas VII 133).
- **"The repeating frigates are not to repeat signals with guns, in the night"** (Sailing Instr. VIII). This governs repeating the Admiral's signals within the fleet. The look-out frigates reporting the enemy did fire guns (section 3).
- **Rain.** In very dark, rainy nights the fog signals (guns, drums, bells, muskets) could be used (Sailing Instr. IX).

Confidence: high.

### 2.3 Figures of lanterns "in the presence of an enemy" (pp. 25–26)

Because firing a counted number of guns might be impracticable near an enemy, a second set of signals uses figures of lanterns alone, with false fires added for some numbers. The plates show small square lanterns joined by lines.

| Signal | Figure |
| --- | --- |
| 8 | three lights in a row |
| 20 | four lights in a row |
| 22 | triangle, point up |
| 30 | lozenge (four) |
| 31 | square (four) |
| 34 | triangle with a fourth light mid-base |
| 40 | inverted triangle with a light hanging below |
| 42 | triangle with a light above the apex |
| 47 | inverted triangle with a light mid-top |
| 50, 52, 59, 60, 61, 62, 64, 70 | the same figures plus false fires |

The index gives the meanings: 30 haul to the wind, larboard tack; 31 wear; 34 lie to, starboard tack; 40 and 50 alter course to starboard or port; 47 bear up and sail large; 52 haul to the wind, starboard tack; 59 and 61 form the line of battle; 62 shorten sail; 64 engage in the closest manner.

Confidence: high (plates seen).

### 2.4 Reporting a strange fleet at night (table, pp. 14–15)

| No. | Meaning | Guns | Lights | False fires or rockets |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 21 | On discovering a fleet (then show the quarter as in Dir. IV) | 2 | 2 at equal heights | |
| 23 | They are ships of war (then hoist one light and lower it once **for each strange sail**) | 2 | 3 | |
| 24 | A merchant fleet or convoy | 2 | 4 | |
| 25 | The chase or ships seen are enemies | 2 | | fired |
| 26 | They are friends | 2 | 2 equal | fired |
| 27 | They are lying to | 2 | 2, one over the other | fired |
| 28 | They are standing by the wind on the starboard tack | 2 | 3 | fired |
| 29 | They are standing by the wind on the larboard tack | 2 | 4 | fired |

Confidence: high (page images seen). Nelson's diary says the frigates "told me by signals which tack they were upon" (Nicolas VII 137). The book's Nos. 28 and 29 were the standard way to do that. Whether they, or only Nelson's private code (2.7), were used that night is not recorded (open question).

### 2.5 Instructions for ships watching an enemy fleet at night (pp. 39–41)

This is the doctrine behind Blackwood's watch on the night of 20–21 October. Confidence: high (primary). The ships were:

1. to keep sight of the enemy, and to place themselves so as both to inform the Admiral by signal and to see his signals, the ships nearest him repeating with great care;
2. to carry distinguishing lights placed to be seen by the Admiral and hidden from the enemy;
3. to report the enemy's situation and any change in it by signals the Admiral chooses to establish, and keeps secret;
4. to keep the lanterns covered until all were placed in the figure, then show them "all ... at the same moment", and cover them together when taken in;
5. to keep the signal lights showing for as long as the enemy stays in that situation, with spare lights ready in bags;
6. if the distinguishing lights are in the poop lanterns, to show the signal lights below them on the stern, as far apart as the upper-deck ports;
7. in a chase, to have the ship nearest the enemy carry a light and signal the enemy's motions, while the other watching ships place themselves at intervals between her and the Admiral to repeat.

### 2.6 What the night signals looked like

- **Lanterns.**
  - The plates draw small square glazed lanterns with a domed top and a ring.
  - Night signal lights were plain lights counted by number and arrangement. Neither the 1799 book nor the 1805 accounts mention coloured glass.
  - Red and green navigation sidelights only came in for British steam vessels in 1848 and sailing vessels in 1858 (Admiralty regulations, reprinted in the *New Zealand Gazette* 1858).
  - Confidence: medium-high.
- **False fires and blue lights.**
  - Smyth (1867) defines a false fire as a composition in a wooden tube that burns with a light blue flame for half a minute to several minutes, used as a night signal; a blue light is a pyrotechnic night signal, also called a Bengal light.
  - Period compositions were saltpetre and sulphur, with orpiment or realgar (arsenic sulphides) or antimony, and gave a bluish-white glare.
  - The French called the British ones "feux bleus" (Scipion), "feux azurés" (Bucentaure) and "amorces", priming flashes (Héros, Intrépide).
  - A French officer quoted by Fraser describes "blue lights casting a bright and sudden glare in the midst of profound darkness".
  - Nelson ordered two burned together "in order to make the greater blaze".
  - Confidence: medium (Smyth is later than 1805, but its author served in the war).
- **Rockets ("sky rockets").**
  - Nelson ordered them fired from the mast-head (Nicolas VII 110–111).
  - The 1778 *Encyclopaedia Britannica* describes naval and army signal rockets with and without reports, headed with flares and serpents.
  - Firework colour before about 1811–1830 was essentially the gold of burning gunpowder and charcoal, with weak reds and greens at best; vivid colours came with chlorate compositions (RSC; *Physics World*: Ruggieri, 1811).
  - So: a rising streak of gold sparks, a burst of a few white or gold stars or a bang.
  - Confidence: medium.
- **Guns.**
  - At night: a short orange-white flash and then the report.
  - Gravina's flagship judged British signal guns at about 9 p.m. to be about two miles off "from the interval which elapsed between the flash and report" (Fraser 94). That is about an 11-second delay.
  - Confidence: high for use, medium for appearance.
- **Visibility (estimate, low).** Figures are for an eye on a dark, clear night, using the IALA nominal-range threshold.
  - A candle lantern of about 1–10 candela is visible to about 1–3 nm. Lights 12 ft apart separate to the eye out to about 2–4 nm, so lantern figures were short-range signals.
  - A pyrotechnic flare of hundreds to thousands of candela is visible to about 9–16 nm, limited also by the horizon.
  - Rockets and gun flashes were seen further.
  - This fits the logs: Mars saw blue lights from the frigates to the NE, and the detached Africa saw "the flashes of several guns WSW" at 4 a.m. (NRS II 297).

### 2.7 Nelson's own orders for the look-out ships

- **4 October, to Duff (Mars):** fire guns by day or night to draw attention (above).
- **10 October:**
  - To Collingwood: a gun every three minutes and a rocket from the mast-head every half-hour, after which Nelson would probably bear up for the Strait (Nicolas VII 109–110).
  - To Blackwood: guns every three minutes and, at night, rockets from the mast-head (Nicolas VII 110–111).
- **20 October, Memorandum (Nicolas VII 136; original then with Captain Hope).**
  - **Stations.** Blackwood with two frigates in sight of the enemy in the night. Two other frigates between him and the Defence. Colossus between Defence and Mars. Mars "to communicate with the Victory".
  - **"Signals by night".** If the enemy were standing to the southward or towards the Strait, "burn two blue lights together, every hour, in order to make the greater blaze". If they were standing to the westward, "three guns, quick, every hour".
  - Desbrière printed a French translation (Desbrière, main text p. 179).
- Confidence: high.

## 3. What happened, 19–21 October 1805

### 3.1 The chain as planned (4–15 October)

- **4 October, to Duff:** Mars, Defence and Colossus "from three to four leagues" between the fleet and Cádiz; Blackwood to throw a frigate west of Cádiz (Nicolas VII 70–71).
- **4 October, to Blackwood (Cádiz east 17 leagues):**
  - Mars, Colossus and Defence four leagues east of the fleet, one of them pushed further east towards Cádiz.
  - The fleet 16–18 leagues west of Cádiz.
  - With a frigate thrown west, "in fine weather, we shall communicate daily" (Nicolas VII 73).
- **11 October, to Ball:** five frigates, a brig and a schooner watching Cádiz closely; an advanced squadron of fast ships between Nelson and the frigates; the fleet 15–18 leagues west of Cádiz (Nicolas VII 111).
- **14 October, diary:** Defence and Agamemnon 7–10 leagues west of Cádiz; Mars and Colossus 5 leagues east of the fleet, "whose station is from fifteen to twenty west of Cadiz" (Beatty app. 96–97; Nicolas VII 122). He hoped "by this chain" to keep constant contact with the frigates.
- **15 October, to Hope (Defence):** take station with the Agamemnon 7–10 leagues west of Cádiz; two or three ships would stay between the fleet and them, "if the Enemy should move, I hope to have instant information" (Nicolas VII 122).

The inshore frigates were the Euryalus (Blackwood), Sirius (Prowse), Naiad and Phoebe, with the brig Weazle and the schooner Pickle (Euryalus log; Robinson's *Sea Drift*).

Confidence: high. The chain was **frigates near Cádiz plus line-of-battle ships (Defence, Agamemnon, Colossus, Mars) further out**, not frigates alone.

- **Links in the morning relay.** Inferred: **Sirius → Euryalus → Naiad → Phoebe → Defence → (Colossus) → Mars → Victory**. The Phoebe and the Naiad are named in the Euryalus log, the Defence was the western ship of the frigates' end of the chain (14–15 October orders), and the Mars was the ship that repeated to Nelson.
  - James puts Mars, Defence and Agamemnon in the cordon.
  - Corbett puts Defence and Agamemnon nearest Cádiz, with Mars and Colossus as connecting ships.
  - The Agamemnon's own log shows her busy with a captured brig on the 19th, and on the 20th she nearly ran into the enemy without noticing signals. She probably did not act as a link that morning.
  - Confidence: medium.
- **Corbett's "easternmost".** Corbett calls the Mars "Duff's easternmost ship". By the 14 October diary the Mars was 5 leagues east of the fleet, the ship of the chain nearest the Victory, as Beatty says. "Easternmost" looks like a slip. Low importance.
- **The telegraph was used down the chain the same morning.** Blackwood to his wife, 19 October, written while the enemy were coming out (Nicolas VII 130–131 n.):
  - "Within two hours, though our Fleet was at sixteen leagues off, I have let Lord N. know of their coming out".
  - He was "talking to Lord Nelson by means of Sir H. Popham's signals, though so distant, but repeated along by the rest of the Frigates of this Squadron".
  - Confidence: high.

### 3.3 How long it took, how far, and where Nelson was

- **Duration.** From No. 370 at about 7.00 (frigate time) to the Mars repeating it at 9.30 (Victory time) is about 2½ hours (Corbett; diary). Blackwood says "within two hours". Browne's 10 a.m. would make it about three hours, and Walker's "about nine" about two.
  - The clock offsets (Euryalus roughly 45 min ahead of the Victory) work against Blackwood's estimate, so 2–2½ hours is the best figure.
  - That time included the Phoebe making sail in light airs to take station.
  - Confidence: medium.
- **Distance.** Sixteen leagues, 48 nm (Blackwood; Nelson's diary and his letter to Emma); "about fifty miles W.S.W. of Cadiz" (Nicolas narrative VII 131); "nearly fifty miles to the westward" (Corbett 333); "twenty leagues" (Robinson, writing in 1858).
  - With six or seven links, that is roughly 7–8 nm per link on average.
  - Confidence: medium-high for 48–50 nm.
- **Nelson's position.** The Victory's noon position was 36° 36′ N, 7° 30′ W (Victory's log, via Corbett 333 n. 1). That is about a degree of longitude west of Cádiz, almost on its latitude, so **the chain ran nearly due west from Cádiz**. Sources disagree on Cádiz's bearing:
  - E.S.E. by compass, 16 leagues (Nelson's memorandum book as printed by Beatty, app. 97; Nelson to Emma at noon, "Cadiz, E.S.E., 16 Leagues", Nicolas VII 132);
  - E.N.E. by compass (the same diary as printed from Clarke and M'Arthur, Nicolas VII 133); Corbett already suspected E.N.E. was a slip for E.S.E.;
  - "16 leagues west-south-west from Cadiz" (James III 389), which is the E.N.E. reading from the other end.
  - Confidence: medium-high for "about 48–50 nm west of Cádiz"; the E.N.E. version is the outlier.
- **The 1799 rules for a relay.** Keep the signal flying until the next ship repeats it; repeat until the Admiral answers; send the answer back. So on the chart each link holds its signal until the next one has it.

### 3.4 What Nelson did next

- **The chase.** He made the general chase in the south-east quarter, towards the Strait, at once, without forming the order of sailing (Corbett 333; Beatty 7). Shortly after, he made the signal to prepare for battle.
- **The evening of the 19th.** He directed the fleet to observe his motions during the night. The slow three-deckers Britannia, Prince and Dreadnought were to keep station as convenient. Mars, Orion, Belleisle, Leviathan, Bellerophon and Polyphemus were to go ahead, "carry a light", and stand for the Straits' mouth (diary, Nicolas VII 133; Beatty app. 98).
  - Téméraire's signal log records signal No. 155 to five of them: keep a look-out ahead at a convenient distance for intercourse by night signals, carrying a light in the stern (Corbett 334 n. 2).
- **Dawn on the 20th.** The fleet was at the mouth of the Strait, between Trafalgar and Spartel. It saw nothing, wore, and stood back north-west. At 7 a.m. the Phoebe signalled "the enemy bearing north" (Beatty 8; James III 390).
- Confidence: high.

### 3.5 Night of 19–20 October

- Euryalus, after 8.10 p.m.: "Observed several rockets made by the ships to the westward of us". At 11.45 she saw the ships to the westward "burn a blue light", and after midnight another blue light to windward (NRS II 146–147).
- Naiad: "At 11.40, burned a blue light" (NRS II 302). This matches the Euryalus's 11.45 within the clock difference.
- Sirius, at 6 p.m.: "Saw a rocket and blue light" (NRS II 313).
- Confidence: high.

### 3.6 20 October by day

- **Morning.** In strong breezes, thick weather and rain, Sirius "Fired minute guns" (NRS II 313).
  - Euryalus meets the Agamemnon. She repeats the enemy signal with many guns, then tells her by telegraph that 34 of the enemy are out, to make all sail and repeat signals between the Euryalus and the Admiral, and to fire every ten minutes with the signal (NRS II 147–148).
  - The Agamemnon fires several signal guns and keeps the signal up (NRS II 300).
  - Naiad, at 10: a ship of the line about 4 miles off fires "two guns quick and one slow with signals" (NRS II 302).
- **2.10 p.m.** Euryalus by telegraph to Sirius: "I am going to the Admiral, but will return before night" (NRS II 148).
- **3.20 p.m. (Euryalus time).** Telegraph to the Victory: "The enemy seems determined to push to the westward". It went with the numeral pendant, 30 and N by E, and the Admiral answered (NRS II 148).
  - Nelson's diary: "In the afternoon". Beatty: "A little before sunset". James: just before 5 p.m.
- **4.10 p.m. (Euryalus time), about 3.30 by the Victory.** No. 413 ("leading ship of the enemy's line bears ...") to the Victory, "north, two guns". Téméraire logs the Naiad making it at 3.34 (Corbett 339 n. 2).
- **5 p.m. (Victory; 5.40 by Euryalus).** The Victory signals "I rely on your keeping sight of the enemy" (diary; NRS II 149; Robinson, who remembered it).
- **5 p.m. (Beatty app. 99) or 6 p.m. (autograph diary, Nicolas VII 137).** Naiad signals 31 sail of the enemy NNE.
- Confidence: high for the events; times as given.

Notes on the night:

- **How it was done.** Beatty (pp. 9–10, also Nicolas VII 136) says the night signals were so well arranged that "a certain number of guns, with false fires and blue lights, announced their altering their course, wearing, and making or shortening sail", and that these were repeated by the look-out ships from the Euryalus to the Victory.
  - Nelson's diary: the frigates and look-out ships "told me by signals which tack they were upon".
  - Lt Browne (Victory): "as the enemy tacked or wore we had immediate intelligence of it" (NRS II 195).
  - Captain Harvey (Téméraire): "during the night we saw their lights, rockets, false fires" (NRS II 224).
  - Midshipman Walker (Bellerophon): "During the night we plainly discerned their signals" (NRS II 323).
  - Confidence: high.
- **The six guns (inference, medium-low).** At 9.35 by the Sirius's clock, roughly 8.50 by the Victory's, the Sirius heard six guns from the fleet. That fits the fleet's wear signal: No. 31 is three guns and two lights in the standard code, and it was repeated by a second flag officer under Dir. I.
  - The Euryalus, flying Collingwood's flag, made "the signal with three guns to wear" on the night of the 22nd (NRS II 154).
  - Sirius's own "three guns" at 9.35 may be Nelson's code for "enemy standing to the westward", but the log does not say so.
- **Speed at night (estimate, low).** The rules allowed a few minutes per link: guns five seconds apart, lanterns uncovered together, flag officers repeating four minutes apart.
  - The night chain was short. Euryalus was 2–3 miles from the enemy, and the British fleet's lights 5–6 miles from her; the Phoebe and the Naiad were alongside the Mars and the Colossus.
  - So a report probably reached the Victory in roughly 10–30 minutes, and Nelson's code was repeated every hour.
  - No log times one signal from end to end.

### 3.8 Seen from the Combined Fleet

- **A French officer (Fraser 88).** Lights were seen continually at various points of the horizon, the signals of the English fleet and its look-outs. Then came gunfire from time to time and blue lights "casting a bright and sudden glare in the midst of profound darkness".
- **Prigny, Villeneuve's chief of staff (Desbrière, annexes 139).**
  - The enemy frigates had announced the sortie with gunfire ("coups de canon par bordées", which can mean in broadsides or on each tack).
  - All night they signalled "with rockets, guns and composition fires".
  - At midnight their signals multiplied so much that the French believed them very close.
- **Scipion** (Bérenger, annexes 154): continual signals "avec des feux bleus", priming flashes ("amorces") and rockets.
- **Bucentaure** (Magendie, annexes 178): at 7 p.m. "plusieurs feux azurés" and rockets at intervals a little ahead of the fleet; during the night, enemy lights and several guns.
- **Héros** (Conor, annexes 167) and **Intrépide** (Infernet, annexes 205): rockets, priming flashes and guns to windward.
- **Cornélie** (annexes 159): the British signals "pouvaient facilement se reconnaître à la couleur des feux". **Argus** (Taillard, annexes 269): "la variété des couleurs de leurs feux". In both cases the colour that stands out is the bluish blue lights against the French yellow lanterns and gold rockets. These remarks do not prove red or green pyrotechnics.
- **Gravina's flagship (Fraser 94).** At nine the English made signals with guns, judged about two miles off by flash and report.
- **Lucas, Redoutable (Fraser 91, in translation).** The British squadron to windward made many signals with "a remarkable display of coloured fires".
- **A British officer in the Defence (Fraser 95).** "we concealed every light", while the Combined Fleet showed profuse lights and night signals. The enemy line looked "like a well lit up street" about six miles long (Fraser 95; the source of that phrase is not given).
- **Prigny (annexes 139) on the French side.** At nightfall the French lit their stern and top lights and fired rockets now and then to mark their positions.
- **Midshipman Hercules Robinson, Euryalus (*Sea Drift* 209; Fraser 96).** For two days they communicated every movement. When the fleets were brought together they took their place "between the two lines of lights, as a cab might in Regent Street". Robinson wrote fifty years later: he names the Hydra among the frigates and says the hoops were painted white; the contemporary sources give the Hydra no part in the watch and the hoops as yellow.
- Confidence: high that British signals were blue lights, guns and rockets and were plainly visible to the enemy.

## 4. Recommendation for the chart

### 4.1 By day: the relay of 19 October (beat 4)

- **Geometry.** Draw a line of 7–8 glyphs from just off Cádiz (Sirius about 4 nm off the harbour, the Euryalus nearby) running west to west-south-west for about 48–50 nm to the Victory and the fleet. The legs:
  - the Naiad and the Phoebe in the first 10–20 nm;
  - the Defence (and, if shown, the Agamemnon) 7–10 leagues (21–30 nm) out;
  - the Colossus and the Mars about 5 leagues (15 nm) short of the fleet;
  - the fleet itself 15–20 leagues west of Cádiz, on about 36.6° N.
  
  Spacing about 6–10 nm per link. The fleet stays below the horizon from Cádiz, but each link sees the next from its masthead.
- **Light.** About 6–9.30 a.m., sun rising in the east-south-east, behind Cádiz; light airs and clear (Euryalus log).
- **Each link "lights up" as:**
  1. **a small cluster of flags at the masthead**, two to four tiny flags, bright red, white, blue and yellow, hand-tinted dabs in the engraved style, that stay up until the next ship repeats;
  2. **a puff of white gunsmoke at the ship's side**, recurring every few seconds of scroll to stand for "every three minutes" (Phoebe "firing three minute guns", Nelson's orders). Smoke drawn as small ink-outlined puffs, drifting slowly, consistent with the Finisterre smoke. No muzzle flash is needed by day; at most a flicker.
  3. Then the next ship, after a pause. Real time: about 2–2½ hours from the Sirius to the Victory. Keep a sense of steps: the relay advances in pulses, never as a continuous moving light.
- **At the Victory.** The general chase signal (one flag, the S.E. compass signal, **two guns**: two puffs). The fleet's glyphs then turn south-east for the Strait under all sail.
- **Optional detail.**
  - For Blackwood's telegraph messages, a longer run of successive hoists under the red and white telegraph flag.
  - For the first signal, Sirius's report of "topsail yards hoisted" (by telegraph), then No. 370.

### 4.2 By night: 20–21 October (beat 5, the night passage)

- **The chain is short.** Euryalus and Sirius 2–4 nm upwind (west and north-west) of the enemy line; Phoebe and Naiad, then Defence, Colossus and Mars; the Victory 5–6 nm from the Euryalus. The whole chain is about 10 nm between the converging fleets, not 50.
- **The British fleet is dark.** Only a few warm points: the stern and top lights of the ships ordered to carry a light, and Victory's blue lights marking her position after she wore. The inshore ships hide their lights.
- **The Combined Fleet is a long string of warm lights**, "like a well lit up street", with occasional gold rockets of its own.
- **Signals, as discrete events:**
  - **Blue lights or false fires:** a small, intense bluish-white flare with a tight halo, lighting its own smoke and the ship's sails for about 30–60 s, then gone. In pairs every hour while the enemy stands south (Nelson's code).
  - **Guns:** a brief orange-white flash and a lit puff. Counted groups, five seconds apart; "three guns, quick" for "standing west".
  - **Rockets:** a thin rising arc of gold sparks from the masthead, 3–5 s, ending in a small burst of white or gold stars or a bang.
  - **Lanterns:** dim warm points in a figure, appearing all at once (the covered-lantern rule) and held. A row, a triangle, a lozenge or a square below the stern lights; or a single light hoisted and lowered once for each enemy sail counted.
- **Pacing.** Each signal answered and repeated link by link within minutes: a ripple of flashes running from the enemy side to the Victory. The fleet's own wear signals at about 8–8.40 p.m. and at 4 a.m.: three-gun flashes from the Victory, answered.

### 4.3 What would be wrong or anachronistic

- **Ship semaphore or telegraph arms.** No ship carried them in 1805. The Admiralty's shutter telegraph (1796) and Popham's semaphore (from 1816) stood on land, and semaphore came into Royal Navy use at sea only in the mid-19th century (Nicolls 1991). The "telegraph" of 1805 was Popham's flag code.
- **Flashing lamps or Morse.** Colomb's flashing signals were adopted on 12 February 1867; before that, night signals "were still made in the primitive manner devised in the seventeenth century" (DNB, Laughton).
- **Beams of light**, searchlights or limelight (19th century).
- **Red and green lights.** No red or green running lights (1848 for steam and 1858 for sail in Britain), no coloured lanterns, no red or green rockets and flares. 1805 colours are warm lantern yellow, bluish-white blue lights, gold sparks and orange gun flashes.
- **Flags at night**, or modern International Code flags in place of the 1799/1804 numeral flags.
- **A continuous glowing line, or a "data packet" running between ships.** It is not historical and it breaks the scene's avoid-list. Signals were separate events at separate ships.
- **Instant transmission.** By day it took about 2 h over 48 nm; at night minutes per link, with hourly reports.
- **"England expects" style long hoists all along the chain on the 19th.** The morning relay carried a numbered signal (370), though Blackwood also sent telegraph messages down the chain.
- **A chain of frigates only.** Three or four of the links were ships of the line.

## 5. Where the sources differ from the brief

1. **The chain was not only frigates.** The brief's caption reads "linked to the coast by a chain of frigates". The sources give frigates off Cádiz (Euryalus, Sirius, Naiad, Phoebe, plus the Weazle and the Pickle) and line-of-battle ships between them and the fleet (Defence, Agamemnon, Colossus, Mars). Suggested wording: "a chain of frigates and ships of the line".
2. **"Lights up" on 19 October means flags and gunsmoke, not lights.** The combined fleet began to sail at about 7 a.m. and the signal ran down the chain in daylight. Lights, blue lights and rockets belong to the nights of 19–20 and 20–21 October.
3. **The chain changed size.** On the 19th it was about 48–50 nm long, west from Cádiz. On the night of 20–21 October it was about 10 nm, between the closing fleets.
4. **Timing.** The relay took roughly two to two and a half hours (07.00 to 09.30), not minutes.

## 7. Open questions

1. The form of the 1805 "Distant Signals" Nelson told Duff to use: shapes, or a flag-and-pendant code independent of colour. The 1799 day book at URI (digitalcommons.uri.edu/sc_pubs/15) is behind a Cloudflare check and was not read.
2. The 19 October logs and signal logs of the Phoebe, Defence, Colossus, Mars and Victory (TNA ADM 51/52) are not printed in the NRS volume, so the time at each link is unknown. Only the ends are timed.
3. Was No. 370 in the printed 1799 book or a manuscript addition? Its wording varies between "coming out of port" (NRS editor) and "coming out of port or getting under sail" (Corbett).
4. On the night of 20–21 October, did the frigates use the book's Nos. 28 and 29 (tack signals), Nelson's private blue-light and gun code, or both? Was Sirius's "three guns" at 9.35 the "standing west" signal?
5. No period figure was found for the range at which flags could be read; the figures above are estimates.
6. The colours of the 1804–1810 numeral flags (Perrin's Plate XIII, last column) were not transcribed. They only matter if flags are drawn large.
7. Was a hoist "broken out" (sent up furled and shaken open) in 1805? That practice is documented for the late 19th century (Dempsey), not for 1805, so it is safer to show flags simply hoisted.
8. The USNI 1932 article by McCracken, "Signaling in the British Navy, 1800", could not be opened. A search summary credits it with a sail-and-gun signal for "discovered a strange fleet"; that was not verified.
