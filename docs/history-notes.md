# History notes

Facts the scene uses, with their sources, confidence and doubts. This file is the summary; the full research notes, with measurements, tables, short quotations from period sources and every URL, are in `docs/research/` (what may and may not go there: `docs/research/README.md`):

- `research/victory-hull.md`: Victory's hull as of October 1805 (dimensions, ports, paint, details, what not to copy from the present ship).
- `research/victory-rig.md`: masts, spars, sails, rigging and flags, with an elevation coordinate table.
- `research/dawn-setting.md`: positions, sun, moon, weather, both fleets, and a fact-check of 54 claims against independent sources (its last section).

Confidence: **high** means several independent primary sources agree, a museum artefact, or a computation confirmed by a second ephemeris; **medium** means one good source or a consistent inference; **low** means an estimate, a measurement from a photograph, or conflicting sources.

Times are local apparent time (ship's time). Bearings in 1805 logs are magnetic; the variation off Cádiz was about 21° W, so true = magnetic − 21°.

## The setting

- **Place and layout.** The British fleet was about 20 nm west of Cape Trafalgar (the Cape "bore E by S about seven leagues", Collingwood). The Combined Fleet lay between it and the Spanish coast, 9–12 nm east of Victory, strung out north–south over about 5 nm, heading south on the starboard tack. Layout high; distances medium.
- **Sunrise.** Upper limb at 06:27 local apparent time (06:36 UT), azimuth 102.5° true; the whole disc clear by about 06:30. Civil dawn 06:01, nautical dawn 05:31; the logs' "daylight" falls at 05:30–06:10. The sun climbs at about 53° to the horizon, up and to the right. High (computed; matches USNO and JPL Horizons DE441).
- **Moon.** A waning crescent about 2 % lit, rising at 05:19 in the east and about 12° up at sunrise; new moon on 22 October. The night had been moonless. No account mentions seeing it. High (computed). The scene shows it faintly at p = 0 and lets it fade.
- **Stars.** The scene's sparse stars fade in the first third of the scroll, before civil dawn would have washed them out. Mercury (magnitude −1.1) was about 5° up in the east at civil dawn, deep in the glow and haze; nobody records it, and the scene leaves it out.
- **Sky and cloud.** The night's squalls had passed. Gravina's flagship logged squall cloud in the north-east and south-east quadrants at dawn, so cloud banks on the eastern horizon are supported; several British logs say "cloudy". The scene has low, broken strands near the sunrise. High.
- **Wind.** Very light (Beaufort 1–2), westerly, dying toward calm by noon. Victory's log at 08:00: "light breezes and cloudy". High for "light westerly"; the exact direction is disputed (NW, WNW, W and WSW in different logs, all magnetic).
- **Swell.** "A great swell from the westward" (Euryalus), "the sea smooth with a great ground swell setting from the westward" (James), "la houle de l'Ouest très forte" (French logs). Few or no whitecaps. High. Period and height were not recorded; the scene uses a long, slow swell.
- **Visibility.** At least 10–12 nm: the enemy's 33 ships were counted at that range. Their hulls were partly below the horizon (3–12 m hidden from an eye 5–10 m up), so sails and upper works show and waterlines do not. Each rig subtends about 0.3 of the sun's diameter. High (computed).

## The fleets

- **British.** 27 ships of the line. At dawn they were in loose cruising order, not yet two tidy columns ("sans ordre", Dumanoir and Scipion), and the columns formed over about two hours. Once formed, the first two ships astern of Victory in the weather column were the three-deckers Téméraire (98) and Neptune (98). Collingwood's lee column was to the south, out of a north-facing frame. High. The scene shows Téméraire, Neptune and a 74 astern, faint in the haze.
- **Combined Fleet.** 33 ships of the line (18 French, 15 Spanish), plus 5 frigates and 2 brigs; easy sail at dawn (mostly topsails), close-hauled on the starboard tack, heading south, a ragged line of about 5 nm. The crescent shape came only after Villeneuve's order to wear (about 08:00–08:30), finished around 10:00, so the dawn line is a ragged straight line, not a crescent. High. Seen from the west, ships heading south show their bows to the right.
- **The enemy's paint** (one eyewitness, Midshipman Badcock): some black and yellow like the British, some with a single red or yellow band, some all black; the Santísima Trinidad had four red bands with white between. Irrelevant at silhouette scale; against the sunrise they read as soft mid-tone silhouettes.

## HMS Victory as of October 1805

After the 1800–1803 great repair. The most valuable source is the contemporary 1:48 model made during that repair (Royal Museums Greenwich SLR0513), photographed broadside-on; several heights and positions are measured from those photographs and from RMG plans (ZAZ0120–0122), scaled from the 186 ft gun deck.

### Hull
- Gun deck 186 ft (56.7 m); figurehead to taffrail about 226 ft 6 in (69.0 m); extreme breadth 51 ft 10 in. High.
- Armament at Trafalgar: 104 guns. Guns per side 15 lower, 14 middle, 15 upper, 6 quarterdeck, 2 forecastle (one a carronade), so some ports carried no gun. High.
- Ports (see the table above). Port spacing about 11.5 ft; the middle-deck ports are staggered between the others. The eight central upper-deck ports (3rd–10th from forward) and the forward quarterdeck openings had no lids, so they show as dark openings even with the ports closed. Medium.
- Heights above the waterline (measured, about ±1 ft): lower port sills about 4 ft 6 in; bands at about 1.5–2.7, 3.7–5.0 and 5.9–7.0 m; channels about 7 m (above the upper-deck ports, moved there in 1800–03); waist rail about 8.8 m, quarterdeck 10.1, forecastle 10.3–10.9, poop at the taffrail about 12.5. Medium-low.
- Sheer: gentle, lowest a little abaft amidships, rising about 1.5 m at the stern and 1.7 m at the bow. Medium-low.
- Paint: black (near-neutral) hull and lids, pale ochre bands: the Nelson chequer, correct for Trafalgar (when she left the repair in 1803 the lids were still ochre). Port linings and inner lid faces red. Medium-high. The whole of the British lower masts, hoops included, were painted yellow; the enemy's mast hoops were black, and Nelson had Belleisle and Polyphemus paint theirs to avoid confusion (Nicolas VII 131). High.
- Copper: only a thin strip shows at the waterline. Low (inferred; no documented figure).
- Bow: an open beakhead with a bulkhead at upper-deck level, three curved headrails, catheads, and a figurehead of the crowned royal arms held by two cupids (blue sash to starboard). The round bow of pre-1922 photographs is the 1814 bow and wrong for 1805. Medium-high.
- Stern: closed in 1803, three tiers of windows; the quarter gallery has three tiers from the middle deck to the quarterdeck; no stern davits (removed in 1800–03). Medium-high.

### Rig and sails
- Spars from David Steel's 1794 Royal Navy table for a 100-gun ship of 2,164 tons (Victory: 2,142). It matches her surviving 1803 fore topsail (80 ft foot, 54 ft head, 54 ft deep) within a foot or two. Main mast 117 ft, topmast 70, topgallant 35; main yard 102 ft 4 in; bowsprit 74 ft at "nearly thirty-six degrees" (drawn at 34°), jib-boom 53 ft. Medium (Steel as a close proxy; McKay's Victory-specific tables were not available).
- Mast positions by the Royal Navy rule (fore 1/9, main 5/9, mizzen 17/20 of the gun deck from the stem); rake about 0.5°, 2° and 3.5°. Low-medium.
- Sails set: fore and main course, topsail, topgallant, royal; mizzen topsail and topgallant; the spanker on gaff and boom (by 1805 a gaff and boom had replaced the old mizzen yard; logs of 1805 say "spanker"); fore topmast staysail, jib, flying jib. Medium-high.
- Topsails had four reef bands (Victory's log, 24 October: "out fourth reef of main topsail") and straight feet; courses one reef band plus a middle band, feet cut slightly hollow. High (main topsail), medium (others).
- The wind was 2–3 points abaft the beam, so the yards were braced about 30–40° from square, turning the sails' forward, sunlit faces toward a viewer on the starboard side. In strict broadside they show about 50–64 % of their width. The ship is drawn with yards braced about 30° and sails at 64 % width, as period ship portraitists (Pocock, Turner) did. Medium.
- Rigging: shrouds about 10 fore, 11 main, 7 mizzen a side (from the restored ship and a kit manual, not an 1805 source); topmast shrouds about 5/5/4. Low-medium.

### Flags
- Vice-admiral's flag of the White (white, plain red St George's cross) at the fore topgallant masthead: confirmed by the 1808 Regulations, an RMG catalogue note and Pocock's 1805 drawing. High for the position.
- White Ensign with the post-1801 Union canton, at the peak of the spanker gaff when flown under sail (Perrin 1922). High for design and position. **Not flown at dawn** (owner's decision, see below); the spec keeps it, with its position and size, for the approach scene.
- No commission pennant while an admiral's flag flies. Medium-high.
- Ensigns were about twice as long as deep (a surviving 1794 ensign is 20 × 40 ft). The flags are drawn about 1:2 and foreshortened, since they stream toward the viewer as well as to the right.
- The Union Jacks on the stays and "England expects" (about 11:45) belong to late morning, not dawn, and are not shown.

## Assumptions and licences

1. **Ports closed at dawn.** An assumption, as the brief says. The lidless ports still show as openings.
2. **No White Ensign at dawn** (owner's decision, session 3). The Euryalus log and James have colours hoisted at about 12:15, as the action began; no source has ensigns flying at dawn. The brief asked for one; the owner removed it. It will be hoisted in the approach scene, before battle, as a deliberate moment.
3. **The vice-admiral's flag at dawn.** Kept (owner's decision, session 3): as he understands it, a flag officer's flag flew for as long as he was aboard in command. The sources do not clearly say otherwise. For: Basil Hall (via Perrin) has the commission pendant never hauled down, day or night, and in a flagship the admiral's flag takes its place; the 1808 Regulations keep even a fallen commander-in-chief's flag flying until the battle ends. Against, at most: the Euryalus log and James have the admirals "hoisting their respective flags" at about 12:16, which can also be read as battle flags added to the ones already flying. No source says Nelson's flag was down at dawn.
4. **No studding sails in scene 1** (owner's decision, session 3). The log has them set on bearing up, about 20–30 minutes after sunrise, and later accounts have them on both sides; at the moment of sunrise Victory was most likely still steering N by E under reefed topsails and foresail. They belong to the approach scene, where the fleet sets every scrap of canvas in the light wind (`docs/backlog.md`).
5. **The British in a column.** At dawn they were in loose order; the scene shows the weather column already forming astern of Victory, which is how it looked within the next two hours.
6. **Scale of the distant ships.** At true angular size the enemy rigs would be a few pixels tall. They are drawn larger, still about a third of the sun's diameter as the research advises, so they read as ships.
7. **The sun in frame.** Artistic licence, accepted by the owner: the real sun rose behind a north-facing viewer's right shoulder (see 8), but the scene shows it rising on the right of the frame.
8. **Viewpoint.** A side view looking north with the sun and the enemy on the right is a stylised panorama: the real enemy line ran north–south across 90° of the eastern horizon, and the sun rose at 102.5° true, behind a north-facing viewer's right shoulder.

## Open doubts

- Lower-deck ports: 16 a side (a Trafalgar midshipman, the 2012 official site, the present ship) against 15 on the contemporary model. Settled by the owner for the scene: 15, as on the model, matching her 15 lower-deck guns a side.
- The main truck height (59.5–61 m by reconstruction against the quoted 62.5 m) depends on the assumed heel depth and Steel's long-pole rule. The owner accepted the 60.2 m reconstruction from Steel's 1794 tables.
- Gaff, spanker boom, flying jib-boom and dolphin striker lengths are rule-based estimates.
- Quarterdeck (7 or 8) and forecastle (2 or 3) openings; the poop bulwark (open rail on the model, solid in one reconstruction).
- Red inside the ports: the museum's specifications use red, but a summary of Goodwin's 2013 paint article says the 1805 accounts list no red. Deferred by the owner until a scene shows the ports open (`docs/backlog.md`); in scene 1 the colour shows only through the lidless upper-deck, quarterdeck and forecastle openings.
- The exact wind direction at dawn; the distances to the enemy (6–12 nm across sources) and to the coast.
- Whether the Spanish coast was visible from deck at first light (certainly seen later in the morning).
- Not consulted first-hand: McKay's *Anatomy of the Ship* drawings and spar tables, McGowan (2003), Goodwin's *Pocket Manual* (2015) and his 2013 *Mariner's Mirror* article.

## Scene 2: the strengths lettered at the tracks' heads (sessions 20 and 21)

On the far views of scene 2 each squadron is lettered at its track's head with its commander and its strength ("Villeneuve, 20 sail"), and the October fleets off Cádiz among their ships ("Nelson, 27 sail"). This section records what the label counts, day by day, and where the sources part. It joins the Scene 2 section when that is merged (`docs/research/scene-02/session-17/history-scene-02-wip.md`). The research behind it, with every page and link: `docs/research/scene-02/session-21/strengths-by-date.md`.

**The rule (the owner's answers of 12 November 2026).** The label counts ships of the line only, as the dispatches counted a fleet's strength and as the finale's 27 against 33 does. Frigates, small craft and prizes are left out. One rule holds everywhere: the figure is the ships of the line actually sailing together at the head of the track on that date. A straggler is added on the day she caught up. A ship that left is taken off on the day she left. Villeneuve's crossing begins with the 14 who went out with him from Cádiz. The figure and the ships that come in at the head as the camera nears always agree, and the figure changes smoothly. Where no source gives the day, the most probable is taken and recorded here. High.

**What "sail" counted then.** Where a dispatch gave a fleet's strength it counted ships of the line apart from the rest. Calder's own dispatch has the enemy at "20 Sail of the Line", with ships armed en flûte, frigates and brigs listed after them, and his own as 15 of the line, two frigates, a cutter and a lugger (Naval Chronicle XIV, p. 163). Nelson's frigates reported the Toulon fleet as eleven of the line, seven frigates and two brigs (Nicolas VI, p. 396). Where strength was the subject, bare "sail" meant ships of the line too: Nelson's memorandum of 9 October gives the enemy as "thirty-four sail" (Nicolas VII, pp. 89–92). In a sighting the same word took in every vessel in view (the Defiance's "24, then 27 sail" when 20 of the line were there; Corbett, p. 196).

**The British fleet off Cádiz, by date.**
- **29 from 6 p.m. on 28 September.** Nelson's three joined Collingwood's 26: 23 in the main body and 6 inshore under Rear-Admiral Louis (the Victory's log and signal log, Nicolas VII 53 n.; Nelson to Acton and to Elliot, 30 September, VII 54, 59; the 26 named in Desbrière 1907, p. 143).
- **23 from 6 p.m. on 2 October.** Louis sailed for Gibraltar and Tetuan with the Queen, Canopus, Spencer, Tigre and Zealous. The log enters this under the nautical "October 3rd. P.M. at 6" (VII 63 n.; Nelson's diary, Hudson 1917, pp. 19–20; Austen, Hubback 1906, p. 148). The Leviathan was also away at Gibraltar: Nelson wrote on the 5th that he had sent "six Sail of the Line" and had "twenty-three Sail with me" (VII 75).
- **24 from the morning of 7 October.** The Defiance joined (VII 84).
- **26 on 8 October.** The Leviathan came back from Gibraltar, and the Royal Sovereign joined at about 4 p.m. (VII 88 and n., 107; Nelson's diary, Hudson 1917, the transcript facing p. 20).
- **27 from the afternoon of 10 October.** The Belleisle joined (VII 101).
- **28 from the forenoon of 13 October.** The Agamemnon joined (VII 119).
- **28 through the night of 13–14 October.** The Prince of Wales left on the evening of the 13th (VII 119–120; Beatty 1825, pp. 5–6; Corbett 1910, p. 330), and the Africa joined by the forenoon of the 14th (the Victory's log under the nautical "October 14th", VII 121 n.; VII 94 n., 121). Strictly the fleet was 27 for those hours; the label takes the two as one exchange at 6 p.m. on the 13th, so the figure stays at 28 (its ships, one for the other).
- **27 from 17 October to the battle.** The Donegal was sent to Gibraltar for a ground tier of casks (the diary, Beatty 1825, p. 97; James III 383); no hour is recorded, 3 p.m. is taken.

The peak was 28. Sources that differ: James (III 379) and Beatty (1825, p. 3) give 27 on Nelson's arrival; James and Clowes give a peak of 29, which needs the Africa in before the Prince of Wales sailed, which no source shows; James and Clowes put the Royal Sovereign "between the 9th and 13th" and the Prince of Wales on the 14th, the log's nautical date; Beatty's narrative has the Donegal leaving on the 18th, probably the log's date; Desbrière (p. 148) has Louis sailing on 3 October, the log's date read literally. High for 29, 23 by the 5th and 27; medium-high for the steps between.

**Louis's brief return, 4–5 October.** On the morning of the 3rd the Euryalus told Louis by signal that the enemy had embarked their troops. He sent the Zealous and the Endymion on with the convoy and turned back with the other four (Austen, Hubback 1906, pp. 148–149; James III 380). On the 4th they were in sight to the southward (the diary, Hudson 1917; Codrington, *Memoir* I 53). They were with the Commander-in-Chief on the morning of the 5th and sent off again that day (Austen; Beatty, p. 4, has them rejoin on the 4th). Nelson counted 23 that day. The label does not count those hours. Medium-high.

**The Leviathan.** She was at Gibraltar from early October and came back on the 8th "loaded" (Nelson to Collingwood, 8 October, VII 88; James III 380). No source records the day she went. The 29 of 28 September include her, and Nelson's 23 of 5 October leave her out. The Victory's log names only Louis's five and the Endymion parting on 2 October, and Austen makes Louis's group five of the line, so she did not sail with them. Nelson's letters of 2 and 5 October count the ships sent to Gibraltar and Tetuan as six (VII 63, 75). She had been in Louis's inshore squadron in August (Austen's log, Hubback 1906, p. 142), and on 1 October Louis came out to the fleet with only part of his squadron (Beatty, p. 4), so she may have gone straight from the inshore station on 30 September or 1 October. The label takes her off with Louis's five at 6 p.m. on 2 October. Low for the day. Her own log (Bedfordshire Archives, X170/1/2) or the Victory's for those days would settle it; neither is online.

**The combined fleet in Cádiz.** 32 ready on 28 September: 18 French and 14 Spanish. Gravina told Villeneuve that day that his 14 were entirely ready and the Santa Ana, a three-decker just out of dock, would follow in four or five days (Gravina to Decrès, 28 September, Desbrière 1907, annexes p. 91). 33 from early October: Desbrière has the Santa Ana ready only in the first days of October (p. 134; his p. 136 paraphrases the same letter as nine days), and on 8 October the council of war counted her with the fleet, "à peine sortis de l'arsenal" (annexes p. 97). The label takes noon on 3 October. Medium; low for the day. Nothing else came or went until the sortie: the Terrible and the San Fulgencio lay disarmed or in dock, and British counts from outside of 34–36 take in ships in harbour that were not with the fleet. Seven of the line got out on the 19th and the rest on the morning of the 20th; the label stays at 33 through the sortie, one fleet under one flag.

**The crossing's 14.** Villeneuve went out from Cádiz at about 2 a.m. on 10 April with 14 by the owner's choice: the 11 from Toulon, the Aigle, the Argonauta and the América (Desbrière 1907, pp. 9–11). The record has two days that differ: at dawn on the 10th only the Argonauta was with the 12 French, and the América rejoined on the 12th (Reille, p. 11), or "next morning" by Villeneuve's report (p. 10); strictly that gives 13 for two days. James (III 342) has 17 getting under way together, with the San Rafael following. The Firme, Terrible and España were found off Fort-de-France on 14 May (17; the hour is not recorded, 4 p.m. is taken), and the San Rafael anchored with the fleet in the afternoon of the 16th after Diamond Rock had fired on her in the morning (18; James III 343–344; 3 p.m. is taken, where session 20 had noon). High.

**Off Finisterre.** The Firme struck soon after 8 p.m. on 22 July and the San Rafael a few minutes later (James III 361; the label takes 8.30): 20 to 18. Lauriston, writing from Cádiz on 2 September with news from the two Spanish captains, has the San Rafael striking at 9 p.m. (Desbrière, annexes p. 111); Desbrière's narrative has both fighting on through part of the night and striking at daybreak on the 23rd (pp. 63–64). Medium for the hour.

**Vigo, A Coruña and Ferrol.**
- **15 out of Vigo on 31 July:** the Atlas, España and América left there (Desbrière pp. 85–86; James: the 30th).
- **13 from 2 August.** On entering the bay the two Spaniards, with Gravina already in the channel, had to go into Ferrol under the Emperor's order, and the 13 French anchored at A Coruña (Desbrière 1907, p. 86; James, Mahan and Corbett's narrative have him in on 1 August). New under the rule; session 20 kept 15 until the 13th.
- **26 from the afternoon of 10 August.** The French worked out of A Coruña on the 8th to 10th. On the 10th, at about 12.30, Gourdon's five and eight Spaniards came out of the ría of Ferrol; the wind died before the last three cleared the narrows, and all anchored in the bay of Ares (p. 97; Escaño's journal, annexes p. 7, has nine Spaniards, which would make 27). The label takes 2 p.m.
- **29 from the forenoon of 13 August.** The last three joined, and all were under sail by 2 p.m. (pp. 97–98; Escaño has the fleet sailing from the ría of Ares at 9 a.m.; James: out on the 11th). The label takes 9 a.m.

High for the split, medium for 26.

**Smaller dates.**
- **Nelson.** The Royal Sovereign left him between 4 p.m. and 6.50 p.m. on 11 May, while Knight's Queen and Dragon were in company, not under Nelson's flag and not counted (Nicolas VI 433); 5 p.m. is taken. His flag was rehoisted in the Victory at St Helens at 11.30 a.m. on 14 September (VII 36), so "Victory" is lettered from then (session 20 had 8 a.m. on the 15th, when she weighed).
- **Collingwood.** The Mars rejoined at midnight on 20–21 August (James III 375): 3 to 4. Bickerton's four joined on the 22nd, hour not recorded, and made 8 (James III 379; Desbrière p. 143); his label closes at noon on the 22nd, as they join: from there he is the fleet off Cádiz, which the scene letters only with Nelson's arrival.
- **Calder.** He became 14 at 8 p.m. on 26 July, when the Windsor Castle went home with the prizes (James III 371), after his window closes at noon on the 26th; the label stays 15.

**What the chart draws for these figures.** A squadron at a track's head is a block of its ships of the line only, the ships its label counts: the frigate Euryalus is no longer drawn with the Victory in September, nor Calder's two prizes with his line. Each block's places are its ships, one each or in turn: off Finisterre the two that struck are the Firme and the San Rafael, Spanish glyphs. The October fleets draw the 27 and 33 of Trafalgar, each ship from the day she was with the fleet (the Defiance, Royal Sovereign, Belleisle, Agamemnon and Africa joining, the Leviathan away from 2 to 8 October, the Santa Ana from 3 October). The seven British ships of the line there in those weeks but not at Trafalgar (Queen, Canopus, Spencer, Tigre, Zealous, Prince of Wales, Donegal) are counted in the label on their days and not drawn: a fleet's ships of the line come in only where every one of them reads one by one, and off Cádiz the British fleet's do not before the Donegal has gone (`scripts/dev/fleet-gates.mjs` shows it).

**Drawn with other types, not counted wrong** (they show only in the close views): Nelson's Canopus and Tigre, 80-gun two-deckers, and his Royal Sovereign, a first rate, are drawn as 74s and a 98; Spanish 80s and 64s, Calder's Malta (80) and two 64s, and four of Ferrol's six French 80s are drawn as 74s.

**What changed from session 20's figures.** The British October label goes by date (29, 23, 24, 26, 27, 28, 27) instead of a constant 27; the combined fleet's in Cádiz is 32 until the Santa Ana joined, then 33; Villeneuve is 13 at A Coruña from 2 August and 26 from 10 August, where 15 was lettered until the 13th; the San Rafael joins at Martinique in the afternoon of 16 May; "Victory" is lettered from 11.30 a.m. on 14 September; Collingwood's label closes as Bickerton's four join.

**Doubts.**
- The day the Leviathan went to Gibraltar. Low.
- Whether the Africa joined in the night of 13–14 October or in the morning. Medium.
- The hour the Donegal left on the 17th. Low.
- The day the Santa Ana joined the fleet in the roads. Low.
- Whether Louis's four were in company on the 4th (Beatty) or the 5th (Austen); either way, for hours only.
- The six inshore on 28 September, of whom only the Canopus, Spencer and Tigre are certain.
- James's 27 on arrival, which leaves out two ships no source names as absent.
- The hour the Firme and the San Rafael struck.
- Whether the strict reading should letter the British 26 for the night of 20–21 October, while the Africa was out of touch to the north. The label keeps 27: she was under Nelson's flag and fought.
- Whether the America's two days astern after Cádiz (13, not 14) should be lettered. The owner chose 14 from Cádiz.

## Scene 2: Boulogne from the sea (beat 1)

What beat 1's painting rests on: the roads of Boulogne, the flotilla, the town and the camps on an August evening in 1805, seen from a boat in the roads looking east at the shore. The research is in `docs/research/scene-02/boulogne-sea/`, and its `README.md` lists the files and their status: four topic notes (`light-and-watch.md`, `flotilla.md`, `camps.md`, `town-and-defences.md`), an independent check of each (`*-check.md`) and the computation of the evening (`evening.md`). Below, files are named without the folder. Where a check corrects its note, this section follows the check. The liberties the painting takes are decisions, recorded in `decisions.md` ("Boulogne from the sea") and named in this section's list "Liberties recorded in decisions.md", before the open doubts.

Stage A painted one moment, the golden light of 18:33. Stage B (sessions 29 and 30) carried the painting's hold on through the sun's last light and the sunset into the dusk of about 19:45, and took in the flotilla check's last verdicts (its sections 2.6–2.13 and items 10–13, finished in session 28). What that added stands in the subsections it belongs to.

**The painting's eye**: 50.7300 N 1.5780 E, 1.1 km due west of the 1805 jetty heads (taken as 50.730 N 1.594 E, uncertain by 200–300 m), 6 m above the water, looking along 98° true. Left is north-north-east, right south-south-east, straight behind 278°. Times are local apparent time (LAT), which French clocks showed in 1805; on 10 August 1805 at Boulogne LAT = UT + 1.3 min. Tide heights are on Desbrière's zero (the tide scale of the ponts et chaussées).

The scene 2 history draft (`docs/research/scene-02/session-17/history-scene-02-wip.md` and `history-scene-02-merged.md`, "Boulogne and the invasion army") is superseded by this section where they differ: the Étaples huts were thatched (tiles and glazing were materials the dig expected to find, not finds), and the 748 huts of 1804 were Legrand's division's at Ambleteuse, not a Boulogne camp's (`camps-check.md`, (a)).

### The evening and its light

- **The date: 10 August 1805**, a Saturday (22 thermidor an XIII). The full moon fell at 11:57 UT that day, and at sunset the moon was 99.9 % lit. Of the owner's evenings (9–11 August), it is the one on which the moon's disc clears the hills over the town at sunset, seen from the eye. High (computed; `evening.md`, 1 and 3).
- **The sun.** Sunset (upper limb on the horizon) at 19:26, azimuth 296.2°, 18° left of straight behind. The golden light, the sun 12° to 5° up, lasts from 18:01 to 18:47. Civil dusk ends at 20:03, nautical dusk at 20:52. High (NASA/JPL Horizons; the check's own computation and the US Naval Observatory agree within a minute; `light-and-watch-check.md`, 2).
- **The hold opens in the light of 18:33**, 52 minutes before sunset: the sun 7.0° up at azimuth 286.2°, 8° left of straight behind. All that faces the sea is lit square-on and nearly without shadow as seen from the boat: the cliffs north of the harbour face about west, the shore towards Le Portel north-west. Shadows fall away from the viewer and a little to the right. The sea ahead mirrors the cool eastern sky; the warm sky and the sun's glitter are behind the camera. High for the geometry; Medium for the look (general optics; `light-and-watch.md`, 2). (Stage A's painting held this one light; since stage B it is the hold's first, and the evening goes on to the dusk below.)
- **The sun's last light.** Below about 5° up (from about 18:47, 37–40 minutes before sunset) the sun's light turns orange; below about 2° (from about 19:09) orange-red and weaker, and the lit faces glow against a sky darker than they are. High for the times (`evening.md`, 2; `light-and-watch-check.md`, 2); Medium for the colours (general optics; `light-and-watch.md`, 1.4).
- **Sunset on the land, from the water up.** The shore at the water's level loses the sun at sunset, 19:26. The cliff tops and the camps on them keep it about a minute and a half longer (93–103 seconds computed for 65 m; the cliff edge stands 45–55 m), and the higher ground behind longer still: the shadow climbs from the water to the crests. Within a few minutes of sunset the land ahead has lost its colour. High for the geometry; Medium for the look (`light-and-watch.md`, "For the painter" point 4 and 1.4; `light-and-watch-check.md`, 2 and item 9).
- **The hold ends in the dusk of about 19:45**, some twenty minutes after sunset. The sun then stands 3–4° under the horizon behind the eye (2.9° under at 19:41, 5.0° under at 19:56), at azimuth about 300°, 22° left of straight behind; civil dusk ends at 20:03. So the hold runs from the golden light of 18:33 through the sun's last light and the sunset on the land to the dusk of civil twilight (`decisions.md`, session 29, "The dusk through the hold"). High for the times (`evening.md`, 1 and 2).
- **The eastern sky after sunset.** Looking east, the eye faces the anti-twilight. After sunset a blue-grey band, the earth's shadow, rises from the horizon behind the town and the camps, with the pink band of the Belt of Venus above it, for roughly 20–30 minutes; at 19:45 both still show. They are centred on the anti-solar point, which moves right as the sun sets further north: 116.2° at sunset (18° right of the view's axis), about 120° by 19:45 (22° right). The sea ahead mirrors that sky band by band. High for the directions (`evening.md`, 2); Medium for the look and the duration (general optics; `light-and-watch.md`, 1.4 and "For the painter" point 5). How high the painting draws the bands is the painter's.
- **The moon.** It rose on the sea horizon at 19:09 (azimuth 108.8°), but from the eye the hills hid it. Its upper limb showed over them at 19:22. At 19:26, the minute of sunset, the whole disc was clear: azimuth 111.95°, centre 1.96° up, the lower limb just on the skyline. That skyline is the southern shoulder of Mont Lambert (ground 154 m, 5.1 km off), with the southern part of the lower town in front of it. So the moon came up 9.3° right of the belfry and 6.9° right of Mont Lambert's summit: over the right-hand part of the town, not over the walled upper town. It then climbed to the right at about 36° to the horizon, 1.9° right and 1.4° up every ten minutes. High for the positions; Medium for the minutes it shows over the hills (modern bare ground, about ±1 minute; `evening.md`, 3).
- **The moon's size and look.** 30.0′ across (0.50°): the full moon came near apogee, so it was about 10 % smaller than one at perigee. At 2° up refraction flattens it to about 0.92 of its width. High. At sunset, 2° up through summer haze, it is a dim, warm disc, apricot to pink, hardly brighter than the sky, and haze can hide it; it whitens and reads plainly from about 5° up (about 19:45). It stands in the pink band of the anti-twilight, and the blue-grey shadow of the earth rises under it within ten minutes. Medium (general optics).
- **The moon through the dusk.** It went on up and to the right along its slanting path: at 19:36 3.32° up at azimuth 113.85°, at 19:46 4.69° up at 115.76°, its centre then 3.15° above the hills. So between sunset and 19:46 it rose about 2.7° (about 50 of the painting's layer units) and moved about 3.8° to the right. By then the earth's shadow had risen a few degrees, so a moon 3–5° up stood in it or near its top (an inference), with the pink band above; the light note's check likewise finds the moon of 11 August, clearing the hills twenty minutes after sunset, rising inside the shadow band with the pink band above it. The disc pales from apricot as it climbs and reads plainly from about 5° up (about 19:45). High for the positions (`evening.md`, 3.1 and 7); Medium for the look (general optics; `evening.md`, 3.3; `light-and-watch-check.md`, 3).
- **How the painting shows the moon's rise.** It is placed where it stood at sunset (above), so through the golden light, when the real moon was still under the horizon or the hills (before 19:22), it is the liberty of the moment (`decisions.md`, session 26, "The evening: 10 August 1805"). Through the hold it climbs 10 layer units, straight up: about a fifth of its true rise from sunset to 19:45, and without its true slide to the right, so it stays over Mont Lambert's southern shoulder, between the near chaloupe's masts and whole at the hold's end. A liberty the owner approved on 14 November 2026 as closer to the truth than a moon standing still (`decisions.md`, session 30, "The owner's answers of 14 November 2026"; session 29, "The dusk through the hold" and "After the critics"). It brightens through the dusk but stays a pale warm gold, not white: 2–5° up through summer haze it is apricot (session 29, "After the critics").
- **Its path on the water.** Once the land is dark, the full moon lays a narrow column of glints on the rippled water under it, from the far water toward the eye. General optics; Medium as inference (no source describes that evening's sea). The painting draws it through the second half of the hold (`decisions.md`, session 29, "The dusk through the hold").
- **The neighbouring evenings**, from the same eye. On 11 August (98 % lit) the disc clears the hills at 19:44, 20 minutes after sunset, straight above the west corner of the walls, 1.4° right of the belfry. On 9 August it clears at 19:06, before sunset, over the Liane valley right of the town; on 12 August at 19:57, left of the upper town. High (computed). The light note's moonrises are for the sea horizon; behind the hills the moon shows later and further right (`light-and-watch-check.md`, item 5).
- **The weather.** Boulogne's weather on 10 August 1805 is not recorded in anything read. Napoleon arrived on 3 August in bad weather, with no craft in the roads, and wrote on 20 August that it had rained a great deal. In London, 150 km away, the Royal Society's journal has light airs on 10 August from the south-west, then the west, cloud in the morning and fair weather at 2 p.m., 16–23 °C, and the barometer at the month's highest. No rain fell there from the morning of 9 August to 14 August, and the afternoons of 11–13 August had light airs from south to west-north-west. So a warm, settled evening with a light westerly air, some cloud and haze (which such weather commonly brings in the Strait) is well founded. High for London; Medium for Boulogne, as inference; Low for any detail of the evening. The British logs kept off Boulogne (The National Archives, ADM 51/52) would settle it; they were not read (`evening.md`, 5; `light-and-watch-check.md`, 5).
- **The sea.** In summer, a low swell from the west or south-west and small waves, usually well under a metre; whitecaps only above force 4. Medium (modern climate statistics). A French report of 25 August 1804 describes a good day in the roads as fine, with "la mer peu houleuse" (Dumas XI 45). High as a state of the sea in which the line was kept.
- **England is out of sight.** From a boat (an eye 3–6 m up) the cliffs of Dover and Folkestone, 46–48 km away, lie below the sea horizon. At most a hair-line 0.03–0.06° tall could show on a rare clear day, and it would lie behind the viewer's left shoulder (Dover at 332–338°). High (geometry; `light-and-watch-check.md`, 8).
- **Lights at dusk.** At 19:45, in civil twilight, the lights a boat in the roads could see were candle, lamp and hearth light some 1.4–3 km off: small deep yellow-orange points, only a little brighter than the sky. Low (an inference from general optics, the critics' reading of session 29 in `docs/reviews/boulogne-sea/s29-dusk-lights.md`; no source read describes them). What the sources say of the camps' lights, the pavilion's lamps and the town's and harbour's lights is under each below; on the craft none is recorded (below, "Lying at anchor"). How many lights the painting shows, and in what order they come up, is the painter's (`decisions.md`, session 29, "Lights at dusk").

### The tide, the strand and the drying harbour

- **The tide of 10 August 1805.** High water at 11:15 (8.2 m), low water at 18:18 (1.42 m), high water at 23:32 (8.3 m). At 18:33, the hold's first light, the water stood at 1.45 m; at sunset at 2.13 m, rising 0.66 m in that half hour. The range, 6.8–6.9 m, made these small springs, because the full moon came near apogee; the mean spring range was 7.9 m and the neap 4.4 m. Medium (the TICON-3 harmonic constants of the Boulogne gauge, good to about ±20–30 minutes and ±0.3 m, plus a few decimetres from wind and pressure; `evening.md`, 4; `light-and-watch-check.md`, 6).
- **The light note's tide table is wrong for low water.** Its simple model puts low water near springs about two hours early (16:10 instead of 18:18 on 10 August): at Boulogne the tide rises in about 5½ hours and falls in about 7. Corrected, low water came within about an hour of sunset on 10–14 and 24–27 August, and high water near sunset on about 4–6 and 19–21 August. So the full moon of 10–11 August did come with low water near sunset. Medium (`light-and-watch-check.md`, 6).
- **The strand.** At the lowest tides of the year (+0.30–0.36 m) the low-water line lay 700–750 m beyond the jetty heads: 386 toises (752 m) on Cadier's plan of 1768, and the end of the 700 m fascine dike from the Pidou jetty. At those tides the strand was 700–800 m wide at the head of the bay and still 450 m at La Crèche. High for the geometry of 1768–1803; Medium for 1805 (Desbrière III 141–142, 148).
- **The water's edge on 10 August.** Low water stood 1.1 m above that line, so the edge lay nearer: about 500 m off the jetty heads at 18:33 and about 350 m at sunset, and the flood reached the jetty heads at about 20:18. Under the cliffs north of the harbour the strand was 610–700 m wide at 18:33, at La Crèche about 395 m. Medium-Low (one plan of 1768 and an even slope of about 1:220; `evening.md`, 4.2). On that slope the eye, some 600 m seaward of the edge, floated in about 2.7 m of water, enough for a chaloupe canonnière. Low. The flood is fast after low water: on 11 August the water rose 0.5 m in the first half hour after sunset and 1.3 m in the first hour, and the edge came in roughly 50–100 m and 130–260 m. Medium. The painting keeps one edge, the year's lowest, through its whole hold (below, "The tide through the dusk"; this bullet earlier spoke of a painting of one moment).
- **The tide through the dusk.** The water stood 2.13 m at sunset and 2.94 m at 19:56, so about 2.6–2.7 m at 19:45, 87 minutes after low water and some 1.2 m above it; on Cadier's slope its edge then lay about 220–250 m off the jetty heads, against about 500 m at 18:33. Medium-Low (interpolated from `evening.md`, 4.1 and 4.2). The painting keeps one strand through the whole hold, drawn on the line of the year's lowest tides, some 500 m beyond the water's edge of 19:45, though the light note's check warns against mixing the strand of one hour with the dusk of another (`light-and-watch-check.md`, item 2): a liberty (`decisions.md`, session 26, "The evening: 10 August 1805", the tide and the strand; session 30, "The dusk's liberties with the history", the tide and the strand).
- **The harbour lay dry all evening.** The bed of the Liane in the harbour stood at +4 to +5.7 m, and on 10 August the water was below +5 m from 14:32 to 20:53 and below +4 m from 15:17 to 20:26. The harbour was then banks of sand and mud, with the Liane running through it in a channel 20–40 m wide. The craft along the quays and round the basin sat upright on the mud; their flat floors, and the prames' three keels, were built for it. The semicircular basin was dug as a drying basin. A dam with a timber sluice (decided in April 1804, standing in August 1805) held water only above itself, and the decrees of 26 and 30 August 1805 kept the craft not needed for sorties afloat there. High for the drying; Medium for the hours and the dam (Desbrière III 141–149, 452, IV 373–374, 381; Corr. XI Nos. 9137, 9164; `flotilla-check.md`, item 3; `town-and-defences-check.md`, item 6 and 1.5).
- **The mouth at low water.** The bar at the west jetty head (+3.70 m) lay dry from 15:32 to 20:18. A bank 150 m in front of the west pierhead (+5.5 m) closed the west half of the mouth from 14:10 to 21:06, and the outflow ran along the east (Pidou) jetty. The jetty heads stood high on the beach: even at spring high water they had only about 3 m of water at their foot. Medium (Desbrière III 143–148; `evening.md`, 4.3).
- **Craft moved only round high water.** The port officer Sauvage had craft leave safely only from half an hour before high water to an hour after it (two hours for oared craft). That night the canonnières had 2 m over the bank from 22:13 to 00:57, the bateaux and péniches 1.3 m from 21:45 to 01:32. Sorties went out on the morning or the evening high water. So through the painting's hold, 18:33 to about 19:45, nothing could pass the entrance. High for the rule; Medium for the hours (Desbrière III 144–145; `flotilla.md`, 2.10).
- **The forts and the tide.** At low water the two stone forts stand on bare rock, and the wooden fort's piles stand on the sand of the low-water line: it uncovered at low tide (a note of 22 August 1803, Desbrière III 158). At high water the stone forts rise from the sea. High.
- **Not checked: the stream round low water**, and so which way the craft at anchor lay. The flood ran north-north-east along the coast at up to about 4 knots (Desbrière III 141). The flotilla check infers that round local low water the stream ran south-west, so the craft lay bows to the north-north-east, to the viewer's left; no tidal-stream atlas was read. Low.

### The flotilla in the roads and in the harbour

- **Rigged gun craft, not barges.** Four classes, from the design table of 1803 (Desbrière III 90 and the plate facing it; James III 216–217). High for the designs:
  - **Prame**: 110 × 25 French feet (about 36 × 8 m), flat-floored on three keels, ship-rigged, twelve long 24-pounders, 38 seamen and over 100 soldiers. The tallest thing on the water: her main truck about 35 m above the water by Steel's rule (taken from her extreme length, so perhaps a metre too high), and the Foudroyante model agrees; the range 28–36 m stands, its upper half the better supported (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.9 and item 11). Medium for the look, Low for heights. The painting's prame stands about 34 m (`decisions.md`, session 29, the flotilla check's points).
  - **Chaloupe canonnière** (James's "gun-vessel"): 76–80 × 17 feet (about 25 × 5.5 m), brig-rigged, some as brigantines; three long 24-pounders at bow and stern and a howitzer (James has an 8-inch mortar); the orders of 1803 also gave two to four 36-pounder carronades on the sides, and the painting's chaloupes carry them; sweeps. Loaded draught 5½ feet (new type) to 8 feet (old). Mastheads 20–25 m by Steel's rule: about 21 m to the topmast head, about 25 m with topgallants; only the "simplified" masting asked for in July 1803 (Desbrière III 88), which is not described, brings her under 20 m (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.9 and item 11, which correct the note's 16–24 m). High for hull and guns; Medium for the side guns; Low for heights. The painting's chaloupes stand about 20 m (see "Open doubts").
  - **Bateau canonnier** (gunboat): 60 × 14 feet (about 19.5 × 4.5 m), lugger-rigged or schooner-rigged, two or three masts (the number is not documented); a 24-pounder on a slide in the bow and a field gun on its wheels at the stern; a removable 18-inch washboard pierced for 28 oars, the side 0.8–1.2 m above the water with it; loaded draught 3½ feet. High for hull and guns; Medium for the rig. The flotilla note's 10–14 m for her lug masts is an interpolation and may be low: the lug-rigged caïque on the plate facing Desbrière III 90 stands about 0.63 of its length, and Hue's luggers about one hull length, though they are not identified as flotilla craft. Low (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.9). The painting's bateaux stand with their side 0.9–1.25 m above the water, lowered 0.35 m from the chart's drawing to meet the 0.8–1.2 m (item 9), and keep lug masts of 0.62–0.69 of their length, which meets the caïque's 0.63 (item 11; `decisions.md`, session 29, the flotilla check's points).
  - **Péniche**: an open rowing boat, 60 × 10 feet (about 19.5 × 3.25 m), up to 40 oars, two or three lug masts and a jib on a bowsprit, a howitzer or a small gun. High.
- **Other craft.** Caïques, ships' launches of about 14 m with one 24-pounder (Willaumez gives two 36s), were few in the lines of 1805: two in the line of June. The bomb craft (bombardes, bomb galliots, bateaux bombardes) were decked vessels drawing about 2.75 m loaded, kept a short distance behind the line; three lay in the June line. The corvettes canonnières Foudre and Audacieuse (ten guns; the British called them brigs) reached Boulogne between 23 and 26 July 1805. Saint-Haouen's plan of 3 August 1805 sent the transports into the roads only on the eve of a general sailing (Desbrière IV 399–400), and no line return the check opened lists one; the Batavian flotilla lay at Ambleteuse: on 17–20 July the line moved up to cover it, and the Batavian craft passed Boulogne's roads but did not lie there. High for the facts; Medium-High that no transports or Batavians lay in the roads on an ordinary day (Desbrière III 90, 102, 556–560, IV 395–404; Willaumez 123; James III 316, 324–325; `flotilla-check.md`, 2.6).
- **How many.** At Boulogne on 20 July 1805: 578 prames and gun craft (13 prames, 530 chaloupes and bateaux together, and some 33 bomb craft, packets, caïques and péniches; the parts do not quite make the total) and 526 transports, 1,104 in all (James III 325, 496, after Dumas). High. The decree of 30 August 1805 kept 6 prames, 72 chaloupes and 72 péniches armed in the port (Corr. XI No. 9164). High.
- **The moored line** (*ligne d'embossage*): gun craft moored in the open roads outside the pier, under the batteries and the forts on the sands, as an advance guard and a screen; divisions took turns in it. The port emptied and filled only round high water, so no more craft were kept out than could come back on one tide, about 100 in 1805. High. In October 1804 about 150 lay in a double line. The lines of 1805 were smaller: 54 on 10–21 July, 27 chaloupes and 27 bateaux, the model for a line in August. High. No prame is listed in any line of 1805; prames lay in the lines of 1804 and went out on 12 August 1805. Medium (`flotilla-check.md`, item 2).
- **August 1805.** When Napoleon arrived on 3 August there was not one craft in the roads, and none until at least 6 August. After that the flotilla's reports have gaps, and neither they nor the generals' reports mention a line (Desbrière IV 398). On 4 August Napoleon told the batteries to defend "les bâtiments qui sont mouillés là", so some craft lay at anchor under them (Corr. XI No. 9042). Low. On 12 August, about noon, 5 prames, 30 chaloupes and 40 péniches went out under Captain Moras and chased the British cruisers some three leagues (Dumas XII 76–77; Napoleon's letters of 13 August). Medium (French accounts). On 13 September Napoleon wrote of having to set up the moored line again (Corr. XI No. 9209). So a moored line on the evening of 10 August reconstructs July's. Low for any given evening.
- **Where the line lay.** In the roads beyond the low-water line, in water deep enough at every state of the tide. In 1801 a division lay about 500 toises (0.9–1 km) off the harbour entrance (Naval Chronicle XIV 400–401); in 1805, Dumas says, the lines spread and lay further out, beyond the batteries' reach (XII 39). No distance for 1805 was found. Medium for the figures. So from the eye, 1.1 km off the jetty heads, the line would lie beside the boat or seaward of it, not between it and the town. Inshore of the line the documents put only péniches (inshore of its two ends), bomb craft just behind it, and boats. Low–Medium (an inference; `flotilla-check.md`, item 1). The line ran along the coast on both sides of the harbour mouth (Lewis's view of October 1804; the roads ran from La Crèche to Alprech), its head at the north-east end. Low. The painting's line runs on both sides of the mouth (`flotilla-check.md`, item 6; `decisions.md`, session 29, "Already as the check has it").
- **The line rode afloat at every state of the tide.** Bruix's trace of 1803 lay in 22 pieds (about 7 m) of water at low water, the bomb craft drawing 2.75 m kept just behind it, and the wide strand kept the line far from the land (Desbrière III 141, 161, 560). On Cadier's slope, at 18:33 on 10 August a chaloupe needed to lie at least about 150 m outside the year's low-water line, and a prame about 370 m; to ride at every state of the tide a chaloupe needed about 400 m. Low for the slope (one plan of 1768, extended); Medium-Low that the line floated clear at low water (`flotilla-check.md`, item 10 and 2.10). The painting moors its rows 24 and 84 m outside that line, some 300–430 m ahead of the eye, where at 18:33 they had about 1.3–1.5 m of water: enough for a bateau canonnier (1.1 m loaded), not for a chaloupe (1.8 m) or a prame (2.8 m). A recorded licence, kept for the composition the owner asked for, the line broadside in front of the town: moored afloat, some 150 m further out, the rows would stand 120–200 m from the eye among the near craft, a palisade of masts across the shore, the town and the camps; and the flat-floored craft took the ground upright, so at their distance afloat and aground differ by about a pixel (`decisions.md`, session 26, "The moored line and its craft are a reconstruction"; session 29, "Kept as the recorded licence: the line's depth of water").
- **Spacing.** The admiral wanted equal distances and a dressed line (order of 16 April 1804). One row of 150 craft over 6–8 km gives a craft every 45–55 m; in a double or triple line each row had 80–110 m between craft, and Napoleon's remark of 1803 (two or three caïques in each interval between chaloupes) points to 60 m or more. Low. Saint-Haouen's plan of 3 August 1805 (prames and chaloupes in the seaward line, two prames side by side at each end) Desbrière thought unrealistic. Low as a picture of a real evening. A double line of 54 craft, 27 to a row, at 80–110 m runs 2.1–2.9 km, about as long as a single row at 45–55 m (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.10). The painting spaces each row's craft 80 m apart, the check's lower bound, the rows staggered by half a step (`decisions.md`, session 29, the flotilla check's points).
- **Lying at anchor.** Tide-rode, all the same way, bows into the stream, so seen broadside from seaward. Medium (inference; for low water see the stream above). They were moored with two anchors, but that kept the swinging circle small without stopping the swing: canonnières moored on two anchors in the Canche swing with every tide (Sganzin's report of 6 December 1802, Desbrière III 163), and so would those off Boulogne: the whole line turned together at each turn of the stream. Medium (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.12, which corrects the note's "swung little"). Sails furled on the yards, yards crossed and squared on brigs and prames: Lewis's view of the line shows it. Medium. Lug yards lowered at anchor is general practice, which no source or image shows. Low (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.11, which lowers the note's Medium). From sunset to sunrise boats rowed guard along the line, and péniche pickets lay a cable (about 195 m) ahead (order of 16 April 1804). High. The painting's dusk, which runs some twenty minutes past sunset, draws neither: a liberty (`decisions.md`, session 30, "The dusk's liberties with the history", the guard boats). No riding lights are recorded: Desbrière's documents have none, and Willaumez's lanterns are for night signals and battle, he calls stern lanterns a thing of the past, and he describes no light for a craft at anchor (Willaumez 1825, s.v. Fanal). So at dusk the line is dark but for a rare lantern. Medium for the absence, which rests on finding no evidence; Low for the rare lantern (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.11, which raises the note's Low). The painting lights none (`decisions.md`, session 29, "Lights at dusk").
- **Riding in a swell.** Most of the craft roll quicker than a swell of 6–10 seconds, so they follow it: they rise and fall with it, about half a metre in a half-metre swell, and tilt by about the wave's slope, 0.6–1.6°. Brigs and prames, with longer roll periods, may roll more as a swell nears their own, though their flat floors damp it; mastheads sweep 0.3–1 m. A westerly swell's crests run along a line moored parallel to the shore, so a row's craft rise together. Inference; Low (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.12 and item 12). The painting's one westerly swell of 8.8 seconds follows this (`decisions.md`, session 29, the flotilla check's points).
- **Flags.** Each craft wore the tricolour at the stern (three equal vertical bands, blue at the staff) and a long pennant at the masthead. The decree of 15 February 1794 that fixed the ensign made the pennant one fifth blue, one fifth white and three fifths red: on a chaloupe's pennant of about 11 m (twice her beam, Willaumez's minimum length, not a typical one), some 2 m blue, 2 m white and 7 m red, probably blue next to the mast. The flags were of plain cloth (*toile*) and hang heavily in a light air; with the sun up they fly. High for the proportions; Medium for the blue's end; Medium-Low for the sizes (`flotilla-check.md`, item 4 and 2.8). Willaumez's rule (1825) gives the large stern ensign the beam for its fly and two-thirds of it for its hoist, and smaller vessels smaller flags; the decree of 1794 left sizes to usage. Medium-Low.
- **The ensign at anchor flew from a staff at the stern.** Lewis's view of the moored line (October 1804), the only first-hand view of it, shows a tricolour on a staff at every stern and a long red or pale red pennant at most mastheads. Medium-High for what it shows (a naive drawing). Willaumez (1825) has the large stern ensign hoisted at the gaff in every vessel that sets a fore-and-aft "brigantine" sail; the line at anchor in Lewis wears it on the staff. Medium for where the luggers wore it (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.7, 2.8, 2.11 and 2.13). So the painting's chaloupes and its prame fly theirs from a staff at the taffrail, as its bateaux and péniches already did (`decisions.md`, session 29, "The ensign at anchor on a staff at the taffrail"; the owner's answer). The pennant at the masthead marked a warship (Willaumez); that it stayed up at anchor, as Lewis shows, and through the night is inference. Medium.
- **Ensigns at sunset.** No French rule of 1805 that struck the ensign at sunset was found: Willaumez's flag entries say nothing of sunset, and Desbrière's documents name sunset only for the guard boats. Medium-Low for the absence. At 18:33, 52 minutes before sunset, every ensign and pennant flies under any rule: High (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.11 and item 13). The painting keeps them flying through the dusk (`decisions.md`, session 29, "Already as the check has it"); see "Open doubts".
- **Numbers.** Every craft had a number (the first-kind craft, a chaloupe canonnière, No. 1 in March 1804, the péniche No. 267 in October 1804, the chaloupe No. 275 in May 1805, the gun-boats Nos. 62, 306 and others in the line of September 1805), but nothing read says where it was painted, or whether it was. High for the numbers; unknown for their display (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.8). The painting shows none.
- **Colours.** Hulls black or very dark tarred brown, matt and weathered, with no bright bands in evidence; sails off-white, weathered to buff and grey; masts and yards brown wood. No period document on the gun craft's paint was found in Desbrière III–IV, Corr. XI, James III, the Naval Chronicle XIV or Willaumez; the only paint on record is James's gun-brig and two yachts with white bottoms, green sides and gilding in August 1804, the exceptions. Two independent period images agree on dark hulls with no band: Lewis's from the roads (dark grey) and Hue's from the heights (dark brown to black; one craft there has dark brown sails). Medium for dark hulls without bands; Low for the shade; Low-Medium for the sails and spars (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.7, finished in session 28; this bullet earlier stood on the note alone).
- **In the harbour.** The plan of the flotilla's sailing order of thermidor XIII (July–August 1805) moors the craft side by side in tiers of five to ten, bows to the quay, by escadrille: péniches in rows round the semicircular basin, canonnières along both banks of the channel, prames and bomb craft by the basin's entrance, transports further up the river. But the plan goes with Saint-Haouen's report of 3 August 1805, a proposed order for a general sailing, which Desbrière printed to show it was not serious; it is not how the craft lay on an ordinary day, and the tiers and the bows to the quay were not checked. Medium-Low for the arrangement in port (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.10). From the roads they show over the jetties as a forest of bare masts and yards in front of the town (a British letter of July 1804; four period views). High. On the evening of 10 August all of them sat on the mud.
- **Not in the roads.** No barges or rafts; no Batavian schuyts (they lay at Ambleteuse until after 30 August); no transports (they stayed in port); no line of 2,000 craft from Étaples to Cap Gris-Nez (Napoleon's flourish to Villeneuve of 26 July). Medium-High.
- **What the period images can tell of the craft.** Lewis's view of October 1804 is the only first-hand view of the moored line: naive in perspective, the line drawn close in front of the beach with the distances squeezed, but reliable for its order and look (two-masted craft with yards crossed and sails furled, a few single-masted ones and at least one three-master, an ensign on a staff at every stern). Pocock's view (signed 1805, a scene of the summer of 1804) is reliable for the town and the mass of masts in the harbour but shows no moored line. Hue's painting (July 1804, painted in the studio for the Salon of 1806) shows the harbour's traffic, some craft under sail, most with bare masts. Baugean's engraving of the lines, published under the Empire after a medal of 1804, is right in its general types and rigs, but its endless rows are idealized and its craft are under way. The plates facing Desbrière III 90 serve for péniches and caïques; the plan of thermidor an XIII is a proposal; the British prints of 1811–1812 are later. Medium-High for Lewis's look; Medium for Hue's and Baugean's rigs; Low for any layout but Lewis's order (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.13).

### The camps

- **Huts, not tents.** The order of 28 August 1803 set two hut camps at Boulogne, one right and one left of the port, to be lived in through the winter. The troops bivouacked for some weeks that September (Nicolaÿ has tents), then built huts (*baraques*). The camps were lived in for two years, until the army marched on 27–31 August 1805 (the order of 26 August). Nothing puts a tent in the hut lines in the summer of 1805: tents appear only as plans for the last move before embarking (1804), for the ladies at the ceremony of 16 August 1804, and as campaign kit sent east in August 1805. High for no tents in the hut lines; Medium that none stood anywhere on the heights (Desbrière III 193–194; Corr. XI No. 9137; Bertrand I 334, 405; `camps-check.md`, (a) and (d)).
- **The hut.** Low, detached and gable-ended, its gable, with an inscription, to the street. Walls of poles, turf and shore stones daubed with clay, in the local manner; a thatched roof. Medium for the form. At Étaples the excavation of 2010 found every hut half sunk and thatched, for 12 or 16 men, with heating forbidden (Palaude and Lemaire 2016). High for Étaples. At Boulogne thatch is attested for the generals' huts and a kitchen (Constant); straw is not named (Ostend used rushes), and no source mentions chimneys. Half-sunk floors are proven only at Étaples; a ridge of 2.5–3.5 m suits them (inference). The often-quoted floor areas of 14–45 m² could not be traced. Low for the size. No text gives a colour: Lewis paints the huts mid-grey with a pale edge, Hue pale, near-white gabled huts with greyish roofs. Low.
- **The camp's plan.** Three long lines of huts parallel to the sea, cut by cross streets between battalions, named after victories; the officers' huts behind the lines, the kitchens a little further back. So the evening smoke rose from the landward edge of each camp. Medium (Bertrand I 335; the Étaples dig has three ranks by battalion and company). The gardens, the clay-and-shell obelisks, the street signboards and the painted gables would show from the roads as a faint mottling at most. Medium.
- **Where they stood.** The right camp on the plateau north of the harbour, from the Tour d'Ordre north-east towards Terlincthun and Wimille; the left camp on the Outreau plateau, from the brow facing the town back past the Petit-Moulin towards Le Portel and Outreau. In May 1804 each held one division of 6,700–7,400 men present: Saint-Hilaire's on the right, Vandamme's at Outreau. High for 1804. Where Soult's four divisions were hutted in the summer of 1805 was not found. The 748 and 518 huts of the report of 1804 were Legrand's at Ambleteuse and Suchet's at Wimereux, so no hut count is known for the Boulogne camps (Ambleteuse had about 10 men a hut). High (Desbrière III 428–434; `camps-check.md`, (a) and (b)).
- **The ground.** North of the harbour the cliff top stands at about 45–55 m at the Tour d'Ordre (an engineer of the time measured 55 m), and the ground rises inland to 70 m at 0.45 km and 92 m at the column's site, 1.8 km from the Tour d'Ordre. South of the harbour the Outreau brow climbs from the harbour flats to 48–59 m within 0.3 km, and the plateau behind it tilts up from 30–40 m above Le Portel to 60–64 m in the east. High (IGN terrain model, modern ground; `camps-check.md`, (b)).
- **What the roads see: a thin, dense band, not Lewis's tiers.** From 1–3.5 km out all of the right camp from the cliff top to the crest of its slope fits in about 0.3–0.4° of height. Rows 50–100 m apart sit only 0.01–0.03° apart, less than a hut's own height, so they overlap into a striped band of grey roofs just above the cliff. Lewis's drawing of 1804 is about ten times taller than the true view: he spread the rows out to count them. It shows how the rows run (along the contours, often in close pairs, with small blocks of huts behind), not their spacing. High for the geometry.
- **The nearer the boat, the more of the right camp hides behind its own cliff edge.** From the eye the plateau's edge is the skyline (up to 2.6°): at the Tour d'Ordre only the first 0.3–0.5 km of huts behind the cliff can show, and up to about 1 km on the rise towards La Crèche; from 700 m off, only 0.1–0.3 km. Of the left camp the eye sees a line of huts along the north-western brow of the Outreau plateau; from 700 m off, the brow's slope facing the harbour shows whole. Wimille and the eastern half of the Outreau plateau are hidden from everywhere in the roads. High for the geometry; Medium for 1805, since the cliff edge has retreated and the hut lines are not known (`camps-check.md`, items 1–2).
- **On the heights besides.** Lewis's view of 1804 shows six dark post mills with four sails (one on a crest left of the town, two pairs part way up the hills, one on the far skyline), two telegraphs (one over the town, one on a skyline hill right of it, his "Telegraph Hill", not identified) and tricolours on the batteries' flagstaffs. Medium; High for the flags (`camps-check.md`, (e)).
- **The camps at dusk.** The soldiers made their huts' doors and casements themselves (Bertrand I 334–335). Medium for windows; Low for glass in the men's huts. At Étaples heating was forbidden in the huts (Palaude and Lemaire 2016), though the soldiers stockpiled wood for lighting and heating (Salavert, Hello and Lemaire 2016, abstract), and every division drew its fuel (Desbrière III 431–434). So at dusk a hut would show candle light at a door or a casement, not a fire. Low (inference). The kitchens stood behind the officers' huts at the camp's landward back (above), where the evening fires burned; from the roads the crests may have hidden the fires themselves, so that their light showed only as a glow on the smoke rising from behind the skyline. Medium for the kitchens' place (`camps-check.md`, (a)); Low for what showed, an inference, since where the hut lines of 1805 ran is not known (above, "The nearer the boat"). The painting sets its kitchens under the skyline, so their fires show only as that glow (`decisions.md`, session 27, "The smoke" and "The shore"). Nothing read says how many lights burned in the camps, or when. The painting lights more of the camps' doors and casements than of the town's windows (in the camps 70 % of the men's doors and 45 % of their casements, a little fewer in the left camp; the officers' windows 60 %; a third of the kitchens' doors showing their hearth), clustered by companies with dark gaps between, and every kitchen's fire: the painter's (`decisions.md`, session 29, "Lights at dusk").

### The town and the defences

- **The period views show 1803–04.** Pocock's view (engraved for the Naval Chronicle; its letter is dated 31 July 1804), Lewis's from HMS *Tartarus* (October 1804), the engraving of the telegraph (an XII, 1803–04) and Hue's painting (a scene of July 1804). By August 1805 the wooden fort, the pierhead battery and the two stone forts had stood finished for a year. High (`town-and-defences-check.md`, item 9).
- **The belfry is the skyline's one tower.** A square stone tower, with four small corner turrets on its square stage, and an octagonal top stage; 35–37 m high on ground at 52 m, so its top stands near 89 m. From the eye it stands at 102.6°, its top 1.92° up. Medium for the form. From 0.7–2.5 km off, its top 4–8 m and the telegraph stand against the sky: the ground straight behind is Mont Lambert's lower north-west shoulder (140–166 m, 5–6 km off), and the summit (188–190 m) lies 2–6° to the right. The lower two-thirds of the tower stand against the hill. Medium (bare-ground terrain model; trees on the crest would lift the skyline a little; `town-and-defences-check.md`, item 1, which refutes the note's "against the hillside").
- **What crowned it in August 1805 is not settled.** The town's leaflet has a spire on the octagon "until 1805". The engraving of 1803–04 and Lewis's view draw a slender pointed top with the telegraph on its point; Pocock's view a blunt top with the arms on it; Bertrand's small turret is the state of 1828. Low: two views of three favour the spire. The painting follows the nearest view in time, Lewis's of October 1804: a slender spire with the telegraph on its point (the owner's rule of 14 November 2026: the nearest image of about 1790–1810 decides; `decisions.md`, session 27). The doubt stands: Pocock's blunt top may be the truer state, and the spire may have gone before August 1805 (the leaflet's "until 1805").
- **The Chappe telegraph** on the belfry, the end of the branch from Lille ordered on 28 August 1803: a mast with a long crossbar and a short dark arm at each end, worked by ropes from below. Its place on the belfry in 1803–04 is High (the engraving, both British views, Bertrand); still there in August 1805, Medium. Lightning later drove it to the rampart's south-west angle, at a date not given.
- **The upper town** is a walled rectangle of about 400–430 × 325 m, its walls 13–16 m above the ground outside, of dark grey-brown stone, with trees on the ramparts (not documented for 1805). It stands corner-on to the sea, its corners near north, east, south and west. From the eye the west corner, nearest the sea, is 1.6° right of the belfry; the north-west face runs off to the left over about 8°, the south-west face to the right over about 2.4°. The château, an octagon of round towers at the east corner, shows behind the middle of the north-west face, 2–3° left of the belfry, not at the right-hand end. The wall top stands about 0.8° above the horizon from 2 km off and 1.1° from 1 km; everywhere below the skyline. Medium (Bertrand 1829, pp. 95–102; terrain model; `town-and-defences-check.md`, item 3; `evening.md`, 3.2).
- **Churches.** The old cathedral was sold on 21 July 1798 and pulled down stone by stone; how much stood in August 1805 is not recorded, and the views of 1803–04 show no church beside the belfry. Medium for "no tall ruin"; Low for its exact state. In the lower town, Saint-Nicolas (its steeple of after 1688) and two small spires in the views of 1804. Medium.
- **The lower town** climbs from the quays up the valley and the slopes; La Beurrière, the fishermen's quarter, climbs in steps under the northern cliff. Its houses had three or four levels (Bertrand's "à deux et trois étages" counts the floors above the ground floor). It was mostly dark stone in 1805, with some new pale fronts: plaster and limewash came in after about 1800 and spread with the peace. Low–Medium. Roofs red-brown tile with some grey slate. Low (Hue's painting; regional usage). A three-storey barracks about 76 m long, the flotilla's store, stood at the head of the harbour. Medium.
- **The harbour works.** Two long timber jetties on piles ran out across the beach, the west one the longer (Luffman's plan of 1801 marks the harbour "Dry at Low Water"). On the west pierhead stood a battery of two tiers, later called Fort Napoléon: ten 36-pounders and two long-range mortars, with a grate for heating shot, finished at the end of August 1804. The old lantern there was razed and a taller wooden harbour light set back from it (Lewis draws a tall, white, pointed "Light House"). Two 36-pounders stood at the end of the east (Pidou) jetty. High for the jetties; Medium for the details (Desbrière III 154–155, IV 381).
- **Fort de l'Heurt**, the southern twin, on a reef off Le Portel, outside a frame looking east. A D-shaped masonry fort of about 48 × 32 m, its round front to the sea, of large dressed blocks; its gun platform 41 French feet (13.3 m) above the rock. In March 1805: twelve 36-pounders and four long-range howitzers in the open, no mortars listed; the parapet raised only 3 feet, its coping unfinished; casemates in the rear as quarters only; plank ramps on piers ran to the shore. Medium-High for size and guns; Low for the stone's colour (grey-buff). (Desbrière III 153–161, IV 381–384; `town-and-defences-check.md`, item 4.)
- **Fort de la Crèche**, the northern twin, of the same design, outside the frame to the left. It stood on a lower, flatter rock close under the cliff, in reach of the cliff batteries; its platform was likewise 41 feet above its own rock, so about a metre lower than l'Heurt's, and more often awash. In March 1805: six 36-pounders, six 24-pounders and four howitzers. Its rock is not located, and nothing of it survives. Medium (`town-and-defences-check.md`, item 5).
- **The wooden fort** (fort en bois, fort Rouge, fort de l'Expédition): a timber gun platform on piles on the low-water line at the end of the 700 m fascine dike, north of the channel, seaward of the harbour mouth. British ships fired on its pile-drivers by mid-August 1803, so the piling began in early August 1803 (Bertrand's dates are a month late); it was finished at the end of August 1804 and carried eight 24-pounders and two howitzers. High for place and guns. Its look is Low: a British view has a low reddish block bristling with guns, the name "Rouge" hints at red paint, and Nicolaÿ's figure of a weatherboarded house on piles is more likely its guard-house.
- **Batteries** lined the cliff tops from La Crèche to Cap d'Alprech, with more at mid-slope, at the cliff foot and on the beach: low earthworks with parapets of earth-filled gabions, embrasures, dark guns and mortars on timber platforms, plank magazines and guard-houses, and tricolours. From the sea they read as low red-brown blocks along the cliff edge (Lewis). The heaviest was at the Tour d'Ordre: twelve 24-pounders, six mortars and six howitzers (Constant), with a lower battery below it. Medium. No source names a "Batterie de l'Impératrice".
- **The cliffs.** North of the harbour, from the Tour d'Ordre to La Crèche, 45–55 m high, broken, brown and grey, with fallen rock and the Roman tower's debris on the beach (it fell in 1644) and stumps of sixteenth-century red-brick works on the top. South of the harbour, dunes, the Châtillon hill (about 40 m), the Outreau brow, and low cliffs of 20–35 m at Le Portel. High for the heights; Medium for the colours (period views; the local stone in grey, brown, yellow and reddish beds, Bertrand 1829, p. 188).
- **The town and the harbour at dusk.** Nothing read says how the town was lit in August 1805, or how many windows showed a light at civil dusk. Candle light in a few windows (the painting lights one in fifteen to twenty), more on the quays and in the lower streets than up the slope, is the painter's reading. Low (`decisions.md`, session 29, "Lights at dusk"). The harbour had a light: the taller wooden lantern set back on the west pierhead, ordered in July 1803 and standing by October 1804, Lewis's "Light House" (Desbrière III 154–155). Whether it was lit in wartime in 1805 was not found; the painting leaves it dark. Low (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.11; `town-and-defences-check.md`, 1.5).

### Napoleon's pavilion

- **Where.** On a small mound inside the ruined English bastions of 1544 round the old Roman lighthouse at the Tour d'Ordre, on the cliff edge just north of the harbour channel. The ground there is about 47 m, the cliff top just inland 57–61 m. On the plan of 1804 the spot lies about 0.4 km north of the root of the east (Pidou) jetty and 0.57 km north-east of the west jetty's head; the 0.9 km in the camps note is the distance to the wooden fort. From the eye it lies about 1.4 km off, at a bearing of about 82–85°, a little left of the harbour mouth, and in full view. Medium (Bertrand I 344–345 and his plan's legend; `camps-check.md`, (c); `town-and-defences-check.md`, 2.8).
- **The building.** A long, single-storey pavilion of carefully planed planks painted grey-white, about 100 × 22½ French feet (32.5 × 7.3 m), with windows along the sides. A semicircular end pavilion closed each end; the seaward one, a rotunda 30 feet (9.7 m) across and wider than the body, held the council room. A wooden paling ran round it, with lamps outside it after dark (their "four feet apart" is doubtful as a spacing; a ring of lights at dusk is safe). It was put up in 48 hours on a brick footing. High for the wood and the grey-white paint; Medium for the plan and the size (Bertrand I 345; Constant I 245; Nicolaÿ 17–18).
- **Which way it faced.** Its long axis ran about east–west, the rotunda at the west end facing the roads: the bedroom off the corridor to the council room had a window onto the right camp with the sea on the left, and the gardens lay on the south side. Medium (an inference from three period statements and the plan; the note had Low). So from the roads it is seen almost end-on: the round end spans about 0.16° from 3.5 km out and 0.59° from 700 m off, and its height 0.07–0.24°. A small pale shape on the cliff top, lit full by the evening sun until sunset (`camps-check.md`, item 4).
- **In use that week.** It stood from November 1803, and Napoleon dated a letter "La Tour d'Ordre" on 12 August 1805 (Corr. XI No. 9068). High.
- **At dusk.** The lamps round the paling were reflector lamps (*réverbères*), lit after dark (Constant I 245; Nicolaÿ 18). Their "four feet apart" would mean some sixty lamps round a paling of 80 m or more, so the spacing is doubtful; a ring of lights at dusk is safe. Medium (`camps-check.md`, (c) and item 8). From the roads, some 50 m below the brow, only the ring's seaward half would show, along the building's foot. Low (inference). The windows ran along the sides in a single row (Nicolaÿ 28); seen almost end-on from the roads they close up into slits. Whether Napoleon worked there on the evening of 10 August is not recorded. The painting lights its lamps, about one every 7 m along the seaward half, with the right camp's first lights, after the kitchens' fires, and 70 % of its windows, more than a hut's, though his evening there is not recorded: the painter's (`decisions.md`, session 29, "Lights at dusk").
- **Its neighbours.** The admiral's hut was a smaller copy of the Emperor's; Bruix had died on 18 March 1805, and Lacrosse commanded the flotilla. The signal mast stood beside it: a tall flagstaff with yards for flag hoists, for France's coastal semaphores with jointed arms came only in 1806. High for a signal mast; Low for arms. Its flag, with a gun, recalled the péniches, boats and fishing boats out for exercise, not the whole flotilla (Bruix's order, Desbrière III 539). Soult's and Decrès's huts were thatched to the ground and glazed at the top, their rooms half underground and the main room round; from afar Decrès's looked like an enormous candle snuffer. They stood inland of the only road, which ran between them and the Emperor's hut, with the great Tour d'Ordre battery a little further on. High for the thatch to the ground; Medium for the cone and the layout (Constant I 248–249; Nicolaÿ 21–22; `camps-check.md`, item 5). Flagstaffs on the Fort de la Crèche and on Mont du Couple repeated the admiral's signals (the order of the day of 17 April 1804). Medium.

### The Royal Navy's watch

- **Who watched.** In the summer of 1805 Rear-Admiral Billy Douglas, his flag in the 50-gun Leopard, was "employed watching the Flotilla at Boulogne" (Naval Chronicle XIV 68, July 1805). Captain Edward Owen in the frigate Immortalité (36) led the inshore squadron: one or two frigates, sloops, gun-brigs and a cutter. High (James III 320–323; Owen's report in Marshall). The Leopard lay at Dungeness on 29 July 1805 (Naval Chronicle XIV 172), so the flagship was at times across the Channel and out of sight; the inshore squadron was what Boulogne saw. Medium.
- **How close they kept.** In August 1804 the main body anchored about 10 miles north-west of the port, and Owen's flying division cruised just out of shell range, about 3 miles off (James III 228–229; a British letter of 31 July 1804). In April 1805 a frigate, two sloops, a bomb and eight gun-brigs lay at anchor before Boulogne. On 17–18 July 1805 they fought within half a mile north-west of Wimereux and anchored again about 5 miles north-west of Boulogne. On 12 August 1805 the French sortie found a ship of the line, two frigates and some thirty corvettes and brigs, and by evening more than sixty British warships were in sight (Dumas XII 76–77, a French account). High for 1804 and July 1805; Medium-High for their presence in August 1805: besides the sortie of the 12th, Napoleon's letters of 4, 13 and 20 August deal with the British cruisers off the coast, and about 3–4 August a British frigate came in and carried off a boat with a flag placed at half range of the batteries (Corr. XI Nos. 9042, 9069, 9074, 9097; `light-and-watch.md`, 5.3 and 5.4; this bullet earlier gave Medium); Low for any position on a given evening.
- **Out of the frame.** From near the jetty heads the British stations, 3–10 miles off, bear about 317–332°, behind the viewer's left shoulder (317–319° from 700 m off the jetty heads, 321–332° from the light note's own point further out). A ship standing in a mile off Wimereux or La Crèche, as the inshore squadron did when it fought off Wimereux on 18 July 1805, bears about 000–030°, some 70–100° left of the view's axis (98°; the check's 60–90°, which this bullet earlier gave, is reckoned from due east). The painting's frame reaches 33° either side of the axis on the laptop's 16:9 screen and 41° on the widest (21:9), so even she lies well outside it. So no British sail belongs in a frame looking east from the eye. Medium (`light-and-watch-check.md`, item 7 and section 7; `light-and-watch.md`, 5.4). The decision not to draw one, with its argument from the composition (the frame shows no open sea: the coast runs across it from edge to edge), is recorded in `decisions.md` (session 29, "No British sail in the view").
- **What a sail would have been.** Close in, most likely a two-masted gun-brig or brig-sloop (hull about 25–30 m), the commonest close watchers; a frigate further out; on a quiet evening at anchor 3–5 miles out with sails furled, as the flying division anchored as the tide required. Medium for the types and the habit (James III 227–230, 318–323; Naval Chronicle XIV 68; `light-and-watch.md`, 5.4).

### What must not be painted

- **No tents** in the hut lines (above). High.
- **No column.** The first stone of the Colonne de la Grande Armée was laid on 9 November 1804, and nothing gives its height in August 1805; it was only 19–20 m high when the work stopped (1811–1814). Its site, 1.8 km north-north-east of the belfry at 92 m, lies 6–22 m below the sightline from the roads near the eye, so foundations, sheds and low scaffolding would not show. High (`camps-check.md`, (e); `town-and-defences-check.md`, 1.6).
- **No earth forts on the hills.** Napoleon first asked for the plan of the redoubts round Boulogne on 29 August 1805 (Corr. XI No. 9159). The entrenched camp's sixteen earthworks and the second line of six forts (Mont de Couple, Petit-Moulin, Ferme du Renard, Mont Lambert and others) were built after the army left; the Fort du Moulin-à-l'Huile is of 1806. High.
- **No Legion of Honour markers** (from 1809) and no statue on the pavilion's site (1856). The ground of the ceremony of 16 August 1804, a hollow near Terlincthun, is at the edge of sight from the roads and no landmark. Medium.
- **No later town.** No tower at the Tour d'Ordre (the Roman lighthouse fell in 1644); no stone lighthouse at the harbour, no long breakwaters, no casino or baths; no town on the west bank (Capécure dates from after about 1809); no basilica or dome (begun in 1827); none of the forts of 1879; no tall church ruin beside the belfry. High; Medium for the ruin.
- **No mortars on the two stone forts** (none in the state of March 1805), no arm semaphore on the signal mast (1806), and no "Batterie de l'Impératrice" (no source names it). Medium.
- **No British ship in the frame and no English coast** on the horizon (above). Medium and High.
- **In the roads:** no barges, rafts, Batavian schuyts or transports, and no riding lights (none recorded). Medium-High; Medium for the lights, an absence of evidence (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.11; this bullet earlier gave Low).
- **On the craft:** no hull numbers (every craft had one, but where, or whether, it was painted is not known). Low.
- **On the huts:** no chimneys (no source mentions any). Low.

### Open doubts

- What crowned the belfry in August 1805: a slender spire or a blunt octagon with a small turret, the telegraph on it either way. Low.
- Whether a moored line lay in the roads on 9–11 August 1805, and where: none is documented; the line of 10–21 July is the model; in 1801 a division lay 0.9–1 km off the entrance, and in 1805 further out, by no stated distance. Low.
- Which way the stream ran round low water, and so which way the craft lay. Low.
- Boulogne's own weather on 10 August; the British logs off Boulogne (ADM 51/52) were not read.
- The huts' colour (mid-grey to near-white), size (a ridge of 2.5–3.5 m) and chimneys; where the four divisions were hutted in the summer of 1805. Low.
- The 1805 jetty heads' position (±200–300 m) and the strand's slope (one plan of 1768), so the water's edge of a given hour. Medium-Low.
- How much of the old cathedral stood in August 1805. Low.
- **The chaloupe canonnière's masts.** By Steel's rule (British practice, 1794; his rule for ships, the mainmast half the sum of the lower-deck length and the beam, with his proportions for the topmast and topgallant and his masthead lengths, here applied to a brig), a chaloupe of 76–80 × 17 feet rigged as a brig stands about 21 m to the topmast head and 24–25 m with topgallants; Steel's own merchant brig of 150 tons, whose topmast is half her lower mast, gives about 23 m (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.9 and item 11; `docs/research/scene-02/session-17/small-craft.md`). The painting's near chaloupe stands about 20 m (19.4–20.4 m above the water), as the owner approved it. Only the "simplified" masting asked for in July 1803, meant to avoid the complication of a square-rigger's masting (Desbrière III 88), whose form is not described, supports less than 20 m. But Steel's rule is British practice and not necessarily right for the flotilla's French craft: no French masting rule, sail plan or draught of the type was found. The owner kept the approved masts on 14 November 2026 (`decisions.md`, session 30, "The owner's answers of 14 November 2026"). A French draught, sail plan or contemporary model of a chaloupe canonnière of 1803–1805 with her mast heights, or the masting table of her building orders, would settle it. The nearest lead is Willaumez's plate B, fig. 10 (1825), a first-kind canonnière rigged as a brigantine (Willaumez 123; his Brigantin, p. 107, carries topgallants at most and no royals), which no note has measured (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.3). Low.
- Whether the flotilla's craft at anchor struck their ensigns at sunset. No rule was found either way; if they did, the dusk at the hold's end, 20 minutes after sunset, shows them flying too long. Medium-Low for the absence of a rule.
- How the camps, the pavilion and the town were lit at dusk, and whether the harbour's wooden lantern was lit in wartime. No source read says. Low.
- Estimates with no source behind them: the craft's freeboards, the lug masts' heights, lug yards lowered at anchor, the hulls' shade (black or dark tarred brown) and where the hull numbers were painted (`flotilla-check.md`, 2.7–2.11). Low. The flotilla check's sections 2.6–2.13, listed here earlier as not finished, were finished in session 28; their verdicts are in "The flotilla in the roads and in the harbour" above.
