### Signals in 1805, by day and by night

- **Flags.** The Admiralty's day *Signal Book* of 1799 kept Howe's numerary system: each signal had a number, made by one hoist of numeral flags, hundreds at the top. The ten numerals and a substitute were bold designs in red, white, blue and yellow. Their values were reshuffled in 1803–04 after a copy was captured, and that set served at Trafalgar, with more than 80 signals added by hand (Perrin 1922; FOTW, from a 1799 copy). High for the system; the 1804 values were not transcribed.
- **Popham's telegraph.** A diagonal red and white flag announced a message. Words and phrases had numbers in Popham's *Marine Vocabulary* (1803); other words were spelt in numerals. Fifty copies were ordered aboard the Victory on 9 September 1805, and the frigates had it too (Corbett 1910, pp. 298–301). High; which half of the flag was red is disputed and invisible at chart scale.
- **Repeating.** A ship kept a signal flying until the next ship had repeated it and it had reached the commander-in-chief. Ships in between repeated a private ship's signal until the Admiral answered (Popham 1803, p. 6; *Signal-Book* 1796, Directions). This rule is what made a chain work. High.
- **Guns.** Guns drew the eye to a hoist and were part of some signals: two for a general chase. Nelson's orders of 10 October told the ship bringing news of a sortie to fire a gun every three minutes, and at night rockets from the masthead (Nicolas VII 109–111). On 20 October the Euryalus's day signals went with one, two, three or seven guns (NRS II 165, 167). High.
- **Distant signals.** When colours could not be told apart, a code of shapes was used: "a flag, a ball, and a pendant" at different mastheads (Cumby 1899, p. 719). Nelson's order of 4 October to Duff called for distant signals whenever flags could not be told by their colours (Nicolas VII 70–71). Medium: one eyewitness, writing years later.
- **Reading range.** No period figure survives. Estimate: colours to about 4–11 nm depending on haze, a masthead to about 25 nm. On 19 October the Mars's flags could not be read from the fleet when only her topgallant masts showed, roughly 15–20 nm off (`research/scene-02/h3-signals.md`, section 1.5). Low.
- **Night numerals.** The 1799 *Night Signals* built a number from guns (the tens), lights in set arrangements (one to four) and false fires or rockets (five), in that order. Guns went five seconds apart, lights at least 12 ft apart; squadron commanders repeated four minutes apart; a signal was answered with one light. Near an enemy, figures of lanterns replaced the guns, but only for the fleet's manoeuvres; reports of the enemy kept their guns (`research/scene-02/h3-signals.md`, section 2; `research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R15). High.
- **Watching an enemy at night.** The same book told shadowing ships to keep sight of the enemy and of the Admiral, carry lights hidden from the enemy, and uncover signal lanterns all at once and hold them while the enemy's situation lasted (pp. 39–41). High.
- **What the lights looked like.** Lanterns were plain candle lights; no coloured glass is recorded, and red and green sidelights came only in 1848 (steam) and 1858 (sail). Blue lights and false fires were slow-burning compositions; Smyth (1867) gives a false fire, burned in a wooden tube, a light-blue flame lasting half a minute to several minutes, and period recipes, chiefly sulphur with saltpetre, give a bluish-white glare (*Encyclopaedia Britannica* 1778, "Pyrotechny"). Rockets threw gold sparks: strong firework colours came after 1811. The French also saw *amorces*, brief flashes of priming powder (Desbrière 1907, annexes 154). Gun flashes were orange-white, the report following; Gravina's flagship judged British guns two miles off by the delay (Fraser 1906, p. 94). Medium.
- **Nelson's night code.** His memorandum of 20 October: if the enemy stood south or towards the Straits, "burn two blue lights together, every hour"; if west, "three guns, quick, every hour" (Nicolas VII 136). High.

### The signal chain, 19 to 21 October

- **The chain as set.** On 4 October Nelson put the Mars, Defence and Colossus 3–4 leagues between the fleet and Cádiz, one of them "as near as possible in the latitude" of the port (Nicolas VII 70–73). From 14–15 October the Defence and Agamemnon lay 7–10 leagues west of Cádiz and the Mars and Colossus 5 leagues east of the fleet, which kept 15–20 leagues west (Hudson 1917, p. 21; Nicolas VII 122). Inshore were the frigates Euryalus (Blackwood), Sirius, Phoebe and Naiad, the brig Weazle and the schooner Pickle. Frigates at the Cádiz end, ships of the line beyond. High.
- **The relay of 19 October.** The inshore links are logged to the minute in the Euryalus's log and signal log; the middle links are not logged; the last rests on an eyewitness. High for the logged links, medium for the rest.

- **How long.** About 2½ hours by the logged clocks: 7.00 by the Euryalus, 9.30 by the Victory. Blackwood wrote "within two hours"; Browne's 10 a.m. makes three. Corbett found the Euryalus's clock about 45 minutes ahead of the Victory's on the 20th (339 n. 2), which would make about 3¼. The scene uses "by half past nine", never "in minutes" (`research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R10). Medium.
- **Direction and length.** Nelson's original diary has Cádiz ESE by compass, 16 leagues, at the chase (Hudson 1917, p. 23; also Beatty 1807, app. 97, and his noon letter, Nicolas VII 132). Browne has it east, 16 leagues (NRS II 194). The Victory's noon position, 36°36′ N 7°30′ W, puts Cádiz 19 leagues off, about due east true (Corbett 333 n. 1). With about 20° W variation, the chain ran about due west (true) from Cádiz and was 47–57 nm long. The "E.N.E." printed in Nicolas VII 133 is a copying slip, and the "W.S.W." of James (III 389) and Nicolas's narrative (VII 131) follows from it. High for the direction, medium for the length.
- **Weather and light.** Clear, with light airs that died to calm off Cádiz by noon (Euryalus log, NRS II 145–146). At the fleet the air was very light and variable: easterly in the morning, south to SSW at the chase (Hudson p. 22; Beatty p. 7). Sunrise about 6.26. The sun stood about 6° up, bearing 107° true, at 7.00, and 32° up, bearing 135°, at 9.30 (computed, local apparent time, 36.5° N). High.
- **Night of 19–20 October.** Rockets and blue lights were seen along the chain while the fleet ran for the Strait (Euryalus, Naiad and Sirius, NRS II 146–147, 302, 313). Six fast ships went ahead carrying lights (diary, Hudson p. 23). High.
- **20 October by day.** In rain and thick weather the Sirius fired minute guns, and the Euryalus made the enemy signal to the Agamemnon with seven guns. At 5 p.m. the Victory signalled that Nelson relied on Blackwood to keep sight of the enemy, and the Naiad reported 31 sail NNE: 5 p.m. in the original diary (Hudson p. 24), 6 p.m. in Nicolas VII 137, which follows a facsimile. The scene uses 5 p.m. High for the events, medium for the hours.
- **The night chain, 20–21 October.** By the memorandum (Nicolas VII 136): Blackwood with two frigates in sight of the enemy (Euryalus, Sirius), two more between him and the Defence (Phoebe, Naiad), then the Colossus, the Mars and the Victory. The Euryalus kept 2–3 nm on the enemy's weather beam. At midnight she had the body of the enemy SE by S, about 3 miles (NRS II 149; Nicolas VII 134 n. prints SE by E), and the British fleet's light S and W, 5 or 6 miles. The Sirius had the enemy east, 4 miles (NRS II 313). So the chain was about 8–10 nm long, not 50. High for the stations, low for the distances.
- **What the logs record.** From about 6 p.m. the frigates burned blue lights and false fires and fired rockets and guns all night (Euryalus, Sirius, Phoebe, Naiad), and the fleet answered (Phoebe). The Pickle repeated the Sirius's signals; the Mars saw a blue light NE at 2 a.m. and another ENE at 3.20; the Leviathan and Orion saw guns, blue lights and rockets to the north and north-east (NRS II 245, 267, 277, 306, 313, 317). Some reports went by hail: the Phoebe spoke the Mars at 0.20 by her clock (1.25 by the Mars's), the Naiad the Colossus at 1.30 (2.00). The French saw the signals too: guns about 9 p.m. from Gravina's flagship, and signals multiplying about midnight (Fraser 1906, p. 94; Prigny, Desbrière 1907, annexes 139). High.
- **Which half of the code.** The Combined Fleet stood S to SSW on the starboard tack for most of the night (Bucentaure and Cornélie, Desbrière 1907, annexes 159, 178; Lucas, Fraser 1906, pp. 91–92; Corbett 338), which is the case for paired blue lights. James (III 388) has it standing north-west after 7.30 p.m., against the French reports. No log says whether the frigates also used the book's tack signals (Nos. 28–29). Medium.
- **The fleet's own signals.** At 8 p.m. (8.40 in James III 390) the fleet wore to the SW by Night Signal No. 31, and the Victory burned blue lights to show her position (Corbett 340 n. 2). At 4 a.m. it wore to the NE (diary; Mars, Dreadnought and Conqueror logs), and the detached Africa saw gun flashes WSW. Beatty's narrative has 2 a.m., against his own appendix and the logs. By the book each ship showed two lights at the ensign staff and one at the bowsprit end while wearing; no log records it. The six guns the Sirius heard from the fleet at 9.35 by her clock may be No. 31 from two flagships (inference). High for the events, medium for the hours, low for the wear lights and the six guns.
- **Dark and light.** No moon, cloud and squalls. The British watching ships hid their lights (an officer of the Defence, Fraser 1906, p. 95), so the British side showed only the Admiral's lights, answers and blue lights. The Combined Fleet showed stern and top lights along a line some six miles long, with rockets, flares and guns of its own (Prigny, Desbrière 1907, annexes 139). High.

### How the chart shows the signals

**By day: the relay of 19 October (beat 4).**

- **Geometry.** A loose line about due west (true) from Cádiz, 47–57 nm long: the Sirius and Euryalus 4 nm SW of San Sebastián, the Phoebe and later the Naiad within 15–20 nm, the Defence about 27 nm out and perhaps 10 nm south of the parallel, then the Colossus, the Mars about 15 nm short of the fleet, and the Victory near 36°34′ N 7°24′ W (table below). Never south-west. Medium for the line, low for single ships.
- **One link lighting up.** A cluster of two to four flag dabs appears at the masthead, red, white, blue and yellow, hand-tinted like the engraving. White puffs of gunsmoke bloom at the ship's side in a counted pair or three (on 23 October the Phoebe repeated No. 370 with two guns, NRS II 308). After a pause the next ship does the same. Flags stay up until the next ship repeats, so flagged ships accumulate westward; when the Victory answers, they come down (the rules; not logged on the day). Medium.
- **Moments.** The Sirius's run of seven telegraph hoists under the red and white flag at first light. The Phoebe sailing west, a puff every three minutes of story time. At the Mars, coloured flags the fleet cannot read, then three dark shapes at three mastheads: a flag, a ball, a pendant. At the Victory, one flag and the SE compass flag with two puffs, and the fleet's glyphs turning SE together. Medium.
- **Light and air.** Low, warm morning light from the ESE, behind Cádiz, the sun climbing to 32° by 9.30. Light airs: flags hang slack, and smoke lingers by each ship and spreads slowly (inference from the weather). No muzzle flash by day. High for the light, medium for the rest.
- **Pacing.** 2½ hours of story time: the inshore steps within 20 minutes, then the long run west. Discrete pulses with gaps. Date line: 19 October 1805; if times are shown, 7 a.m. at the Cádiz end and 9.30 at the Victory. Medium.

**By night: 20–21 October (beat 5).**

- **Geometry.** A short chain between the converging fleets, running SW from the enemy's western flank: the Euryalus and Sirius 2–4 nm west and north-west of the enemy's line, the Phoebe and Naiad beyond, then the Defence, Colossus and Mars, and the Victory 5–6 nm SW of the Euryalus (table below). Place the frigates relative to the enemy's track. Low.
- **Paired blue lights, hourly.** The leading motif: two small, intense bluish-white flares together at a frigate, lighting her sails and their own smoke, held half a minute or more, then gone; repeated a few minutes later at the next link, down to the Victory, and answered by a light. Medium.
- **Between them.** Rockets from the masthead: a gold streak rising for a few seconds and a small burst. Counted gun flashes five seconds apart, orange-white, each leaving a lit puff. Brief priming flashes. Busiest around 6 p.m., 9 p.m. and midnight. Medium.
- **The fleet's wears.** At 8 p.m.: three gun flashes at the Victory, then two lights; the repeat at the Royal Sovereign four minutes later; the Victory's blue lights; three lights on each ship for the minutes of the turn. At 4 a.m. the same, with no record of blue lights. Medium (the book; the wear lights unlogged).
- **Lanterns and the dark.** The British side: a handful of warm points, the Admiral's lights and answering or signal lanterns that appear all at once and are held; the watching ships dark. The Combined Fleet: a string of warm points about 6 nm long, with its own gold rockets and gun flashes. No moon until the thin crescent rises at about 5.20 a.m. High.
- **Range.** Lanterns read to about 1–3 nm, blue lights to about 5–14 nm, rockets and gun flashes further (estimates; `research/scene-02/a-h3.md`, R21). The night chain could see itself from end to end; the British hulls stayed unseen. Low.

**What would be wrong.**

- **Later inventions.** Semaphore arms or shutter telegraphs on ships (the 1805 "telegraph" was Popham's flag code); flashing lamps (Colomb's system, adopted in 1867), beams or searchlights; red or green lights, lanterns or pyrotechnics (sidelights 1848 and 1858, firework colours after 1811); International Code flags in place of the 1799 numerals. High.
- **Wrong in kind.** A glowing line or travelling light between the ships (also on the avoid-list); an instant relay; a chain of frigates only; a chain drawn south-west; lantern figures as the frigates' reports; flags at night; a lit British fleet; moonlight. High.
