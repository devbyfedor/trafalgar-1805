# The camps on the heights of Boulogne, summer 1805, as seen from the roads

Research note for scene 2, beat 1 ("Boulogne from the sea"): the army's hut camps on the plateaus either side of the Liane, and Napoleon's own hut on the Tour d'Ordre plateau, as a viewer anchored in the roads 1.5–3 km out would see them on an August evening in 1805.

Builds on `../history-scene-02-merged.md` ("Boulogne and the invasion army"), `../h2-boulogne-cadiz.md` (H2-02) and `../a-h2a.md` (R15), and corrects one point in them (the Étaples roofs, under (a)). Confidence scale: the one at the top of `docs/history-notes.md` (high: several independent primary sources agree, or a checked computation; medium: one good source or a consistent inference; low: an estimate, or sources in conflict). Every fact says what it rests on: **period** (documents, letters, memoirs, plans and images of 1803–1805, and memoirs by eyewitnesses published later), **later** (19th- and early 20th-century histories: Bertrand 1828–29, Nicolaÿ 1907, Desbrière 1900–02, James), **modern** (archaeology reports, web pages; leads unless they report excavated evidence) or **inference** (mine). The spellings Tour d'Ordre and Tour d'Odre are both period forms of the same place.

## For the painter

1. **Rows of huts are what showed.** A British officer anchored off Boulogne in 1804 drew the heights north and south of the town covered with long, evenly spaced rows of small dark huts, several rows stacked up each seaward slope to the skyline, standing back behind the cliff-top batteries (E. D. Lewis, HMS *Tartarus*, "A S.E. View of the Town and Harbour of Boulogne with the Encampments on the Heights"). From the roads the camps read as dotted lines following the contours, not as single buildings. High for rows visible from the sea; medium for their exact placement.
2. **The hut.** A low, detached, gable-roofed hut for about 12–16 men, half sunk into the ground, walls of timber poles or planks with mud daub, turf and coastal stone, roof thatched with straw (rushes at Ostend). Footprints at Étaples 14–45 m², so a men's hut of roughly 6 × 4 m; ridge perhaps 3–4 m above the ground (inference). Colour: thatch golden when new and grey-brown after two winters; walls earth-ochre, though one period painting shows them white. High for thatch and mud, medium for size, low for colour.
3. **Camp plan.** Three long lines of huts parallel to the front (the sea), cut every battalion by cross streets; the officers' huts behind the third line, the kitchens behind those. So the evening kitchen smoke rose from the back (landward) edge of each camp, not between the front rows. Medium (one local historian; the three ranks by battalion and company match the Étaples excavation).
4. **Little gardens and streets.** Each hut had a garden of flowers and vegetables, with obelisks, columns, pyramids and figures modelled in clay and faced with shells; the streets carried signboards with victory names; inscriptions were painted on the hut gables; the officers' huts looked like small villas with aviaries and poultry yards. None of this shows from 2–3 km beyond a faint mottling of green around the rows. Medium.
5. **Extent.** Right camp: on the plateau north of the harbour, from the Tour d'Ordre north-east towards Terlincthun and Wimille, a front of more than a league (over 4 km). Left camp: on the Outreau plateau, from the brow facing the town back past a windmill (the Petit-Moulin) towards Le Portel. Each held one or two divisions, at roughly 600–800 huts per division plus officers' huts, kitchens and guard-houses (1804 counts of 518 and 748 huts for two of Soult's divisions). Medium for the places, low for the counts in 1805.
6. **Heights.** Right camp: cliff top about 55 m at the Tour d'Ordre (an 1810 eyewitness figure, matched by the modern terrain model), the ground then rising gently inland to about 70 m at 0.8 km and 90 m at 2 km. Left camp: the Outreau plateau is flat at 50–57 m, its brow 0.6–1 km back from the shore above the harbour flats and the Capécure beach. High.
7. **What the roads actually see.** From a boat 2–3.5 km out the whole shore stands only about 0.7–1.4° above the sea horizon; a 10 m hut at 3–4 km spans about 0.15°. The right camp's seaward slope is visible from the cliff top up to its crest about 1.5–2 km inland, so its rows climb in tiers; on the left camp only the huts on the brow and on the slopes facing the harbour show, as a line along the skyline, the flat plateau top being hidden behind its own edge. High for the angles (computation); medium for the hidden and visible areas (modern terrain).
8. **Napoleon's hut.** A long single-storey wooden pavilion of carefully planed planks painted light, whitish grey; about 32.5 × 7.3 m (100 × 22½ French feet), its end towards the harbour a rotunda about 9.7 m across (the council room), with a semicircular room at each end and windows along the sides; a wooden paling round it, lit after dark by reflector lamps every 1.3 m (4 ft). Built in 48 hours from prepared timber on a brick footing. High for wood and grey paint; medium for the plan and the dimensions (one local historian, 1828, and a valet's memoir that differ in detail).
9. **Where it stood.** On a small mound inside the ruined 1544 English bastions of the Tour d'Ordre, at the cliff edge immediately north of the harbour channel, about 50–55 m above the beach and about 0.9 km east of the jetty heads, with its rotunda end towards the harbour mouth, that is towards the roads. From 3.5 km it would be a small pale grey shape on the cliff top, about 0.5° long if seen side-on and much less end-on, lit full by the evening sun; at dusk, a ring of lamps. Medium.
10. **Its neighbours.** The admiral's pavilion, built like the Emperor's but smaller, with a signal mast ("sémaphore") between the two; the national flag hoisted at the admiral's hut with a gun recalled all craft into port. Marshal Soult's and the navy minister Decrès's huts were built "en forme de hutte de sauvage", thatched right down to the ground and glazed at the top, their rooms half underground and the main room round; Decrès's, said a later writer, looked from a distance like an enormous candle snuffer (a cone). The road from the town ran between the Emperor's hut and the other three; the great mortar battery of the Tour d'Ordre was a little further on. High for the thatch to the ground (a period memoir); medium for the cone shape and the layout.
11. **No tents.** In August 1805 the Boulogne camps were hut camps that had been lived in for two years; no source puts tents on the Boulogne heights that summer. Tents appear only as plans for the last move before embarking, for a 1804 grenadier camp by the basin, for ladies at the 16 August 1804 ceremony, and as campaign kit sent east in late August 1805. High that the view shows no tents.
12. **The column.** Its first stone was laid on 9 November 1804 on the plateau about 2 km east-north-east of the Tour d'Ordre, at about 92 m; by 1811–1814 it had reached only 19–20 m. In August 1805 it was at most a low grey stump of foundations and pedestal with a builders' yard, about 4–5 km from the roads: not a landmark. Low for its height in 1805.
13. **Windmills.** The 1804 drawing from the sea shows several post windmills on the heights north and east of the town and on the skyline; place names of the period (Moulin Hubert near Terlincthun, the Petit-Moulin on the Outreau plateau, Moulin-à-l'Huile) agree. A few dark wooden post mills with sails belong on the skyline. Medium.
14. **Telegraphs and signal masts.** A Chappe telegraph stood on the belfry of the upper town (an engraving of year XII, 1803–04), and the 1804 drawing marks two telegraphs; flag masts on the Tour d'Ordre, on the Fort de la Crèche and on the Mont de Couple repeated the admiral's signals to the flotilla. Expect a tall mast with arms over the town and flag masts with tricolours on the cliff tops. Medium.
15. **Later things to leave out.** The Legion of Honour ceremony of 16 August 1804 left only an earthen mound in a hollow at Terlincthun, hidden from the roads; its first marker stone dates from 1809 and its obelisk from later still. Napoleon's hut stood until about 1809–1810; the statue later put on its site (1856) and the Fort du Moulin-à-l'Huile (built 1806) do not belong in an 1805 view. Medium.

## Findings

### (a) The huts

**From tents to huts.** The troops reached the plateaus of the Tour d'Ordre and Outreau from 1 vendémiaire an XII (24 September 1803; Bertrand prints 23 September) and bivouacked for the first days or weeks; Nicolaÿ says they pitched tents first (later: Bertrand 1828, I, 334; Nicolaÿ 1907, 240–241). Napoleon had ordered on 28 August 1803 that the camps be built so that the troops could be lodged in them all winter, as close as possible to the embarkation points, and that the generals of division and brigade be housed in wooden huts in the middle of their camps (period: First Consul to the Minister of War, Saint-Cloud, 10 fructidor XI, in Desbrière, *Projets* III, 192–194). The hut camps were still in use in August 1805, and Napoleon's orders for the army's departure charged the general left on the coast with "la conservation des camps" (period: Corr. XI, No. 9137, p. 143). High.

**Materials.**
- Poles from the forest, turf cut from the plain and stones from the shore, the soldiers making posts, frames, partitions, plank beds, arms racks, doors and windows themselves, almost without tools (later: Bertrand 1828, I, 334–335, writing in Boulogne within a generation, probably from eyewitnesses).
- "Construites en terre et en feuillage", earth and branches, divided into streets by regiment, brigade and division, with broad avenues between (period: Méneval, Napoleon's secretary at Boulogne, *Mémoires* I, 347–348).
- Mostly wood and mud walls with thatched roofs, made for 15 men; to spare timber, after the forests of Boulogne, Guînes and Hardelot had been heavily cut, the huts were built partly of wood and partly of mud walls in the local peasant manner, with villagers, women included, helping to build the walls (later: Nicolaÿ 1907, 242–244, citing the Boulogne writer Brunet; not checked against Brunet).
- On the dunes at Ostend in September 1803 Davout's huts were of fir poles roofed with rushes, which the country preferred to straw (period: Davout to the First Consul, 15 September 1803, in Desbrière III, 207). In March 1805 Napoleon proposed shipping the timber of the Ostend huts to Ambleteuse to build huts for a new division, so the frames were reusable timber (period: Napoleon to Davout, 25 March 1805, Desbrière IV, 405).
- For his headquarters guard at Pont-de-Briques Napoleon asked for plank huts ("baraques en planches") about 150 paces behind the house (period: to Soult, 12 March 1804, Corr. IX No. 7614); the Guard's hutment there was finished by May–June 1804 (period: monthly report of prairial XII, Desbrière III, 436).
- The excavated camp at Étaples (Ney's corps, the 69e de ligne and the 6e and 25e légère, October 1803 to August 1805): 191 huts in six or seven rows, all of them half sunk into the ground and thatched, crowded with 12 to 16 men; inscriptions and graffiti cut on rubble stones, among them a stele with a bursting grenade; shallow ditched enclosures behind the last huts, probably the companies' vegetable gardens (modern, excavated evidence: Palaude and Lemaire 2016, 108–110, on the Inrap excavation of September–December 2010). An earlier dig at Étaples (2005, 19 huts) found huts of 14–45 m² opening on two parallel roads (modern: Salavert, Hello and Lemaire 2016, *Antiquity*; this figure seen only as quoted by a search engine, not in the full text).

High for timber frames with earth, turf and stone walls and thatch; medium for 15 men per hut and for the sizes.

**Roofs: thatch, not tiles.** The project's merged note says the Étaples huts had tiled roofs and glazed windows, citing Fondation Napoléon "newsletter 410". The page at that address is newsletter No. 345 (28 October–3 November 2005), and it says only that the 2005 dig of 19 huts would show how materials were used, listing roof tiles, and glass and lead for glazed windows. The excavators' later account of the far larger 2010 excavation says all the huts were half-sunk and thatched (Palaude and Lemaire 2016, 108). At Boulogne itself the period accounts name thatch: Soult's and Decrès's huts were thatched to the ground, Prince Joseph's hut in the left camp was thatched, and the scullery by Napoleon's pavilion was a small thatched lodge (period: Constant, *Mémoires* I, 248–251); Nicolaÿ gives thatch for the men's huts (242). Tiles, then, were at most occasional (perhaps officers' huts or reused material); the men's huts were thatched. High for thatch at Étaples; medium for the men's huts at Boulogne.

**Windows and doors.** The soldiers made doors and windows (Bertrand I, 335); window glass and lead came from the 2005 Étaples dig (newsletter 345). Glazed windows in at least some huts: medium.

**Size and shape.** No period dimension of a Boulogne men's hut was found. Inference from 12–16 men with plank beds, the 14–45 m² footprints at Étaples and the half-sunk floors: a men's hut about 5–7 m long and 4–5 m wide, eaves low (about 1–1.5 m above the ground), a steep thatched roof with its ridge about 3–4 m up, a door in the gable end facing the street. Low.

**Colour.** No period text gives the colour of the men's huts. Jean-François Hue's painting of the camp in July 1804 (Salon of 1806, Versailles) shows the huts on the far slope as small white-walled gabled huts with grey-brown roofs in a regular row, and plank sheds with dark roofs on the beach (period image). Lewis's 1804 drawing from the sea shows them as dark grey marks (period image, schematic). Daub of local clay is ochre to grey-brown; whitewash is possible but undocumented; thatch is golden when new and weathers to grey-brown or silvery grey within a year or two, and these roofs were up to two years old. Low; the painter may choose within this range.

**Men per hut and number of huts.** 15 men (Nicolaÿ 242); 12–16 (Étaples). In May–June 1804 one division of Soult's camp of Saint-Omer had 748 huts standing and 30 more to build, another 518 standing and 61 to build, a third 63 to build and 34 to repair; the first rank of huts of one regiment needed repair and the third rank of another was still to be built (period: report on the army for prairial XII, Desbrière III, 430–434). So a division lived in roughly 600–800 huts, in at least three ranks per regiment. Which divisions stood in the Boulogne camps in August 1805 is not fully clear (see (b)). High for the 1804 counts; low for any total in August 1805.

**Officers' huts.** Generals' huts stood in the middle of their camps (order of 28 August 1803). Officers' huts were behind the third line of men's huts and looked like country villas, with gardens, aviaries, poultry yards, dovecotes and artificial warrens (Bertrand I, 335–336; Nicolaÿ 242–243). Three officers' huts were built for the artillery in May–June 1804 (Desbrière III, 435). Medium.

**Kitchens and smoke.** Behind the officers' quarters, a little further back, stood the kitchens, placed so as to serve each battalion separately (Bertrand I, 335; Nicolaÿ 242). The Étaples excavators say heating was forbidden in the huts (Palaude and Lemaire 2016, 108), although the same team's charcoal study speaks of firewood stockpiled for light and heat in the camp (Salavert et al. 2016, abstract). So the evening smoke rose mainly along the kitchen lines at the landward back of each camp, perhaps with some from huts. Hue's 1804 painting shows a single plume rising from a building low on the left-camp slope. No source found mentions chimneys on the men's huts, or says what the kitchens looked like (sheds, open hearths in trenches or field ovens); the army's bread came from the bakery (manutention) in Boulogne, which drew workmen from every division (Desbrière III, 431–434). Inference: draw the men's huts without chimneys and put the smoke over low kitchen sheds behind the lines. Medium for the kitchens' position; low for their form and for smoke from the huts themselves.

**Streets, names and decorations.** The lines were cut at intervals by cross streets separating the battalions, named after the main victories (Bertrand I, 335); Nicolaÿ lists Valmy, Jemmapes, Fleurus, Campo-Formio, Zurich and the Saint-Bernard, with avenues of the États-Généraux, the Constituante, the Jeu-de-Paume, the Fédération, the Convention, the Pyramides and Marengo, a signboard at each corner, and cross streets paved like a mosaic (242). Constant instead says the right camp's streets bore the names of distinguished generals (I, 249). Inscriptions, heroic or burlesque, were read on the gables (Méneval I, 347–348). Gardens of flowers, fruit and vegetables held obelisks, columns, pyramids and allegorical figures of clay faced with shells from the beach, which at a short distance looked like fine marble; crosses of the Legion of Honour and other designs were cut in the turf (Bertrand I, 335; Nicolaÿ 242–243). Medium.

### (b) Where the camps stood, and what showed from the roads

**The right camp.** On the plateau north of the Liane mouth, from the Tour d'Ordre to Wimille, and the left camp from the summit of the Outreau hill to Le Portel (later: Bertrand I, 334; Nicolaÿ 241, who adds that the site chosen was a stretch of four kilometres along the coast). The lines formed a "front de bandière" of more than a league (Bertrand I, 334–335). For scale: the Tour d'Ordre is 4.4 km from the centre of Wimille in a straight line, north-east along the plateau, and the Outreau brow 1.4 km from Le Portel (computed from French Wikipedia coordinates), so the right camp was much the longer. Soult's order of 21 September 1804 placed the column between the Emperor's headquarters at the Tour d'Ordre and the camp of the first division, and the navy's order of 8 November 1804 sent its detachment to the first division's plateau at the Tour d'Ordre (period orders as printed in English by Nicolaÿ 309–311). In May–June 1804 Soult's first division gave most of its workmen to Wimereux and some to the Fort de la Crèche, his second to the port of Boulogne and the Fort de l'Heurt off Le Portel, his third to Ambleteuse and his fourth to Wimereux (Desbrière III, 431–434), which fits the first division at the Tour d'Ordre end of the right camp, the fourth further north towards Wimereux and the second in the left camp (inference). Similar camps stood at Terlincthun, Wimereux, Ambleteuse, Étaples, round the ramparts of Boulogne and on the plateaus of Ostrohove and Herquelingue (Bertrand I, 336); Dupont's division camped in December 1803 on the height of Ostrohove above Boulogne in a camp laid out in the Roman manner, a perfect square, its huts not yet built (period: Maurice Dupin's letter of 30 frimaire XII, printed in George Sand, *Histoire de ma vie*). The Wikipedia page's "Terlincthun valley" for the right camp is therefore supported by Bertrand's separate Terlincthun camp and by the Tour d'Ordre–Wimille extent. Medium.

**Which troops in August 1805.** Napoleon meant in March 1805 to put Legrand's and Suchet's divisions "dans tout l'arrondissement de Boulogne", the reserve and the Guard at Wimereux and twenty battalions of Davout's at Ambleteuse (period: to Soult, 13 March 1805, Desbrière IV, 404–405); the embarkation scheme of 1805 gave the Boulogne escadrilles the divisions of Saint-Hilaire, Vandamme, Legrand and Suchet, the Guard and the reserve grenadiers (Desbrière IV, 430). Soult's corps of the camp of Saint-Omer counted 45,907 men in the spring-1805 return (H2 notes). The Guard infantry was ordered from Paris to Boulogne on 7 August 1805, partly held back on the 8th (Corr. XI Nos. 9056, 9058); Coignet, a grenadier of the Guard, says they camped by the port of Ambleteuse with Oudinot's grenadiers above them (period memoir, *Cahiers*, 113). Medium.

**Heights.** Henry, an engineer officer who wrote a history of Boulogne in 1810, gives the cliff at the Tour d'Ordre as 55 m (period, quoted by Nicolaÿ 45). Constant puts all four camps on "une falaise très-élevée", the left camp at about the same height as the right (I, 245, 251). The modern terrain (IGN RGE ALTI, bare earth, sampled every 50 m; my computation) gives:

- Tour d'Ordre to the column (east-north-east): the cliff rises from the beach to about 60 m within 300 m; then 61–66 m at 0.3–0.6 km, 70 m at 0.8 km, 77 m at 1.1 km, 83 m at 1.4 km and 92 m at the column, 2 km inland.
- Due east from the Tour d'Ordre the plateau reaches 72 m at 0.7 km and then falls into a valley (about 32 m at 1.2 km) behind the town before rising to 98 m at 2.2 km.
- At 50.740° N the ground rises from the shore through 20–40 m at 0.4–0.8 km and 55 m at 1 km to 80–90 m at 1.4–2.5 km: a broad slope facing the sea. A hollow at 30–40 m lies about 1.2 km north of the Tour d'Ordre (the Terlincthun hollow), and a coastal rise of 60–70 m at about 50.748° N (the heights over La Crèche).
- Outreau: from the harbour flats (6–9 m) the ground climbs to 42–50 m at 0.6–0.8 km (the brow facing the town), then stays almost level at 53–57 m for 2 km south. Westward the plateau edge stands about 1.1 km from today's shore line at 50.715° N; at Le Portel the ground rises from 13–30 m near the shore to about 60 m 2 km inland.

The column's base computed at 92 m agrees with Bertrand's figure for its summit, 143.11 m above mean sea level, less its height of about 53 m (later: Bertrand II, 174). The cliff edge has retreated since 1805 and the port and the Capécure and Outreau shore have been rebuilt and reclaimed, so the shore lines are not those of 1805; the plateau surfaces are little changed. High for the heights.

**Distance from the cliff edge.** No plan of the hut lines was found. Bertrand says the left camp's first hut began at the very brow of the Outreau hill facing the town and its last stood beyond the Petit-Moulin (later: Bertrand II, 52). The cliff tops themselves were taken by batteries: Lewis's drawing shows batteries along the edge and the first rows of huts a little up the slope behind them; Hue's painting shows a row of huts part way up the slope of the left camp and a long row along its crest (period images). Napoleon's own hut stood "sur le bord de la falaise" (Bertrand I, 344), though Nicolaÿ says it was set somewhat back for fear of landslips (49). Inference: in both camps the hut lines began a few hundred metres back from the cliff edge, behind the batteries, and ran up the slopes and along the plateaus. Low.

**What showed from the roads.** Computed from the modern terrain for a boat at 50.733° N 1.548° E (about 3.5 km west of the Tour d'Ordre, eye 3 m above the water, standard refraction):

- The Tour d'Ordre cliff top stands about 0.7–0.8° above the sea horizon, due east (bearing 92°); the column's site about 1.0° at bearing 80°, 5 km; the Outreau brow about 0.8° at bearing 123°, 3.1 km; the upper town (the belfry) about 0.6° at bearing 99°, 4.7 km.
- The land skyline runs at 0.7–1.06° from bearing 60° to 160°; behind the town it is formed by hills 6–7 km away (110–120 m), and beyond the edge of my terrain sample Mont Lambert (about 190 m, some 8 km off) would lift it to about 1.3° (inference).
- From 1.5–2 km off the harbour (50.730° N 1.565° E) the same shore stands 1.0–1.44° high.
- A hut 10 m long spans 0.14° at 4 km and a 4 m ridge 0.06°; the Emperor's pavilion, 32.5 m long, 0.5° at 3.5 km side-on.
- Visibility: from the roads the right camp's seaward slope is in view from the cliff top up to its crest, 1.5–2 km inland, and also the Tour d'Ordre plateau to about 0.7 km behind the edge, beyond which the ground drops out of sight into the valley behind the town. On the left camp only the north-western brow and the slopes facing the harbour and the beach are in view; the level top of the Outreau plateau is hidden behind its own edge, so huts there show only where they stand on the brow, as a line along the skyline.

So the camps showed from the roads as rows of roofs on the slopes and along the skyline, the right camp's rows climbing in tiers towards the crest, the left camp's lying along the brow, matching Lewis's drawing. High for the angles; medium for the visible areas, given the modern terrain and the unknown lines.

### (c) Napoleon's hut and its neighbours

**Who describes it.** Constant, Napoleon's valet, describes it with details supplied by the nephew of its builder, Lecat de Rue, then aide-de-camp to Soult (period memoir, published 1830, *Mémoires* I, 245–250). Bertrand gives its size and setting (later, 1828, I, 344–345, with the plan legend at the head of the volume). Méneval describes the site and the telescope (period memoir, I, 346–347). Nicolaÿ combines these with Marco de Saint-Hilaire, Morand and local memory (later, 1907, 16–31).

**The building.**
- Wood, like the booths of a fair but with carefully worked planks painted "gris blanc" (Constant), "gris" (Bertrand), light grey (Nicolaÿ). High.
- A long rectangle with a semicircular pavilion at each end (Constant); 100 feet long and 22½ wide, the end towards the port a rotunda 30 feet across holding the council room (Bertrand). In French feet of 0.3248 m: 32.5 × 7.3 m, rotunda 9.7 m, so the rotunda bulged wider than the body. Louis Nicolaÿ, writing in 1864, called it a wooden house 40 m long (Nicolaÿ 17). Medium for the dimensions.
- Windows along the sides; a surrounding enclosure of wooden lattice; outside lamps ("réverbères") four feet apart (Constant); a wooden fence and reflector lamps (Nicolaÿ, following Constant). Medium.
- Built in 48 hours from timber framed in advance, the frame set in a brick basement against the wind (Nicolaÿ 17, citing Louis Nicolaÿ and Florentin Lefils); by the engineer of military communications Sordi (Constant; Nicolaÿ). Medium.
- Single storey, one row of windows, so that later writers compared it with Longwood (Nicolaÿ 28). Medium.
- Inside: the council room papered silver-grey with a ceiling of golden clouds and an eagle in a blue sky; the bedroom papered pink; a room with a telescope; a drawing room, vestibule and dining room on the main body; a passage to the kitchens; and outside, towards the kitchens, a small thatched lodge for washing up (Constant I, 245–248). None of the interior shows from the sea.
- Gardens on its south side, with lawns, paths of yellow sand, loose-stone walls, hedges, clumps of shrubs and a pond with two black swans (Nicolaÿ 59–60; local tradition). Low.

**Where.** On the edge of the cliff, inside the old fortifications the English built in 1544 round the Roman lighthouse, on a small mound from which one saw the camps, the town, the port, the roads and the English coast (Bertrand I, 344–345); in one of the old bastions of the Tour d'Ordre (Bertrand's plan legend, I, no. 34). Nicolaÿ identifies the mound with the curtain of the 16th-century work, set back from the crumbling edge (45, 49). The lighthouse itself had fallen in 1644; in 1805 its rubble littered the beach below (Henry 1810, via Nicolaÿ 45). The "Plan du port et de la ville de Boulogne en l'an XII (1804)" (a 19th-century copy by the engineer Vivenon of the original in the Dépôt des fortifications) marks "Tour d'Odre" on the plateau at the cliff edge, the "Batterie de la Tour d'Odre" just north of it, and the cliff running north from the town along the east side of the harbour channel; measured on its 1:5,000 scale, the Tour d'Ordre lies about 370 m north-north-east of the root of the jetties and about 0.9 km east of the jetty heads, the harbour mouth bearing a little north of west from it (period plan, later copy; my measurement). Modern coordinates of the lighthouse site: about 50.7317° N, 1.5972° E (French Wikipedia, approximate). Napoleon dated letters from "la baraque de la Tour d'Ordre" in August 1804 and "La Tour d'Ordre" on 12 August 1805 (R15). High for the place; medium for the metres.

**Orientation, inferred.** The rotunda faced the port (Bertrand) and the pavilion "qui regardait la mer" held the council room (Constant); the bedroom's window looked over the right camp with the sea on the left (Constant I, 246). With the harbour mouth to the west-north-west, the long axis ran roughly east–west, the rotunda end facing the roads. From the roads the hut would be seen nearly end-on: the rounded grey end, about 10 m wide, on its mound at the cliff top, the body behind it. Low (inference from three statements and a plan).

**Could it be picked out?** It was the largest building on the right camp's cliff top, pale grey against grass and earth, on a mound at the very edge, and from the roads it would stand 0.7–0.9° above the horizon, lit full on by the low western sun. At 3–3.5 km a 10 m end subtends about 0.17° and the full 32.5 m length 0.5°; it could be picked out with a glass, and as a pale fleck with the naked eye (inference). No view from the sea was found that marks it. Medium.

**The neighbours.**
- The admiral's hut: the same plan as the Emperor's on a smaller scale, elegantly furnished (Constant I, 248; Nicolaÿ 21). Its site was later called the Enclos de l'Amiral (Nicolaÿ 16 n.). Admiral Bruix died on 27 ventôse XIII (18 March 1805) (Nicolaÿ 186–187); Rear-Admiral Lacrosse commanded the flotilla at Boulogne by May 1805 (period: Decrès to Lacrosse, 6 May 1805, Desbrière IV, 409). So in August 1805 the "admiral's hut" was Lacrosse's, or stood empty. High for Bruix's death; medium for the hut's use in 1805.
- The signal post: "le sémaphore des signaux, sorte de télégraphe maritime qui faisait manœuvrer la flotte", beside the admiral's hut (Constant I, 248); between the two pavilions (Nicolaÿ 22), with a signalling terrace beside it (Nicolaÿ 29). The admiral's order of 15 prairial XII (4 June 1804) made a national flag hoisted "à la baraque de l'amiral", with a gun, the signal for all craft out at sea to return (period: Desbrière III, 537–538 n.); the order of 27 germinal XII (17 April 1804) had flagstaffs on the Fort de la Crèche and the Mont de Couple repeat the admiral's signals from the Tour d'Ordre (period order, quoted by Nicolaÿ 29). A semaphore at the Tour d'Ordre still served as a survey mark in 1809 (Bertrand I, 426). Expect a tall mast with yards for flags. High for a signal mast; medium for its form.
- Soult's hut: to the right of the Tour d'Ordre, "en forme de hutte de sauvage, couverte en chaume jusqu'à terre et vitrée par le haut", one door leading down into rooms as if buried, the main room round (Constant I, 248–249; Nicolaÿ 22). Decrès's hut was made and laid out like it (Constant I, 249), but smaller and less comfortable, and seen from afar it looked like an enormous candle snuffer (Nicolaÿ 22, source of the image not given). So two thatched domes or cones near the cliff top. High for thatch to the ground and half-buried rooms (Constant, with Bertrand's "barraques des ministres, du général en chef et de l'amiral" at some distance from the Emperor's, I, 345); medium for the cone.
- The road: the only road from Boulogne to the right camp left the town by the rue des Vieillards and crossed the cliff top between the Emperor's hut and those of Bruix, Soult and Decrès (Constant I, 249); Sordi cut a paved zigzag path from the hut down the cliff to the beach in three days and nights (Constant I, 250; Nicolaÿ 30–31). Medium.
- The Tour d'Ordre battery, a little further on: six mortars, six howitzers and twelve 24-pounders (Constant I, 248); the battery and the lower battery of the Tour d'Ordre appear in the 1804 reports (Desbrière III, 435). Left to the note on forts.

**After 1805.** The hut was not kept up after 1805: it still stood in 1808 (Nicolaÿ 23), was beginning to fall into ruin by 1809 and stood only in part in 1810; on his visit of September 1811 Napoleon went to the admiral's hut "car la sienne n'existait plus" (Bertrand I, 424, 428; Nicolaÿ 23). By 1829 only its outline could be traced (Bertrand II, 176). A statue was set on its site in 1856 and fell in 1894 (Nicolaÿ 26–27). None of this belongs in the 1805 view.

### (d) Tents

The question is whether any tents stood on the Boulogne heights in summer 1805. The evidence:

- Hut camps from autumn 1803, built to last the winter and kept in use until the army marched on 26–31 August 1805; the general left behind was to see to "la conservation des camps", and the Italian division and third battalions were to occupy them (period: orders of 28 August 1803 and 26 August 1805; Desbrière III, 192–194; Corr. XI No. 9137, pp. 143–144). High.
- Tents as a plan, not a practice: in March 1804 Napoleon told Ney that when the boats arrived and the expedition drew near, "avec des tentes" the troops could be placed wherever wanted (period: Corr. IX No. 7609, 10 March 1804). In July 1804 he asked whether Boulogne had the tents needed to camp Junot's ten battalions of reserve grenadiers near the circular basin (period: Corr. IX No. 7869, 26 July 1804). Whether that grenadier camp was ever pitched was not found. Medium.
- Tents at a ceremony: at the Legion of Honour distribution of 16 August 1804 there were tents reserved for the ladies behind the crowd (period: *Journal des Débats* report quoted by Nicolaÿ 299); Hennequin's painting of the day shows a few white wall tents on a knoll at the edge of the crowd (period image). A 1804 event only. High.
- Tents as campaign kit: Napoleon's order of march of 26 August 1805 had camp equipment sent from Metz to Strasbourg so that by 1 vendémiaire (23 September) there would be "de quoi tenter 80,000 hommes" (period: Corr. XI No. 9137, p. 142, checked on the page image). The army at Boulogne, in other words, was not carrying its own tents.
- The Guard, arriving in August 1805, "camped" by the port of Ambleteuse (Coignet 113), not at Boulogne; what it lived in is not said. Low.

Conclusion: no source puts tents in the Boulogne right or left camps in summer 1805, and the camps were long-standing hut camps; tents in the view would be wrong. High for no tents in the hut lines; medium that no tent at all stood anywhere on the heights (an officer's marquee or a sutler's awning cannot be excluded, and would be invisible at 2–3 km anyway).

### (e) Other things on the heights

**The column.** On 21 September 1804 Soult's order fixed a column of 50 m on a square pedestal, with a colossal bronze statue, between the Emperor's headquarters at the Tour d'Ordre and the camp of the first division, its first stone to be laid on 18 brumaire XIII (period order, Nicolaÿ 309). Soult laid the first stone on 18 brumaire XIII, 9 November 1804, about 200 m from the Calais road, on a plateau in line from Mont Lambert to the Fort de la Crèche (Bertrand I, 383, 390; Nicolaÿ 311–312, whose translator misconverts the date to 6 November). The foundations were dug 6 m deep (Nicolaÿ 312) and built of rock from the cliffs and shore; the shaft was to be of local marble from Marquise, a brown "stinkal" for the pedestal and an ash-grey for the column, 150 feet high in all (Bertrand I, 390–391). Work "took on at once a great activity" (Bertrand I, 390), but the column had reached only 19 m when work stopped in 1814 (Bertrand II, 174); Wikipedia gives about 20 m when the works closed for lack of money on 3 December 1811 (modern, uncited). A Boulogne pamphlet of 1841 says the works went slowly and with many interruptions until September 1811, that the Restoration found the column "à peine sorti de terre" with all its marble cut and lying about and a scaffold nearly 180 feet high (built at some unstated date), and quotes the prefect in 1817: the column stood 50 feet high of a planned 150 (later: A*** P***, *Colonne de la grande armée à Boulogne-sur-Mer*, 1841, on Wikisource). Nothing found gives its height in August 1805. Site: 50.7412° N, 1.6183° E, ground about 92 m (computed). Inference: in August 1805 a builders' yard with the foundations and at most the lower courses of the pedestal, perhaps with sheds, cranes or scaffolding, 2 km inland and 4–5 km from the roads; at most a fleck on the skyline. Low.

**The Legion of Honour site.** The distribution of 16 August 1804 took place in a natural amphitheatre in the hollow between Moulin Hubert and Terlincthun, opening towards the cliffs, close to the Emperor's hut; the throne stood on a mound, its platform 16 feet square and 8 feet high (period: *Journal des Débats* and *Journal de Paris* reports, quoted by Nicolaÿ 298–302). By 1809 the ground was under the plough; the Agricultural Society bought it and on 3 December 1809 set a marble block on a masonry base at the throne's site (Bertrand I, 424–425); that stone was destroyed after 1815, replaced in 1830 and later by the present monument (Nicolaÿ 307–308). In August 1805 there was at most the earthen mound in the hollow; the terrain computation puts the hollow at 30–40 m behind a coastal rise of 40–50 m, hidden from the roads. Medium.

**Windmills.** Lewis's 1804 view from the sea shows about five post windmills on the hills north and east of the town, two pairs part way up and one on the far skyline (period image). Place names in the period and in Bertrand: Moulin Hubert (between the Tour d'Ordre and Terlincthun), the Petit-Moulin (Outreau plateau), Moulin-à-l'Huile (by the Calais road; a fort named after it was built there in 1806), and a Moulin-Wiber near the Tour d'Ordre in 1618 (Bertrand II, 52, 173–174; Nicolaÿ 49, 298). Medium.

**Telegraphs and signals.**
- The Chappe telegraph: Nicolaÿ reproduces an engraving of year XII (1803–04) of "le tachygraphe de Chappe sur le beffroi de Boulogne" (Nicolaÿ 33); French Wikipedia also says the belfry served as a relay of the Chappe telegraph. A telegram from Soult to Napoleon of 12 June 1804, reporting the English squadron still off Boulogne, was sent by Chappe telegraph (period document, Archives nationales AE II 2302, on Commons). Lewis's key of 1804 lists "The Telegraph" and "The Telegraph on Telegraph Hill"; his drawing shows a tall mast with arms and small vanes at its head beside the church spire over the town. So: a Chappe instrument on the belfry of the upper town, standing above the roofs. Medium (the date the Boulogne branch opened was not checked).
- Signal masts: on the Tour d'Ordre, the Fort de la Crèche and the Mont de Couple (above). Flags on the batteries along the cliff tops appear throughout Lewis's drawing. High for flags on the batteries; medium for the masts' form.

**Field works.** Bertrand's plan legend shows parts of an entrenched camp (camp retranché) round the town, with the Italian troops and Corsican chasseurs camped in front of it (I, plan legend nos. 21–33); the 1804 plan shows a bastioned battery and a redoubt by the Tour d'Ordre and a long line of entrenchment to the east. Low-lying earthworks: low priority for the view. Medium.

**The Tour d'Ordre itself.** Only rubble on the beach and stumps of the 1544 brick works on the cliff top remained (Nicolaÿ 44–45, citing Henry 1810); nothing tower-like stood there in 1805. High.

## Doubts and disagreements

- **Thatch or tiles.** Newsletter 345 of the Fondation Napoléon (2005, at the address `newsletters/410.html` that the merged note calls "newsletter 410") lists roof tiles among the materials of a 2005 dig of 19 huts at Étaples; the excavators' account of the 2010 excavation of 191 huts says thatch; every Boulogne account that names a roof says thatch. The merged note's "tiled roofs" should read: thatched as a rule, tiles found at one Étaples site. The merged note should be corrected (not done here: outside this note).
- **Hut colour.** Hue's painting (1804–06) shows white walls; Lewis's drawing (1804) shows dark marks; no text gives a colour. Thatch weathers grey. Unresolved.
- **Hut size and height.** No period dimension for Boulogne; the Étaples 14–45 m² comes from a search-engine quotation of the *Antiquity* paper, not read in full. My 5–7 × 4–5 m and 3–4 m ridge are inference.
- **Men per hut.** 15 (Nicolaÿ, from Brunet) against 12–16 (Étaples); a division of perhaps 7,000–10,000 men in 600–800 huts gives 10–15 including all hut types (inference).
- **Street names.** Victories (Bertrand; Nicolaÿ with a list) or distinguished generals (Constant). Both may have been used.
- **Distance of the lines from the cliff edge, their orientation and the exact extent of each camp in 1805.** No period plan of the hut lines was found; a plan of the camps may survive in the Service historique de la Défense (not consulted), and Gallica's plans of 1804–05 show the port, not the camps. Bertrand's own plan (1828) was not scanned unfolded in the copy consulted.
- **The Emperor's hut.** 100 × 22½ feet (Bertrand) against 40 m (Louis Nicolaÿ, 1864); "on the edge of the cliff" (Bertrand) against set back from it (Nicolaÿ); a semicircular pavilion at each end (Constant) against a rotunda at the port end (Bertrand), which agree if both ends were round and the port end larger. Its orientation is inferred.
- **Constant.** His memoirs (1830) were written up with help from others; for the hut he names his informant, the builder's nephew. Nicolaÿ (1907) condenses Constant, Marco de Saint-Hilaire, Bertrand and others without always saying which detail comes from whom.
- **The column in August 1805.** No source gives its height then; 19–20 m by 1811–14 is the only measure.
- **The Guard in August 1805.** Ordered to Boulogne on 7 August and partly stopped on the 8th; Coignet puts the grenadiers by Ambleteuse. What they lived in is unknown.
- **Which divisions in which camp in August 1805.** The first division (Saint-Hilaire) at the Tour d'Ordre is inferred from the 1804 orders about the column; the others are not placed.
- **The terrain.** The heights come from a modern bare-earth model; the cliffs have retreated, the shore and port have been rebuilt, and the left bank of the Liane has been filled. The view angles are for two chosen viewpoints and change with the boat's position.
- **Do not use images of the later camp.** "Camp de Boulogne" also names Napoleon III's camp of 1854–57, whose photographs and plans (Royal Collection; Gallica's "Carte topographique du territoire occupé par les camps de Boulogne en 1854") show thatched huts of another army and another date.
