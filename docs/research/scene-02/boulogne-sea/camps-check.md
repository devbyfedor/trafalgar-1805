# Check of `camps.md`: the camps on the heights of Boulogne, summer 1805

An independent, adversarial check of `camps.md` (same folder), claim by claim. I did not write that note and do not edit it. Every verdict says what I opened and where. Verdicts: **confirmed** (the source says it, at that place, and nothing better contradicts it), **corrected** (substantially right, but a page, figure, date, attribution or confidence needs changing), **unsupported** (the cited source does not say it, or I could not find it there or anywhere), **refuted** (the evidence says otherwise). Confidence uses the scale at the top of `docs/history-notes.md`. Kinds of evidence as in `camps.md`: **period**, **later** (19th–early 20th century), **modern**, **inference**.

## Verdicts

### (a) The huts

- **From tents to huts, autumn 1803.** **Corrected** (Nicolaÿ's date). Bertrand I p. 334 prints "1er vendémiaire an XII (23 septembre 1803)", has the troops bivouac for the first days and then build huts ([n366](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n366/mode/1up)). The year XII began on 24 September 1803, so the note's correction stands. Nicolaÿ p. 240 dates the move to "the beginning of September, 1803" and has the troops leave their cantonments "to pitch their tents" on the plateaux of Odre and Outreau; p. 241 says they had to bivouac "for several weeks" ([n259](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n259/mode/1up)). So Nicolaÿ gives both tents and bivouac, and an earlier date. The orders of 29 August 1803 sent Soult and Davout off on 3–7 September (Desbrière III p. 206, [n233](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n233/mode/1up)). Later. Medium.
- **The order of 28 August 1803.** Confirmed. The heading (First Consul to the Minister of War, Saint-Cloud, 10 fructidor XI) is on Desbrière III p. 190. Page 194 has the camps placed so that the troops can occupy them all winter, as near as possible to the embarkation points, and the generals of division and brigade housed in the middle of their camps in "baraques en bois" ([n221](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n221/mode/1up)). Added: page 193 orders "deux camps en baraques à Boulogne : l'un sur la droite, l'autre sur la gauche du port" ([n220](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n220/mode/1up)). Period. High.
- **"La conservation des camps", 26 August 1805.** Confirmed: Corr. XI No. 9137, p. 143 ([n157](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/n157/mode/1up)). Page 144 adds that the three Italian regiments were to come to Boulogne "au camp de gauche", and that one senior artillery officer was to be camped in the right camp of Boulogne and one in the left ([n158](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/n158/mode/1up)). Period. High.
- **Materials: poles, turf, shore stones, almost no tools (Bertrand).** Confirmed: Bertrand I p. 334, with posts, frames, partitions, plank beds, arms racks, doors and "croisées" (casements) all made by the soldiers ([n366](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n366/mode/1up)). Later. Medium.
- **Méneval: earth and branches, streets, avenues.** Confirmed: Méneval I p. 347 ([n383](https://archive.org/details/mmoirespourser01mnuoft/page/n383/mode/1up)); the inscriptions "au fronton" of the huts run onto p. 348. "Feuillage" says branches or leafy brushwood, not thatch. Period memoir. Medium.
- **Wood and mud walls, thatch, 15 men (Nicolaÿ, from Brunet).** **Corrected** (attribution). Nicolaÿ p. 242 has the huts "mostly built of wood and mud-walls, with thatched roofs", made for fifteen men, placed "at equal distances" in three rows over more than four kilometres ([n261](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n261/mode/1up)). The credit to Brunet on p. 243 closes the passage of pp. 241–243. The next paragraphs (pp. 243–244), on sparing the felled forests of Boulogne, Guînes and Hardelot, building partly in mud walls in the peasant manner, and calling in villagers, women included, come after that credit and name no source ([n262](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n262/mode/1up)). Later. Medium.
- **Ostend: fir poles roofed with rushes.** Confirmed: Davout, 28 fructidor XI (15 September 1803), Desbrière III p. 207: "perches de sapin et couvertes de jonc", a covering the country preferred to straw ([n234](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n234/mode/1up)). Period. High.
- **Ostend hut timber to Ambleteuse, 25 March 1805.** **Corrected** (wording). In Desbrière IV p. 405 (Corr. 8481) Napoleon says that "the project" of shipping the timber used for the huts "pourrait être avantageux". He approves a proposal; he does not make it ([n64](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n64/mode/1up)). The same letter sends the first division of the Ostend camp to Ambleteuse to take the place of Legrand's division. Period. High.
- **Plank huts at Pont-de-Briques.** Confirmed: Corr. IX No. 7614 (12 March 1804), p. 285: three "baraques en planches", each for three companies of grenadiers, about 150 paces behind the house ([n297](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede09napouoft/page/n297/mode/1up)). The prairial XII report says the Guard's hutment at Pont-de-Briques was finished (Desbrière III p. 436, [n463](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n463/mode/1up)). Period. High.
- **The Étaples excavation of 2010.** Confirmed, with one point added. Palaude and Lemaire 2016 say on p. 108 that all the troops lived in half-sunk, thatched huts, and in their opening summary that heating was banned in them. On p. 109 they give 191 huts in six or seven rows, about fifty pits and shallow ditched enclosures behind the last huts (probably the companies' vegetable gardens), inscriptions, graffiti on rubble stones and a stele with a bursting grenade. On p. 110 the huts held 12 or 16 men. Added: p. 109 says the camp followed the order of battle of the 1791 regulation, the men's huts laid out in **three ranks by battalion and company**, which the note uses only in its painter list. Modern, excavated evidence. High.
- **14–45 m² and two parallel roads (2005 dig).** **Unsupported.** I opened the HAL record (refused by bot protection), the *Antiquity* abstract on cambridge.org (no hut size; it does say the soldiers stockpiled wood "for lighting and heating") and Inrap's 2005 press release (HTTP 403). The figure is still only a search-engine quotation. Low.
- **Roofs: thatch, not tiles.** Confirmed. The page at `newsletters/410.html` is Newsletter No. 345 (28 October–3 November 2005). It reports 19 huts and lists roof tiles, and glass and lead for glazed windows, among the materials whose use the dig would show. It is not a report of finds. The 2010 excavators give thatch for all the huts (p. 108). At Boulogne: Constant I p. 248 (the thatched scullery), pp. 248–249 (Soult's hut thatched to the ground) and p. 251 (Joseph's hut "couverte en chaume") ([n273](https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdeconst01wairuoft/page/n273/mode/1up), [n276](https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdeconst01wairuoft/page/n276/mode/1up)). Added, for the "peasant manner": Bertrand II p. 51 describes the country houses of the Boulonnais as built of stone, brick and "charpente plaquée d'argile" (timber frame daubed with clay), and those of Équihen as made of earth ([n61](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_MLwWAAAAQAAJ/page/n61/mode/1up)). High for thatch at Étaples; medium for the men's huts at Boulogne, as the note says.
- **Windows and doors.** Confirmed for doors and casements (Bertrand I p. 334). Palaude and Lemaire list door and window fittings ("éléments d'huisserie") among the finds (p. 108), but no window glass. The newsletter's glass and lead are an expectation, not a find. Medium for windows; low for glazing in the men's huts.
- **Size and shape (inference).** Fair as inference, with one support added. Méneval's inscriptions "au fronton" (p. 348) and the row of huts in Hue's painting, which shows pointed gable ends to the viewer, both put a gable end on the street. On half-sunk floors the walls above ground would be low, so a ridge nearer 2.5–3.5 m than 4 m is as likely. Low.
- **Colour.** Confirmed as unresolved; my reading of the images differs a little. In Hue's painting (Commons copy, 1,945 px wide) the row on the far slope is of pale, near-white gabled huts with greyish roofs, too small to tell walls from roofs with certainty. In Lewis's drawing (full 3,800 px TIFF) each hut is a **mid-grey** rounded mark with a pale edge left in the paper, not a dark one. No text gives a colour. Low.
- **Men per hut; hut counts.** **Corrected** (whose huts). The prairial XII report (21 May–19 June 1804) is on Desbrière III pp. 430–434. The 748 huts (with 30 more to build) belong to its third division, which gave 1,000 workmen a day to the port of Ambleteuse; the 518 (with 61 to build) to its fourth, which gave 640 to Wimereux ([n459](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n459/mode/1up), [n460](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n460/mode/1up)). The situation of 21 May 1804 on p. 428 places these divisions: **Legrand's at Ambleteuse and Suchet's at Wimereux** ([n455](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n455/mode/1up)). The division of the Boulogne right camp (the first) has only "34 to repair and 63 to build" (p. 430), and the Outreau division (the second) no count. So no hut count is known for the two Boulogne camps. As a scale: Legrand's 7,755 men present had 748 huts, about 10 men per hut counting every kind of hut. The fourth division's note that "le 3e rang du 34e" was still to be built (p. 433) supports three ranks per regiment. Period. High for the counts; low for any Boulogne total.
- **Officers' huts.** Confirmed: Bertrand I pp. 335–336 (huts as elegant and comfortable as country houses, aviaries, poultry yards, dovecotes, artificial warrens; [n367](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n367/mode/1up), [n368](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n368/mode/1up)); Nicolaÿ pp. 242–243; three officers' huts built for the artillery (Desbrière III p. 435, [n462](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n462/mode/1up)). Medium.
- **Kitchens and smoke.** Confirmed for the position, **corrected** for one attribution. Bertrand I p. 335 puts the officers' lodgings behind the lines and the kitchens "un peu plus en arrière" ([n367](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n367/mode/1up)). "Placed so as to serve each battalion separately" is Nicolaÿ's alone (p. 242). Bertrand adds that the second and third lines were built only as the army grew. The ban on heating at Étaples (p. 108) and the *Antiquity* abstract are as the note says. Added: every division in the prairial XII report has its fuel ("Chauffage") marked good or up to date (Desbrière III pp. 431–434), so firewood was being issued. Each division sent men daily to the "manutention" (16, 16, 10 and 11), but the report does not say where it was; Bertrand I p. 346 has the First Consul inspecting the bakery ("la boulangerie") at Boulogne in November 1803 ([n382](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n382/mode/1up)). Hue's single plume from a building low on the far slope is there; several more plumes rise from the town. Nicolaÿ p. 21 has even the Emperor's cooks working in the open air ([n40](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n40/mode/1up)). Nothing found on chimneys. Medium for the kitchens' position; low for their form.
- **Streets, names, decorations.** Confirmed: Bertrand I p. 335 (cross streets separating the battalions, named after the main victories); Nicolaÿ p. 242 (the list of streets and avenues, the corner signboards, the mosaic paving); Constant I p. 249 (the right camp's streets named after distinguished generals, [n274](https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdeconst01wairuoft/page/n274/mode/1up)); Méneval I pp. 347–348 (inscriptions, heroic or burlesque, on the gables). Clay-and-shell obelisks, columns, pyramids and figures, like fine marble at a short distance: Bertrand I p. 335 and Nicolaÿ pp. 242–243. **Corrected** (attribution): the crosses of the Legion of Honour cut in the turf are Nicolaÿ's (p. 243), not Bertrand's. Medium.

### (b) Where the camps stood, and what showed from the roads

- **Extent: Tour d'Ordre to Wimille, Outreau to Le Portel.** Confirmed: Bertrand I p. 334 ([n366](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n366/mode/1up)); Nicolaÿ p. 241, with the site "a stretch of four kilometres of land along the coast" ([n260](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n260/mode/1up)). Later. Medium.
- **A "front de bandière" of more than a league.** Confirmed as a quotation (Bertrand I pp. 334–335), **corrected** as applied. Bertrand gives the league for the lines of huts in general, and Nicolaÿ's "over four kilometres" (p. 242) is for the three rows as a whole. Neither assigns it to the right camp. The painter list's "front of more than a league" for the right camp is an inference.
- **4.4 km to Wimille, 1.4 km from the Outreau brow to Le Portel.** Confirmed: from the French Wikipedia coordinates I get 4.40 km from the Tour d'Ordre to Wimille, and 1.36 km from the note's brow point (50.7178 N 1.5849 E) to Le Portel. Added: Bertrand II p. 52 runs the left camp from the brow of the hill facing the town to beyond the Petit-Moulin ([n62](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_MLwWAAAAQAAJ/page/n62/mode/1up)), and p. 51 puts the (later) fort of the Petit-Moulin a few hundred paces from the village of Outreau ([n61](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_MLwWAAAAQAAJ/page/n61/mode/1up)). So the left camp also ran south across the plateau towards Outreau, not only west to Le Portel.
- **The column and navy orders of 1804.** Confirmed: Soult's order of 21 September 1804 puts the column between the headquarters at the Tour d'Ordre and the camp of the first division (Nicolaÿ p. 309, [n328](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n328/mode/1up)); the navy's order of 8 November 1804 sends its detachment "to the Plateau of the First Division at the Tour d'Odre" (pp. 310–311). Period, in translation. High.
- **Which division in which camp (the note's inference).** **Corrected**: it is documented, not inferred. The army's situation of 21 May 1804 in Desbrière III p. 428 lists Soult's first division (Saint-Hilaire: 10e légère, 14e, 36e, 43e and 55e de ligne) as "Camp de droite de Boulogne", the second (Vandamme: 24e légère, Tirailleurs du Pô, 4e, 28e, 46e and 57e de ligne) as "Camp à Outreau", the third (Legrand) at Ambleteuse and the fourth (Suchet) at Wimereux ([n455](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n455/mode/1up)). So in 1804 each Boulogne camp held one division of 6,700–7,400 men present, and the fourth division was at Wimereux, not at the northern end of the right camp. Joseph Bonaparte, colonel of the 4e de ligne, had his hut in the left camp (Constant I p. 251), which fits Vandamme's division there. Period. High for 1804.
- **Other camps, and Ostrohove.** Confirmed: Bertrand I p. 336 ([n368](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n368/mode/1up)). Maurice Dupin's letter "Du camp d'Ostrohow, 30 frimaire an XII" is in Sand, *Histoire de ma vie* (Project Gutenberg 39101): a camp laid out "à la romaine", a perfect square, the arriving troops' huts not yet built. Added: in July 1804 Napoleon still asked what it would cost to finish "le camp que devait occuper la division Dupont" so as to camp three regiments there (Corr. IX No. 7869, p. 430, [n442](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede09napouoft/page/n442/mode/1up)). Medium.
- **Which troops in August 1805.** Confirmed: Napoleon to Soult, 13 March 1805, Legrand and Suchet "dans tout l'arrondissement de Boulogne", the reserve and the Guard at Wimereux (Desbrière IV pp. 404–405, [n63](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n63/mode/1up)); the embarkation note of 10 March 1805 (Corr. 8410) giving the four divisions, the Guard and the reserve grenadiers to the Boulogne escadrilles (IV p. 430, [n89](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n89/mode/1up)); the Guard ordered to Boulogne on 7 August and partly held back on the 8th (Corr. XI Nos. 9056 and 9058, pp. 70–71, [n82](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/n82/mode/1up)); Coignet p. 113, camped by the port of Ambleteuse with Oudinot's 12,000 grenadiers above ([n147](https://archive.org/details/lescahiersducapi00coig/page/n147/mode/1up)). The figure 45,907 is in the table of each camp's strength in Desbrière IV p. 433 ([n92](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n92/mode/1up)); the OCR of the table is too broken for me to confirm which column it heads. Where four divisions were hutted around Boulogne in summer 1805, two to a camp or partly at Ostrohove, Herquelingue and the ramparts, was not found. Medium.
- **Heights from period writers.** Confirmed: Henry's 55 m for the cliff "on which the tower stood" (Nicolaÿ p. 45, [n64](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n64/mode/1up)); Constant I pp. 244–245 (all four camps on a very high cliff) and p. 251 (the left camp at about the same height as the right).
- **Heights from the terrain model.** Confirmed in substance, with small corrections (my own 25 m grid of the IGN RGE ALTI; see Recomputations). The Wikipedia point of the Tour d'Ordre lies at 47 m on the brow; the cliff top just inland stands at 57–61 m, above a beach at 3–8 m. Towards the column the ground reaches 70 m at 0.45 km (not 0.8 km), 77 m at 0.9 km, 83 m at 1.2 km and 92 m at the column, which is **1.8 km** from the Tour d'Ordre, not 2 km. Due east the plateau reaches 72 m at 0.5 km and falls to 31 m at 1.0 km before climbing to 100 m at 2.0 km. North of the Tour d'Ordre, the Terlincthun hollow bottoms at 23–28 m about 1 km north, and the coastal rise at 50.748 N reaches 60–71 m. The column's ground is 91.6 m; Bertrand II p. 174 does give its summit as 143.11 m above mean sea level ([n184](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_MLwWAAAAQAAJ/page/n184/mode/1up)). The Outreau brow climbs from the harbour flats (5–8 m) to 48–59 m within about 0.3 km, and along 1.595 E the plateau then stays at 54–63 m for 1.7 km. **Corrected** ("level"): from west to east the plateau is tilted, rising gently from 30–40 m above Le Portel to 60–64 m at 1.59–1.60 E (section at 50.710 N). That tilt matters for what the roads see (below). High for the modern ground.
- **Distance of the lines from the cliff edge.** Confirmed: Bertrand II p. 52 (above); Bertrand I p. 344, the hut "sur le bord de la falaise" ([n380](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n380/mode/1up)); Nicolaÿ p. 49, set back for fear of landslips ([n68](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n68/mode/1up)). On the full-size scan of Lewis's drawing the batteries stand on the cliff edges with the nearest rows of huts a little way up the slopes behind them. In Hue's painting a row of huts stands part way up the far slope, with a long line along its crest. Added: Constant I p. 249 has the right camp's cliff "hérissée de batteries" from Boulogne to Ambleteuse, more than two leagues ([n274](https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdeconst01wairuoft/page/n274/mode/1up)). Low, as the note says.
- **Angles from the note's viewpoint (50.733 N 1.548 E, eye 3 m).** Confirmed by my own ray-marching (see Recomputations). The Tour d'Ordre point stands 0.76° above the sea horizon at 92°; the column site 1.04° at 80°, 5.0 km; the Outreau brow point 0.92° at 123°, 3.1 km; the belfry's foot 0.64° at 101° (the note says 99°), 4.7 km. Hut sizes check: 10 m at 4 km is 0.14°, a 4 m ridge 0.06°, and 32.5 m at 3.5 km 0.53°. High.
- **The skyline.** Confirmed, with Mont Lambert **corrected**. From the note's viewpoint the land skyline runs at 0.6–1.2° above the sea horizon from bearing 45° to 165°. Mont Lambert lies inside my grid; it stands at 1.41°, not the inferred 1.3°. From the town note's camera A (50.7300 N 1.5650 E) the skyline is 0.9–1.7°, and from camera B (50.7300 N 1.5780 E) 1.0–2.6°. There the right camp's own plateau is the skyline at bearings 60–90°, 2.4° up at 1.6 km.
- **What the roads see of the right camp.** Confirmed, with detail. From all three viewpoints the cliff top and its first 0.3–0.5 km inland are in full view. Between about 50.737 and 50.747 N the broad seaward slope rising to 85–92 m is in view up to about the column's longitude, 1.6–2.4 km from the shore, from camera A and the note's viewpoint. Much of that slope is seen so obliquely that the ground is hidden by up to 3 m and only hut roofs would show. Camera B, closer in, loses everything beyond the first 0.3–0.5 km behind the cliff. At the Tour d'Ordre's own latitude the plateau shows for 0.45 km (camera A) to 1.0 km (the note's viewpoint), then the valley behind the town drops out of sight. North of about 50.749 N (beyond the rise over La Crèche), and all the way to Wimille, only a strip 0.1–0.4 km deep along the coast is in view. Wimille itself is hidden by 80–120 m. High for the geometry; medium as a picture of 1805, since the cliff edge has retreated and the hut lines are not known.
- **What the roads see of the left camp.** **Corrected** (it depends on the camera). From cameras A and B, 1–2 km off the harbour mouth, only a strip along the north-western brow shows, roughly 1.577–1.588 E at 50.715–50.722 N. The rest of the plateau, and all of its eastern half above the Liane, is hidden by its own edge, as the note says. From the note's own viewpoint, 3.5 km out and further west, the western half of the plateau tilts towards the camera. It is in view, mostly at grazing angles, for up to 2 km south of the brow at 1.585 E and from 1.585 to 1.60 E at 50.710 N. The eastern half (1.590–1.600 E north of 50.712 N) stays hidden from every viewpoint. So the left camp shows as a line on the brow from close in, and as a shallow band of roofs climbing the western plateau from further out. High for the geometry; medium as a picture of 1805.
- **Lewis's drawing is not to vertical scale.** Added. On the 3,800 px scan the land rises about 800 px from the beach to the skyline across a width of about 3,700 px. For a view of 70–90° of coast that is ten times or more the true 1–2.5°. Lewis spreads the rows out so that they can be counted. A true view packs them into a thin band above the cliffs, overlapping one another. Inference from the image and the angles. Medium.

### (c) Napoleon's hut and its neighbours

- **Who describes it.** Confirmed. Constant I p. 245 names his informant, Lecat de Rue, nephew of the builder Sordi and in 1805 aide-de-camp on Soult's staff ([n270](https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdeconst01wairuoft/page/n270/mode/1up)). Bertrand I pp. 344–345 and the plan legend; Méneval I p. 346 ([n382](https://archive.org/details/mmoirespourser01mnuoft/page/n382/mode/1up)). Nicolaÿ p. 16 lists his sources: Constant, Marco de Saint-Hilaire, Bertrand, Hautefeuille and Bernard, and Morand ([n35](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n35/mode/1up)).
- **Added: the hut already stood in November 1803.** On 12 brumaire XII (4 November 1803) the First Consul, back from the works after midnight, "se retira dans sa barraque", and Bertrand describes it at that point (I p. 344, [n380](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n380/mode/1up)). Napoleon dated a note "A la baraque de la Tour d'Ordre" on 27 August 1804 (Corr. IX No. 7964) and a letter "La Tour d'Ordre" on 12 August 1805 (Corr. XI No. 9068, p. 78, [n90](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/n90/mode/1up)). So it was in use in the very week of the scene. Period. High.
- **Wood, planks, painted grey-white.** Confirmed: Constant I p. 245 ("peintes en gris blanc"); Bertrand I p. 345 ("Faite en bois peint en gris", [n381](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n381/mode/1up)); Nicolaÿ p. 17 ("light grey", [n36](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n36/mode/1up)). Period and later. High.
- **Plan and size.** Confirmed: 100 × 22½ French feet, the end looking to the port "terminée par une rotonde de trente pieds de diamètre, formant la salle du conseil" (Bertrand I p. 345); 32.5 × 7.3 m and 9.7 m at 0.3248 m to the foot. Nicolaÿ p. 17 repeats the same figures, and Louis Nicolaÿ's 40 m of 1864 is on p. 17. **Corrected** (painter list): Constant's ends are "pavillons de forme semi-circulaire" (p. 245), and the one facing the sea held three rooms and a corridor (council room, bedroom, telescope room). So "a semicircular room at each end" should read "a semicircular end pavilion at each end, the seaward one holding the council room". Constant's wording ("à chaque extrémité, deux pavillons") could even be read as two at each end; Nicolaÿ reads it as one at each end. Medium for the plan and dimensions.
- **Windows, paling, lamps.** Confirmed: Constant I p. 245 has the windows "placées latéralement", a surround closed by a wooden lattice ("grillage en bois"), and outside lamps ("réverbères") placed four feet apart. Nicolaÿ p. 18 adds "after dark" ([n37](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n37/mode/1up)). A lamp every 1.3 m round an enclosure of 80 m or more would mean 60 or more lamps, so the four feet may be a slip; the ring of lights at dusk stands either way. Medium for the lamps' spacing.
- **Built in 48 hours on a brick footing; Sordi; single storey.** Confirmed: Nicolaÿ p. 17 (citing Louis Nicolaÿ and Florentin Lefils), p. 17 and Constant p. 245 for Sordi, p. 28 for the single row of windows and Longwood ([n47](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n47/mode/1up)). Added: Sordi's bill was 50,000 francs, cut to 30,000 (p. 22). Later. Medium.
- **Interior.** Confirmed: Constant I pp. 245–248 ([n271](https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdeconst01wairuoft/page/n271/mode/1up)). Not visible from the sea.
- **Gardens.** Confirmed: Nicolaÿ pp. 59–60 ([n78](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n78/mode/1up)). Nicolaÿ himself calls the grounds "somewhat barren". Low.
- **Where.** Confirmed: Bertrand I pp. 344–345 (on the edge of the cliff, inside the 1544 English works round the lighthouse, on a small mound); legend no. 34 ("Barraque de Napoléon, placée dans un des anciens bastions de la tour d'Ordre ou de Caligula", [n27](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n27/mode/1up)); Nicolaÿ pp. 45 and 49 (the mound as the old curtain, set back from the edge); the tower's fall in 1644 on p. 47. Added: the same legend marks a "Barraque des ministres" (35) and a "Barraque de l'amiral" (36) separately. **Corrected** (what the 0.9 km measures). I measured the 1804 plan myself at full resolution against its own scale bar (0.32 m per pixel). The label "TOUR D'ODRE" sits in a field between two roads, with no building drawn. The root of the Jetée Pidou lies about 0.40 km from it, almost due south (bearing 186°): close to the note's 370 m. The 0.9 km "a little north of west" is the distance to the **Fort-en-Bois** at the outer end of the long work on the north side of the channel (0.92 km at about 284°). The head of the Jetée de l'Ouest is only about 0.57 km away to the south-west (about 232°). The harbour mouth therefore lies between west and south-west. Medium for the metres.
- **Orientation (the note's inference).** Supported, with a stronger argument than the note gives. Constant I p. 246: one entered the council room by a corridor, and in that corridor, "à droite", was the bedroom, lit by a window that looked out onto the right camp with the sea on the left ([n271](https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdeconst01wairuoft/page/n271/mode/1up)). If the rotunda is at the western, seaward end, someone walking along the corridor towards it faces west. The bedroom is then on the north side, and a north window looks across the right camp (which runs north and north-east) with the sea on the left. Any orientation with the rotunda facing south (towards the town and channel) puts the sea in front of that window, not to the left. The gardens on the south side (Nicolaÿ p. 59) fit a long side facing south. So: long axis about east–west, rotunda at the west end facing the roads. From cameras A, B and the note's viewpoint (bearings 82–92°) the pavilion is seen almost end-on. Inference from three period statements and the plan. Medium (the note says low).
- **Could it be picked out?** Confirmed. The Tour d'Ordre point is in full view from all three cameras (see Recomputations); the rotunda end spans 0.16–0.41° and the height of about 4 m 0.07–0.17°, depending on the camera. Added on views from the sea: in Lewis's drawing a long, low, pale roof shows behind the parapet of the cliff-top battery just north of the harbour, and a row of four small white gabled buildings with a dark end stands on the slope further left. Neither is keyed. Either might be the headquarters huts, the battery's own buildings or the "ferme d'Ordre" that Bertrand names in 1809 (I p. 426). Low.
- **The admiral's hut.** Confirmed: Constant I p. 248 ("les mêmes dispositions que celle de l'empereur, mais en petit", [n273](https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdeconst01wairuoft/page/n273/mode/1up)); Nicolaÿ p. 21 and the Enclos de l'Amiral on p. 16 n. Bruix died on 27 ventôse XIII (Nicolaÿ p. 186, [n205](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n205/mode/1up)), after falling ill on the day he left his pavilion for the coronation. Lacrosse was "commandant la flottille à Boulogne" on 6 May 1805 (Desbrière IV p. 409, [n68](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat02histgoog/page/n68/mode/1up)). Added: in September 1811 the admiral's hut still stood, with a terrace on which Napoleon walked for an afternoon (Bertrand I p. 438). High for Bruix's death; medium for the hut's use in 1805.
- **The signal post.** Confirmed for the place: Constant I p. 248 ("A côté de cette baraque", the admiral's, "le sémaphore des signaux, sorte de télégraphe maritime"); Nicolaÿ p. 22 (between the two pavilions) and p. 29 (the signalling terrace and the order of 27 germinal XII on the flagstaffs of La Crèche and the Mont du Couple, [n48](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n48/mode/1up)). **Corrected** (page and scope of Bruix's order). The order of 15 prairial XII begins as a footnote on Desbrière III p. 537. The flag paragraph is on **p. 539** ([n570](https://archive.org/details/projetsettentat00histgoog/page/n570/mode/1up)), and it recalls the péniches, boats, "bateaux de pêche" and other small craft out for exercise or fishing, not all craft. **Caution** on the 1809 semaphore (Bertrand I p. 426, [n464](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n464/mode/1up)). France's chain of coastal semaphores, masts with jointed arms on Depillon's system, was installed in July–September 1806 after a ministerial circular of April 1806 (F. Cabane's slides, Ifremer 2008, which I opened; a French defence ministry page giving 1806, seen only in a search summary). So the 1809 semaphore may be a new post of 1806 and says nothing about the form of the 1805 mast. For August 1805: a tall flagstaff by the admiral's hut, with yards for flag hoists, is the safe reading. High for a signal mast; low for arms.
- **Soult's and Decrès's huts.** Confirmed: Constant I pp. 248–249 ("en forme de hutte de sauvage, couverte en chaume jusqu'à terre et vitrée par le haut", rooms entered by a door leading down, as if buried, the main room round; [n274](https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdeconst01wairuoft/page/n274/mode/1up)); Decrès's "faite et distribuée" like it (p. 249). Nicolaÿ p. 22 ([n41](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n41/mode/1up)) adds "less spacious and commodious" and that it looked from a distance "like an enormous extinguisher". Méneval I p. 346 has the admiral's and the navy minister's huts a short distance from the Emperor's. Added: the prairial XII report says the works the minister had asked for at the Tour d'Ordre were finished by June 1804 (Desbrière III p. 436). For the painter: a conical or domed thatch reaching the ground, with a glazed lantern or skylight at the top. High for the thatch to the ground; medium for the cone.
- **The road and the zigzag path.** Confirmed: Constant I p. 249 (the only road, from the rue des Vieillards, across the cliff top between the Emperor's hut and those of Bruix, Soult and Decrès); p. 250 (a path of stones held together with iron cramps, built in three days and nights, [n275](https://archive.org/details/mmoiresdeconst01wairuoft/page/n275/mode/1up)); Nicolaÿ pp. 30–31 ([n49](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n49/mode/1up)). Constant lists the buildings in this order: the Emperor's hut, the admiral's, the semaphore beside it, "un peu plus loin" the Tour d'Ordre battery, Soult's hut "A droite" of the battery, Decrès's last. Read together with the road passing between the Emperor's hut and the other three, this puts the Emperor's hut on the cliff side of the road and the others inland of it. Inference. Medium.
- **The Tour d'Ordre battery.** Confirmed: Constant I p. 248 (six mortars, six howitzers, twelve 24-pounders); Desbrière III p. 435 (the low battery of the Tour d'Ordre). Added: Méneval I p. 346 has mortars "d'un calibre inusité" there, ranging 3,000 toises.
- **After 1805.** Confirmed with one **correction**. Still standing in 1808 and only in part in 1810: Nicolaÿ p. 23 ([n42](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n42/mode/1up)). Not kept up and beginning to fall into ruin in 1809: Bertrand I p. 424 ([n462](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n462/mode/1up)). "Car la sienne n'existait plus" (September 1811) is on Bertrand I **p. 438** ([n476](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n476/mode/1up)), not p. 428, and Nicolaÿ p. 23 does not mention 1811. The outline still "parfaitement reconnaissable" in 1829: Bertrand II p. 176 ([n186](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_MLwWAAAAQAAJ/page/n186/mode/1up)). The statue of 1856 and its fall in 1894: Nicolaÿ pp. 26–27.

### (d) Tents

- **Hut camps in use until the army marched.** Confirmed, with the dates **corrected**. The order of 28 August 1803 (Desbrière III p. 194) and Corr. XI No. 9137 (pp. 142–144) are as the note says. No. 9137 is dated 26 August 1805. It sets the first divisions of each corps marching on 10 fructidor (28 August), the second on the 12th (30 August) and the third on the 13th (31 August) ([n156](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/n156/mode/1up)). No. 9135 of the day before (p. 140) wanted the countermarch to begin on 9 fructidor (27 August) ([n154](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede11napouoft/page/n154/mode/1up)). So the order came on 26 August and the army left on 27–31 August. Added: Bertrand I p. 405 says the camps, "après avoir été habités pendant deux ans", were then lived in by the flotilla's seamen ([n443](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n443/mode/1up)). Period and later. High.
- **Tents or bivouac before the huts, 1803.** Confirmed: Bertrand I p. 334 (bivouac); Nicolaÿ p. 240 ("pitch their tents") and p. 241 ("obliged to bivouac for several weeks"). Medium.
- **Tents as a plan (March and July 1804).** Confirmed: Corr. IX No. 7609, p. 282, "avec des tentes, les faire placer où nous voudrons" once the boats had arrived and the expedition drew near ([n294](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede09napouoft/page/n294/mode/1up)). Added: the same letter tells Ney not to change his camps "parce que cela vous coûterait du bois et des transports", which shows that a move meant building new huts. Corr. IX No. 7869 (26 July 1804, p. 430) asks whether Boulogne had the tents to camp Junot's ten battalions of reserve grenadiers near the circular basin ([n442](https://archive.org/details/correspondancede09napouoft/page/n442/mode/1up)). Whether that camp was pitched: not found by me either. Medium.
- **Tents at the ceremony of 16 August 1804.** Confirmed: the *Journal des Débats* report, Nicolaÿ p. 299, "the tents reserved for the ladies" behind the crowd ([n318](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n318/mode/1up)). In Hennequin's painting (Commons copy, 1,885 px) several white wall tents and a marquee stand on the slope at the right edge of the crowd. High.
- **Tents as campaign kit.** Confirmed: Corr. XI No. 9137, p. 142, camp equipment from Metz to Strasbourg so that by 1 vendémiaire there would be "de quoi tenter 80,000 hommes". The inference that the Boulogne army had no tents of its own with it is fair. Period. High.
- **The Guard in August 1805.** **Corrected** (dating). Coignet p. 113 says the Guard was camped at the port of Ambleteuse with Oudinot's 12,000 grenadiers above, and served afloat on a line of 200 péniches, sleeping in hammocks on board ([n147](https://archive.org/details/lescahiersducapi00coig/page/n147/mode/1up)). His account is undated, and on p. 115 Napoleon in the same episode is "le Consul". Its fit to August 1805 rests on Oudinot (who led the reserve grenadiers in 1805) and on the order of 7 August 1805, not on Coignet. What the Guard lived in is not said. Low.
- **Conclusion: no tents in the hut lines.** Confirmed. Nothing I opened puts a tent in the Boulogne right or left camp in summer 1805. High for no tents in the hut lines; medium that none stood anywhere on the heights.

### (e) Other things on the heights

- **The column: order, site, first stone, materials.** Confirmed: Soult's order of 21 September 1804 (Nicolaÿ p. 309); the site fixed on 1 vendémiaire XIII "à deux cents mètres de la route de Calais, dans la direction du mont Lambert au fort de la Crèche" (Bertrand I p. 383, [n419](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n419/mode/1up)); the first stone on 18 brumaire XIII (9 November 1804), foundations dug down to the rock, and "Les travaux prirent aussitôt un grand degré d'activité" (p. 390, [n426](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n426/mode/1up)); foundations of rock from the cliffs and shore, Marquise marbles (brown "stinkal" for the pedestal, ash-grey for the shaft), 150 feet in all (p. 391, [n427](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n427/mode/1up)); Nicolaÿ pp. 311–312 (6 m foundations, and the translator's "6 November", [n331](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n331/mode/1up)). Site 50.7411 N 1.6183 E, ground 91.6 m (my grid). **Corrected**: it stands 1.8 km from the Tour d'Ordre point, not 2 km. High.
- **The column's height: 19 m by 1814, 20 m by 1811.** Confirmed: Bertrand II p. 174 (19 m when the work was suspended) and Nicolaÿ p. 313 (19 m in 1814). English Wikipedia's 20 m at the closure of 3 December 1811 has no citation, as the note says; the same article misdates the first stone to 9 *October* 1804.
- **The 1841 pamphlet.** **Corrected** (who said what). The Wikisource text does have the works going slowly and with many interruptions until September 1811, and the Restoration finding the column "à peine sorti de terre", with all its marble on site and a scaffold of nearly 180 feet. But the prefect is not quoted with "50 feet of 150". The municipal petition of December 1817 says the column "n'est qu'au tiers de sa hauteur". The "50 pieds" of a planned 150 comes from an unsigned proposal to the departmental council of June 1819. The prefect is only said to have pressed for completion. Later. Medium.
- **The column in August 1805 (inference).** Confirmed as inference, with the visibility added. Nothing gives its height in August 1805, nine months after the first stone. My recomputation puts the site's ground 2 m below the sightline from the note's viewpoint, 6 m below from camera A and 22 m below from camera B. So foundations, low courses and sheds would not show at all from close in. Scaffolding or a crane over about 6 m might just show from camera A, against the skyline at 72°, 4.0 km off. Low.
- **The Legion of Honour site.** Confirmed for the sources: the *Journal des Débats* (the hollow between Moulin Hubert and Terlincthun, an amphitheatre opening towards the cliffs) and the *Journal de Paris* (close to the pavilion; the throne on a mound, its platform 16 feet square and 8 feet high), Nicolaÿ pp. 298 and 302 ([n317](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n317/mode/1up), [n321](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n321/mode/1up)); the ground under the plough by 1809, bought on 29 November 1809, and on 3 December a block of the column's marble, 90 × 80 cm, set on a masonry base at the throne's place (Bertrand I pp. 424–425, [n463](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n463/mode/1up)); destroyed in 1815–16 (Bertrand I p. 427) and replaced in 1830 (Nicolaÿ p. 308). **Refuted** ("hidden from the roads"). From all three cameras the ground at the memorial (50.7429 N 1.5992 E, 37 m) is only 0.3–0.6 m below the sightline. The hollow opens to the sea, and a mound or a platform 8 feet high would be just in view. The eyewitness of 1804 saw "the entrance to the harbour, and part of the roadstead" from the throne (Nicolaÿ p. 299). It is no landmark either way: a mound of about 3 m at 2.8 km spans 0.06°. Medium.
- **Windmills.** **Corrected** (count). On the full-size scan of Lewis's drawing I count **six** post mills, with dark bodies and four sails: one on the crest of a hill on the left, two pairs part way up the hills left of the town, and a faint one on the far skyline. Place names confirmed: Moulin Hubert (Nicolaÿ p. 298); the Petit-Moulin (Bertrand II pp. 51–52); the Moulin-à-l'Huile, whose fort was built in 1806 (Bertrand II p. 173, [n183](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_MLwWAAAAQAAJ/page/n183/mode/1up)); the Moulin-Wiber of 1618 (Nicolaÿ p. 49). Added: a "mont des Moulins" near Wicardenne (Bertrand I p. 407). Medium.
- **Telegraphs.** Confirmed: the engraving of year XII, "Le tachygraphe de Chappe sur le beffroi de Boulogne" (Nicolaÿ p. 33, [n52](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n52/mode/1up)); Soult's telegram of 12 June 1804 "envoyé par télégraphe Chappe" (Archives nationales AE II 2302, Commons description). I read Lewis's key myself: "32 The Telegraph" and "34 The Telegraph on Telegraph Hill". On the scan, a mast with a cross-arm and small dark vanes at its head rises from the top of a spire over the town. A second mast of the same kind, with a small hut beside it, stands on the far skyline right of the town, which is probably no. 34. French Wikipedia on the belfry relay was not opened. Medium.
- **Signal masts and flags.** Confirmed: the flagstaffs of La Crèche and the Mont du Couple repeating the admiral's signals from the Tour d'Ordre (Nicolaÿ p. 29). On the scan, tricolours fly from flagstaffs on most of the cliff-top batteries. High for flags on the batteries; low for arms on the 1805 signal mast (see (c)).
- **Field works.** **Corrected** (date). Bertrand's legend says nos. 9–12 of the entrenched camp were "construit en 1805 pour défendre la ville et le port" ([n26](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n26/mode/1up)). His text (I pp. 406–408) has Napoleon order the works before leaving: sixteen earthworks, mostly bastions with palisaded gorges, built "avec une étonnante rapidité" by the flotilla's crews, four of them on the left camp's plateau and four at the start of the right camp's plateau up to the Tour d'Ordre. A second line of six forts followed, among them the Mont de Couple, the Petit-Moulin, the Ferme du Renard and Mont Lambert ([n444](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n444/mode/1up), [n445](https://archive.org/details/prcisdelhistoir01bertgoog/page/n445/mode/1up)). Bertrand II p. 51 dates the forts of the Renard and the Petit-Moulin to the Austerlitz campaign. So the entrenched camp belongs after the army left: **leave it out of an August 1805 view**. What the 1804 plan shows, a bastioned "Batterie de la Tour d'Odre" north of the Tour d'Ordre and a line running east, is in solid line, so of 1804, as the note says. Medium.
- **The Tour d'Ordre itself.** Confirmed: Nicolaÿ pp. 44–45 (stumps of 16th-century red brick works on the cliff top, rubble on the beach) and p. 47 (the fall of 1640–44). Lewis shows no tower there. High.

## Recomputations

**Method (mine, independent of the note's).** The terrain is the IGN RGE ALTI bare-earth model, fetched from the Géoplateforme altimetry service on 13 November 2026. It is the same model the note used, but on my own 25 m grid: 356 × 339 points over 50.695–50.775 N and 1.545–1.665 E, which takes in Mont Lambert. Between grid points I interpolate bilinearly. For each target I march along the ray from the camera in 8–10 m steps, with the camera's eye 3 m above the model's sea level. Earth curvature is included with refraction k = 0.13 (a drop of d²(1 − k)/2R). A point is in view if its sightline is steeper than every point in between. Where it is not, I record how far its ground lies below the grazing sightline. I sort the ground into three classes: in view; hidden by up to 3 m, so that only a hut's roof would show; and hidden. The sea horizon dips 0.052° at a 3 m eye. As a cross-check on the heights I used a second model, EU-DEM 25 m (via OpenTopoData). It gives 93.0 m at the column (IGN 91.6 m) and 38.1 m at the Tour d'Ordre point (IGN 46.7 m; the coarser model smooths the cliff edge). Cameras: the note's viewpoint N (50.7330 N 1.5480 E) and the town note's cameras A (50.7300 N 1.5650 E, about 2 km off the harbour mouth) and B (50.7300 N 1.5780 E, about 1.1 km off). Scripts and outputs are kept outside the repository (`.claude/s26/history/dem/`).

**Angles above the sea horizon, distance and bearing (true).**
- Tour d'Ordre point (47 m; the cliff top just inland 57–61 m): N 0.76° at 3.47 km, 92°; A 1.14° at 2.28 km, 85°; B 1.88° at 1.37 km, 82°.
- Column site (91.6 m): N 1.04° at 5.04 km, 80°, ground hidden by 2.1 m; A 1.32° at 3.96 km, 72°, hidden by 5.8 m; B 1.68° at 3.10 km, 67°, hidden by 22 m. So from the cameras near the harbour mouth a stump of a few metres at the column would not show at all.
- Outreau brow point (50.7178 N 1.5849 E, 50.5 m): N 0.92° at 3.10 km, 123°; A 1.44° at 1.95 km, 134°; B 1.93° at 1.44 km, 160°.
- Belfry's foot (52 m): N 0.64° at 4.68 km, 101°; A 0.86° at 3.44 km, 99°; B 1.16° at 2.54 km, 103°. The foot is at the edge of view from all three, so the town on its spur is seen against the hills behind it.
- Mont Lambert (186 m): N 1.42° at 7.49 km; A 1.71° at 6.24 km; B 1.99° at 5.35 km.
- Legion of Honour memorial (50.7429 N 1.5992 E, 37 m): N 0.55°, A 0.73°, B 0.98°. Its ground is hidden by only 0.3–0.6 m, so the mouth of the hollow, which opens to the sea, is just in sight. The 1804 eyewitness who saw "the entrance to the harbour, and part of the roadstead" from the throne (*Journal des Débats*, Nicolaÿ p. 299, [n318](https://archive.org/details/napoleonatboulog00nicouoft/page/n318/mode/1up)) agrees.

**The skyline.** From N it runs at 0.6–1.2° above the sea horizon between bearings 45° and 165°, rising to 1.41° at Mont Lambert (105°). From A it runs at 0.9–1.7°, and from B at 1.0–2.6°. From B the right camp's own plateau edge forms the skyline at bearings 60–90°, up to 2.6° at 1.6 km.

**The right camp's band.** Along the line from the Tour d'Ordre to the column, ground or roofs stay in view for 1.75 km from N. From A only the first 0.25 km is in full view, and roofs only after that to 1.45 km. From B nothing shows beyond 0.25 km. Seen from A, everything from the cliff top (1.1°) to the crest of the slope (1.35–1.48°) fits in **about 0.3–0.4° of height**. From N the band runs from 0.76° to about 1.0–1.1°. Rows of huts 50–100 m apart up that slope would stack 0.01–0.03° apart: closer than a hut's own height of about 0.04–0.06°. So from the roads the rows overlap into a dense, striped texture above the cliff rather than separate lines.

**The left camp.** In north–south sections across the plateau, cameras A and B see only 0.3–0.6 km of the brow at 1.580–1.585 E, and nothing at 1.590–1.605 E. From N the section at 1.585 E is in view, or shows roofs, for about 2 km south of the brow, and the one at 1.590 E from 1.3 to 2.5 km south. The plateau's surface slopes gently down to the west, so it faces a camera out to the west and turns its back on one off the harbour mouth. That is why the camera matters.

**The pavilion's angular size.** At 32.5 × 7.3 m with a 9.7 m rotunda, and a height of about 4 m (my assumption), it is 0.82° long side-on, 0.24° end-on and 0.10° tall from A. From B the same figures are 1.36°, 0.41° and 0.17°. From N they are 0.54°, 0.16° and 0.07°.

**Lewis's vertical scale.** On the 3,800 × 3,286 px TIFF the land occupies about 800 px of height across about 3,700 px of width. The angles above give the true height of the land as 1–2.5° over a coast of 70–90°. That makes Lewis's vertical exaggeration roughly ten times.

High for the geometry on the modern ground. Medium for 1805: the cliff edge has retreated, the shore and harbour have been rebuilt, and the hut lines are not known.

## Additional facts found

1. **Two hut camps were ordered from the start.** The order of 28 August 1803 already calls for "deux camps en baraques à Boulogne : l'un sur la droite, l'autre sur la gauche du port" (Desbrière III p. 193). Period. High.
2. **Who lived where in 1804.** In the situation of 21 May 1804 the right camp held Saint-Hilaire's division (10e légère, 14e, 36e, 43e and 55e de ligne; 7,368 present) and the camp at Outreau held Vandamme's (24e légère, Tirailleurs du Pô, 4e, 28e, 46e and 57e de ligne; 6,703 present). Legrand was at Ambleteuse and Suchet at Wimereux (Desbrière III p. 428). Period. High.
3. **The camps kept their names after the army left.** The departure order of 26 August 1805 sends the three Italian regiments "au camp de gauche" and camps one senior artillery officer in the right camp of Boulogne and one in the left (Corr. XI No. 9137, p. 144). The flotilla's seamen then lived in the camps (Bertrand I p. 405). By 1809 they were far from the "habitations charmantes" of 1803–04 (I p. 424). Period and later.
4. **The first garrison, autumn 1803.** Eight demi-brigades of line infantry, two of light infantry and twenty companies of gunners formed the nucleus (Bertrand I p. 334). Later.
5. **The local way of building.** Rural houses of the Boulonnais were of stone, brick or timber frame daubed with clay, and those of Équihen of earth (Bertrand II p. 51). This is the "peasant manner" in which the huts' mud walls were built: clay-coloured walls under thatch. Later.
6. **The right camp's plain ran from the Tour d'Ordre to the column.** Bertrand describes "le plateau de la barraque Napoléon, la plaine du camp de droite, terminée par la Colonne" (II p. 53, [n63](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_MLwWAAAAQAAJ/page/n63/mode/1up)) and places the right camp's plateau west of the column (II p. 175, [n185](https://archive.org/details/bub_gb_MLwWAAAAQAAJ/page/n185/mode/1up)). That is the very slope the roads see best (Recomputations). Later. Medium.
7. **The semicircular basin lay below the left camp.** Constant puts the basin "Au bas de ce camp [de gauche] et de la falaise" (I p. 251), and the 1804 plan draws the Bassin on the left bank of the channel, by the Arsenal and the Fort du Châtillon. Period. Medium.
8. **What the throne saw.** The *Journal des Débats* has the Emperor see from the throne the two camps, the batteries, the harbour entrance and part of the roadstead to his right, and Wimereux and the English coast to his left (Nicolaÿ pp. 298–299). The throne therefore faced inland, with its back to the sea. Period, in translation.
9. **Lewis's key, items 27–39** (read on the scan): 27 Pile Battery; 28 Bomb Point; 29 Batteries; 30 Pier Heads; 31 the flotilla in the harbour; 32 the Telegraph; 33 the Encampment; 34 the Telegraph on Telegraph Hill; 35 an admiral's flag in the harbour; 36 the flotilla; 37 the praam bearing the admiral's flag; 38 a battery of guns on the pier head; 39 the lighthouse. Items 1–26 are the British ships and boats, among them "12 Tartarus Bomb" and "25 Tartarus's Boat". Period.
10. **Hue's far slope** (Commons copy): seen from the Tour d'Ordre side, the Outreau slope shows a regular row of pale gabled huts part way up, a line of small pale huts along the crest, flagstaffs with tricolours on the crest, a zigzag road, long low sheds at the foot and the dark cliff at the brow's seaward end, with the British squadron in the offing. Period image. Medium.
11. **Fires.** In December 1803 Maurice Dupin wrote from Ostrohove that there were no good fires to dry by (Sand, *Histoire de ma vie*). The Emperor's own cooks worked in the open air (Nicolaÿ p. 21). Period and later.
12. **The Tour d'Ordre works were finished by June 1804.** The works the minister had asked for at the Tour d'Ordre were "achevés", and four new guard-houses on the south coast were being given doors and windows (Desbrière III p. 436). Period.
