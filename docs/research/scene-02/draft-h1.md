### Dates and conventions

- **Civil and nautical days.** Dates here are civil, midnight to midnight. British logs of 1805 kept the nautical day, which began at noon of the civil day before, so a log's afternoon of "the 10th" is the civil 9th. Nicolas prints the Victory's log in civil order and corrects it where he noticed ("March 27th [i.e. 26th]", Nicolas VI 374); the Sally's "Sunday 16th June, P.M. 7" is civil Saturday 15 June (Nicolas VI 474). James and Clowes give some British events by the log: Gibraltar 7 May (civil 6), Lagos 10 May (9), Knight's arrival 12 May (11), Antigua 13 June (12), Tetuan "24th at noon" (23). High.
- **The Republican calendar.** French documents are dated by it. Year XIII began on 23 September 1804 and year XIV on 23 September 1805. 1 germinal XIII is 22 March 1805, 1 floréal 21 April, 1 prairial 21 May, 1 messidor 20 June, 1 thermidor 20 July and 1 fructidor 19 August, so 9 germinal is 30 March, 24 floréal 14 May, 16 prairial 5 June, 14 thermidor 2 August and 27 thermidor 15 August; 29 vendémiaire XIV is 21 October. High (computed; Desbrière's own glosses agree).
- **James's one-day shift.** James converted Republican dates himself and came out a day early. Only the dates he took from French documents are affected: Toulon 29 March (for 30), Cartagena 6 April (7), Gibraltar and Cádiz 8 April (9), under way "on the 9th" (10), Martinique sighted 12 May and reached 13 May (13, 14), Martinique left 4 June (5), Vigo left 30 July (31). Clowes copies them. His dates from British reports stand (San Rafael at Martinique 16 May, Diamond Rock 29 May to 2 June, the convoy 8 June, Corvo 30 June, the gale 9 July). Two are two days early (Vigo reached 26 July for 28, out of Ferrol 11 August for 13), and his A Coruña on 1 August follows British reports. No James date can be corrected mechanically (`research/scene-02/a-h1ac.md`, H1-02b). High.
- **The Atlas's tables.** Desbrière prints the 74 Atlas's noon positions in two tables. In the first (Toulon to Cádiz) a row labelled N germinal gives the noon at the end of that day, so "18 germinal", the Strait, is 9 April; the frigates' sightings and Orde's letters confirm it. Whether the second (the crossing) follows the same rule is disputed, so the crossing gets no day marks (`research/scene-02/a-h1ac.md`, H1-07). His "7 h. 45 du matin" for the anchoring off Cádiz is a slip for the evening. High for the first table, low for the second.
- **Positions, leagues and times.** French longitudes, reckoned from Paris (2°20′14″ E of Greenwich), are converted to Greenwich. A sea league is 3 nm. Mid-ocean positions are dead reckoning: at the Martinique landfall the French reckoning was 36 leagues too far east, at Finisterre about 2° too far west. Times are ship's times as logged (local apparent time). High.
- **Bearings.** Logs and letters give bearings by compass unless they say "true". The variation in 1805 (NOAA's gufm1 model) was about 20° W off Toulon, 18½° W south of Sardinia, 21° W off Cádiz, Cape St Vincent and São Miguel, 24° W at the Finisterre action and 25° W off Ushant, but 2½–3° E in the Lesser Antilles; with west variation, true = magnetic − variation. Positions worked from bearings say which reading they use. High for the model, medium for each worked position.

### Toulon to the West Indies, 30 March to 11 June

- **Toulon, 30 March.** Villeneuve weighed at about 4 p.m. on 30 March 1805 and was a league south-east of Cap Sépet by 7 p.m. (Reille and the Atlas's log, Desbrière 1907, pp. 7, 9; his letter of 9 germinal). Nelson's frigates Active and Phoebe saw him at 8 a.m. on the 31st, ten leagues south of Cape Sicié, steering SSW (Nicolas VI 396–398). Nelson dated the sailing "in the night of Saturday, March 30th"; the "31st" of two other letters is the sighting. "29 March" is James's slip (III 339), copied by Clowes. The scene uses 30 March. High.
- **His force.** 11 of the line, four 80s and seven 74s (Bucentaure, Formidable, Neptune, Indomptable; Pluton, Mont-Blanc, Berwick, Atlas, Swiftsure, Scipion, Intrépide), six frigates and the brigs Furet and Naïade (Reille's "Pléiade" is a misreading), with about 3,070 troops under Lauriston (Desbrière 1907, pp. 6–7, 27). The British frigates counted seven frigates; James has 3,500 troops. High for the ships, medium for the troops.
- **Inside the Balearics.** He meant to pass east of the Balearics, but on 1 April a merchant ship, Ragusan by James and Corbett, told him Nelson was south of Sardinia, and he went between the islands and Spain. He lay becalmed off Cartagena on the morning of 7 April; Salcedo's six Spanish ships there did not join. A fresh easterly took him past Cape de Gata that night (Desbrière 1907, p. 8; Corbett 1910, pp. 56, 61). High.
- **The Strait, 9 April.** The Rock and Ape's Hill were in sight in the morning, and at about 9 a.m. the fleet entered the Strait in two columns, frigates ahead (Desbrière 1907, p. 9). Every British document written at the time gives the 9th: Orde's two letters of 10 April, Gardner's of 22 April, the Admiralty's order of 25 April and the Beagle's sighting off Cape Spartel; so do the American consuls at Cádiz and Lisbon. "8 April" goes back to a Ragusan's report that reached Nelson on 18 April (Nicolas VI 408) and runs through Nicolas's chronology, James, Clowes, Mahan and Adkin. The scene uses 9 April. High.
- **Orde.** Sir John Orde lay off Cádiz with five of the line: Glory 98, Defence 74 and the 64s Polyphemus, Agamemnon and Ruby. Strachan's Renown, which had seen the French at daybreak off Tarifa, joined him about midday with the warning, and the six drew off towards Lagos; the French frigates signalled them about 4 p.m. (Corbett 1910, pp. 62–63; Desbrière 1907, p. 17 n.). High.
- **Cádiz, 9–10 April.** The French anchored outside the bay at nightfall on 9 April, about 7.45–8 p.m., and were under sail again by about 2 a.m. on the 10th (Desbrière 1907, pp. 9–11; Corbett 1910, p. 68). Out to them came the French Aigle 74, the corvette Torche and the brig Argus, and Gravina with Argonauta 80, San Rafael 80, Firme 74, Terrible 74, América 64, España 64 and the frigate Santa Magdalena, with 1,930 Spanish troops (Decrès's return; James: 1,600). The scene anchors the French in open water about 3 nm west of the entrance shoals, an estimate. High for the times, low for the position.
- **Fourteen crossed together.** The San Rafael grounded coming out. At dawn on 10 April only Argonauta was with the 12 French, and América rejoined on the 12th, so 14 crossed in company. Firme, Terrible and España, with the Magdalena, crossed on their own and were found off Fort-de-France on 14 May; San Rafael came in on the 16th (Reille, in Desbrière 1907, pp. 11, 26; Troude III 342; `research/scene-02/a-h1ac.md`, GLYPH-crossing). The notes' "17 sailed together" is wrong. High.
- **The crossing.** South along the Moroccan coast to Cape Cantin, west from about 15 April, north of the Canaries and close to the Salvages, then south-west; westerlies forced a run SSW from about 26 April to 2 May, then west along 18–14½° N (Desbrière 1907, p. 25; a Spanish log captured later, Nicolas VI 468). Formidable, Intrépide and above all Atlas held the fleet back. At 1 p.m. on 13 May a frigate signalled land seven leagues to the west, 36 leagues before the reckoning expected it (Desbrière 1907, p. 26 n.). High for the route, medium for the daily positions.
- **Martinique, 14 May.** At daybreak the fleet entered the St Lucia Channel and during the day anchored off Fort-de-France (Fort Royal), fired on in passing by the British post on Diamond Rock: 17 of the line that day, 18 when San Rafael came in on the 16th (Villeneuve's report, Desbrière 1907, pp. 25–26). James's 13 May is his slip; Clowes gives both. High.
- **At Fort-de-France.** The Didon brought Napoleon's orders of 29 April: take British islands, wait 35 days after Magon's arrival, then return to Ferrol. She arrived on 29 May (Mahan), 30 May (Reille), 31 May (Corbett) or 1 June (James). Diamond Rock was attacked from 31 May; its garrison, 107 (James) or 128 (Desbrière), surrendered on 2 June (Corbett: 3 June). On 4 June Rear-Admiral Magon joined with Algésiras and Achille and 861 soldiers (James: 840), making 20 of the line, 14 French and 6 Spanish (Desbrière 1907, p. 36). High, except the Didon's date.
- **North through the islands.** The fleet sailed at 5 a.m. on 5 June (James: the 4th), passed under the lee of Dominica on the 6th, embarked troops off Basse-Terre, Guadeloupe, that afternoon and spent the night of the 7th under the lee of Antigua (Desbrière 1907, pp. 40–41). At 10 a.m. on 8 June, to leeward of Barbuda, it sighted a convoy of 15 sail from St John's and after a five-hour chase took 14. The escort escaped: the schooner Netley under Lieutenant William Carr by Corbett (p. 166), the Barbadoes and Netley under Harward by James (III 351). High.
- **The turn, 10 June.** The prisoners said Nelson was at Barbados with 12 or 14 of the line. By 10 June the borrowed troops were in four frigates for Guadeloupe and the fleet stood NNE for the Azores and Ferrol (Corbett 1910, p. 167; Desbrière 1907, pp. 41–43). Villeneuve's letter is dated at sea on 11 June, and Desbrière once has him sailing that day; James has "the 9th or 10th". The scene puts "10 June" at the turn, about 19°14′ N 61°44′ W on Corbett's chart. Medium.

### Nelson's pursuit

- **Where he was.** After showing himself off Barcelona, Nelson anchored in the Gulf of Palmas, south-west Sardinia, at 4.40 p.m. on 26 March (the log's "March 27th [i.e. 26th]", Nicolas VI 374; James and Nicolas's chronology give the log date, 27 March). He weighed at 6.15 p.m. on 30 March, about two hours after Villeneuve, anchored in Pula Roads at 5.30 p.m. on the 31st and sailed again at 5.30 a.m. on 3 April (Nicolas VI 390, 392). High.
- **The news.** The Phoebe rejoined at about 10 a.m. on 4 April, some six leagues WSW of Toro (Nicolas VI 393). Nelson had 11 of the line, four frigates and two corvettes (Nicolas VI 398 n.); the eleven names, his ten later West Indies ships and the Royal Sovereign, are inferred. High for the count, medium for the names.
- **East first.** Fearing for Sicily, Naples or Egypt, he waited 48 hours between Sardinia and Galita (4–6 April), bore up for Palermo from the south end of Sardinia on the 7th, and was off Palermo on 9–10 April. He turned west on the 9th by his letter of 18 April, or the 10th by one dated off Palermo that day (Nicolas VI 401–408); Corbett's arrival at Ustica on the 7th is wrong (`research/scene-02/a-h1b.md`, S3-PALERMO). High; medium for the 9th or 10th.
- **Against the wind.** Beating back took nine days to regain Sardinia. Off Toro on 18 April he learned the French had passed the Strait, and on the 19th he was still ten leagues west of Toro with the wind dead against him (Corbett 1910, pp. 93–95). He sighted the Rock on 30 April, watered at Tetuan from 10.30 a.m. on 4 May and lay in Gibraltar Bay from 2 to 6 p.m. on 6 May (James: the 7th, a log date). There, by his own later complaint, Rear-Admiral Donald Campbell of the Portuguese navy came aboard as Nelson "passed the Gut" and told him where the enemy had gone (Nicolas VI 431 n.); Mahan and Desbrière put the visit at Lagos. High; medium for Campbell.
- **Lagos and the bear-away.** He anchored in Lagos Bay at 9 p.m. on 9 May to clear Orde's storeships, weighed at 9.10 a.m. on 11 May (Corbett's move off the cape on the 10th is not in the log), met Rear-Admiral Knight with Craig's troop convoy at 4 p.m., gave him the Royal Sovereign, and at 6.50 p.m. bore up for the West Indies with Cape St Vincent NW by N 7 leagues, about 36°47′ N 8°41′ W (Nicolas VI 433; `research/scene-02/a-h1b.md`, S3-11MAY). The sloop Martin parted ahead that afternoon. High for the times, medium for the position.
- **The squadron.** Ten of the line: Victory, Canopus, Superb, Spencer, Donegal, Tigre, Leviathan, Belleisle, Conqueror and Swiftsure, with the frigates Decade, Amphion and Amazon (Nicolas VI 432 n.; Corbett 1910, p. 108). James and Clowes print nine names. High.
- **Barbados, 4 June.** He was 38 leagues from Madeira on 14 May and saw it on the 15th. Barbados was in sight at 6 a.m. on 4 June, and the squadron anchored in Carlisle Bay at 4.50 p.m. (Nicolas VI 436, 445; Desbrière's "4.30" is an OCR error). He had run 3,227 miles from Cape St Vincent and gained about ten days on Villeneuve (Nicolas VI 471; Corbett 1910, p. 160). No source gives a date other than 4 June. Cochrane's Northumberland and the Spartiate made 12 of the line; Dacres had kept four of Cochrane's ships at Jamaica (James, Clowes) or five of his six (Corbett). High; medium for Cochrane's ships.
- **Trinidad.** On a report from St Lucia that 28 sail had passed Gros Islet on the night of 28 May, Nelson embarked about 2,000 troops and sailed south at 9.30 a.m. on 5 June. Off Tobago at 6.10 p.m. on the 6th a schooner made, by chance, the signal for the enemy at Trinidad. On the 7th he went through the Bocas into the Gulf of Paria ready for battle and found nothing; at daylight on the 8th he learned the enemy had been at Martinique on the 4th (Nicolas VI 446–451). High.
- **Antigua.** Off Grenada at noon on 9 June he heard the enemy had passed Dominica on the 6th, steering north. He was off Guadeloupe and Montserrat on the 11th, anchored in St John's Road, Antigua, at 7 p.m. on the 12th, sent the brig Curieux home with dispatches, and sailed for Europe at 11.50 a.m. on 13 June with 11 of the line, the Northumberland staying (Nicolas VI 451–456). High.
- **Homeward.** Thinking the allies bound for Cádiz or Toulon, he kept south of their track and passed through the Azores north of São Miguel on 8–9 July (log, Nicolas VI 468; Mahan), not south of the islands as the notes had it. His frigates left on the way: Decade and Martin on 19 June, Amazon on 13 July, Amphion on a day not recorded. He made Cape St Vincent on 17 July and anchored in Rosia Bay, Gibraltar, at 7.30 a.m. on 19 July; ashore on the 20th, he noted it was his first time since June 1803 (Nicolas VI 471–475). High.
- **North.** He watered at Tetuan on 22–23 July. Off Tarifa at about 4 a.m. on 25 July the sloop Termagant brought a Lisbon paper with the Curieux's news, and he went north at once against northerly winds; his diary has 39°1′ N 16° W on 3 August and 41°8′ N 18°14′ W on the 7th (Desbrière 1907, pp. 74–75). On 15 August he joined Cornwallis off Ushant (24 of the line in sight at noon) and left him nine ships, then anchored with Victory and Superb at Spithead at 8.30 a.m. on 18 August and struck his flag on the 19th (Nicolas VII 4–8). High.

### Cape Finisterre, 22 and 23 July

- **Villeneuve's run home.** From the turn the fleet ran NNE to about 33–35° N (21–24 June), then ENE, north of the whole Azores group (Reille, in Desbrière 1907, p. 57). The American schooner Sally saw "about 22" large ships at 7 p.m. on 15 June near her noon position, 27°28′ N 60°58′ W (Nicolas VI 463; her log has the nautical "16th"). High for the route, medium for the positions.
- **The Curieux.** Bettesworth, carrying Nelson's dispatches, saw the allies on 19 June, by his report as far north as 33°12′ N, in 58° W (Corbett 1910, p. 181; James III 354), but Corbett's own chart, after Desbrière's, marks the sighting near 30°13′ N 58°06′ W. He reached Plymouth on 7 July, and Barham at once sent Stirling's Rochefort squadron to join Calder off Ferrol and both to cruise 30–40 leagues west of Finisterre. High for the date, low for the position.
- **Corvo to Finisterre.** Five frigates rejoined about 20 leagues NE of Corvo on 30 June (James); Reille has Corvo sighted on 2 July. On 3 July the fleet took the Liverpool privateer Mars and her prize, the Spanish galleon Matilda. A north-east gale on 9 July cost the Indomptable her main topmast; on the 12th the fleet was within about 300 miles of the coast west of Oporto, was then driven back west for four days, and from the 19th ran east, in fog on the 21st and 22nd (Corbett 1910, p. 195; Desbrière 1907, p. 57). Villeneuve wrote of twenty days against north-easterly winds. High for the events, medium for the 30 June and 2 July positions.
- **Calder's squadron.** After Stirling's five joined on 15 July, Vice-Admiral Sir Robert Calder had 15 of the line, four 98s, one 80, eight 74s and two 64s, with the frigates Egyptienne and Sirius, the lugger Nile and the cutter Frisk (James III 356–357; Calder's dispatch). High.
- **Villeneuve's fleet.** 20 of the line, 14 French and 6 Spanish, with 7 frigates, 2 brigs and the Matilda (James III 357; Troude III 352). Calder reported 20 of the line, three large ships armed en flûte, five frigates and three brigs. High.
- **Fog.** On the morning of 22 July the allies steered ESE for Ferrol in three columns, the Spaniards on the right, in thick fog and a light WNW breeze. It lifted a little about noon, and the Defiance reported 24, then 27 sail to the SSW (Corbett 1910, p. 196; James: SW). High.
- **Where.** There are three British noon positions: 43°30′ N 11°17′ W in Calder's dispatch (the primary one), 43°54′ N 11°38′ W in James, and 44°17′ N 11°52′ W at 11.30 a.m. in Desbrière. The allies were about 16 miles SSW of the British. The French reckoning (13°53′ W) ran about 2° too far west, and Corbett's chart (12°20′ W) and his figure of 300 miles from Ferrol are also too far west. The fight lay within about 43°20′–43°55′ N, 11°15′–12°00′ W, 95–130 nm WNW of Cape Finisterre; the scene puts its smoke at 43°35′ N 11°40′ W, 113 nm from the cape (`research/scene-02/a-h1ac.md`, H1-26). Medium.
- **The action.** At about 5.15 p.m. the Hero tacked and the Argonauta opened fire (Desbrière: about 5). The lines fought on the starboard tack heading about WSW, in fog and smoke so thick that "no ship could see much beyond her own length" (James III 361). The firing stopped at about 7.30 in the dark (Desbrière), at nine (Calder at his trial), or at 9.30, an hour after the signal to discontinue (James). High for the start, medium for the end.
- **Two prizes.** The San Rafael 80 (84 in the Gazette) and the Firme 74 drifted dismasted into the British line and struck: soon after 8 p.m. by the British account, at daybreak on the 23rd by the French (James III 361; Desbrière 1907, p. 64). Their captains were Montes and Villavicencio. High for the ships, medium for the hour.
- **Losses.** British 41 killed and 158 wounded, ship by ship in Calder's return; James's 39 and 159 is his own count. Allied: 155 killed and 341 wounded in Corbett's table (p. 202), 476 killed and wounded in James, about 580 with Desbrière's footnote for the prizes. A caption can say "about 500". High for the British, medium for the allies.
- **23 and 24 July.** At dawn the fleets were about 17 miles apart. Calder drew together round the crippled Windsor Castle and his prizes and stood NE; Villeneuve, now in the frigate Hortense, bore down between noon and 3 p.m., then hauled his wind three or four leagues off. On the 24th the wind went round to the north and Calder, now to windward, did not attack; they lost sight of each other that evening (James III 363–365) or the next morning (Corbett 1910, p. 205). High.
- **Calder's fate.** At his own request he was tried in Portsmouth harbour on 23–26 December 1805 and cleared of cowardice and disaffection, but for an error in judgment in not renewing the action he was "severely reprimanded" (Naval Chronicle XV 174–175). Nelson let him go home in his own flagship, which parted on 14 October by the Victory's log, so he missed Trafalgar. High.

### Ferrol, the turn south and Cádiz

- **Vigo.** On 25 July the Didon sighted Cape Finisterre ESE. With the north-east wind rising and sickness spreading, Villeneuve chose Cádiz, then Vigo, tried for Ferrol again on the 26th, and on the 27th wrote from 40 leagues WSW of Finisterre. The fleet anchored in Vigo Bay at nightfall on 28 July (Desbrière 1907, pp. 67–69, 85; Mahan); the Cíes came in sight at 6 p.m. on the 27th or the 28th. James's "evening of the 26th" is two days early. High for 28 July, low for the Cíes.
- **To A Coruña.** Leaving the Atlas (with about 800 sick), España and América at Vigo, he sailed on 31 July (James: the 30th) with 15 of the line, 13 French and 2 Spanish, into a north-west gale. The wind went south-west on 1 August, blew Calder off Ferrol and let Villeneuve double Cape Finisterre that evening; he entered the bay on 2 August (Desbrière 1907, p. 86; Corbett 1910, p. 219). Napoleon's order of 16 July kept the French out of Ferrol: the 13 French anchored at A Coruña and the two Spaniards went on into Ferrol. James and Mahan have him in on 1 August; James ties it to Calder's being blown off that morning, so it is not simply his slip. The scene uses 2 August. Medium.
- **Twenty-nine of the line.** With Gourdon's five French and nine Spanish from Ferrol, the combined fleet had 29 of the line, 18 French and 11 Spanish, with 6 frigates and 4 corvettes: one three-decker, the Príncipe de Asturias (112 guns by Desbrière, 120 by Corbett), and six 80s; the San Julián stayed behind (Desbrière 1907, pp. 98–99; Corbett 1910, p. 239). The ships came out between 8 and 10 August to the outer bay off Ares, the last three joined before noon on 13 August, and by 2 p.m. all were under sail (Desbrière 1907, pp. 97–98; Villeneuve's letter, "25 thermidor (13 août)"). James has the fleet out at sea on the 11th, two days early. On the 10th Villeneuve had written that he would steer for Brest or Cádiz as circumstances allowed. High.
- **West-north-west.** The fleet ran west in a fresh north-easterly, stood NNE towards Cape Prior for some hours on sighting a British ship, and steered NW again from 2 a.m. on the 14th. The frigate Iris watched it, on a course W by N ½ N at 44°17′ N 9°26′ W, until about 2 p.m. (Corbett 1910, p. 259 n.). At sunset the Dragon saw 26 sail; a Danish merchantman she had boarded, told she belonged to a fleet of 25 of the line, passed the story to a French frigate (Corbett 1910, p. 262; James III 374). High.
- **The turn, 15 August.** Still standing WNW against the north-east gale, Villeneuve turned for Cádiz "à l'entrée de la nuit" on 15 August (his letter of 22 August, Desbrière 1907, p. 112 n.). Reille's noon put the fleet 40 leagues WNW of Finisterre, about 43°39′ N 11°48′ W; Villeneuve wrote 80, which Corbett shows to be impossible; Desbrière's staff map has it near 44°40′ N 12°25′ W; James implies the evening of the 14th. The scene bends the line south at about 43°45′ N 12°20′ W. High for the date, low for the position.
- **South.** The fleet ran south before the north-easterly, out of sight of land, and was off Cape St Vincent on 17 August (Desbrière's text) or 18 August (James; Desbrière's map), where it burned three merchantmen of a convoy whose escort, the brig Halcyon, escaped; from the 18th Blackwood's Euryalus shadowed it. At 10 a.m. on 20 August, with Cádiz NE about nine leagues, it sighted Collingwood's three ships right ahead, chased them towards the Strait (Magon led, says Desbrière; Gravina, says Corbett), bore up for the harbour at 3 p.m. and anchored in Cádiz road that evening (James III 375; Desbrière 1907, p. 107). Medium for St Vincent, high for Cádiz.
- **20 August.** The date is in James (from the ships' muster rolls), Desbrière, Corbett, Escaño's day-by-day relation and Collingwood's letter of the 21st, which says "yesterday". 21 August is in the Précis des événements militaires, which James rejects, and in Escaño's later letter to MacDonnell; Troude's 17 August is impossible, since the turn came only at nightfall on the 15th, some 450–500 nm away. The scene uses 20 August. High.
- **Collingwood.** He had the Dreadnought 98, Colossus 74 and Achille 74 with the frigate Endymion and the bomb Thunder, "only three poor things with a frigate and a bomb", as he wrote next day (Corbett 1910, p. 270). He drew off about 21 miles towards the Strait; the Mars rejoined from Tangier about midnight, and he was back off the port before daylight (James III 375; Desbrière: back the same evening, with four). High.
- **After.** 29 of the line came in; with the six Spaniards already in the harbour James counts 35, and Collingwood's "36 sail of men-of-war" counts frigates too. Bickerton's ships joined Collingwood from 22 August, and Calder came south from Cornwallis's fleet on 30 August, giving about 26 of the line (James III 379); Calder's ships included eight of the eleven Nelson had brought back from the West Indies (Desbrière 1907, p. 109 n.). Desbrière's total of 32 counts some ships twice (`research/scene-02/a-h1b.md`, S6-BRIT-CADIZ). Where the fleet lay in the bay is in the Cádiz part (`research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-36d). High for 29, medium for 35 and 26.

### Routes on the chart

- **How they are drawn.** The engine joins each route's waypoints with a centripetal Catmull–Rom curve in Mercator map space. Every waypoint, every straight segment and the drawn curve were checked against the chart's own coast (Natural Earth 10m land and minor islands): no line touches land, and outside harbours and rías every line keeps at least 2 nm off the coast. Stage A's line passes Cap Cépet at 2.2 nm, Punta Cires at about 2.5, Tarifa at 4.1, Cape Trafalgar at 4.9, Europa Point at 6.4, Cape Spartel at 10 and Las Puercas at 3.2. Elsewhere: Cape Finisterre 6.7, Cape St Vincent 8.7, São Miguel's north coast 3.3, Diamond Rock 2.6; Nelson's lines pass Tarifa at 2.3–2.8 nm and come within 2 nm of Europa Point and Punta Carnero only in the mouth of Gibraltar Bay. Natural Earth lacks the Cíes, Toro, the Hormigas, the Columbretes and Diamond Rock, so the lines were set against them by hand. Points that no source gives are marked "est." in the route data. High for the check.
- **Toulon to Cádiz (Stage A).** Table A of `research/scene-02/h1-campaign.md`, section 7, refined by `research/scene-02/v-h1c.md` (H1-35) and `research/scene-02/a-h1ac.md` (H1-35e); see the table below. The Atlas's noon latitudes are firm, her longitudes exist only for 1–3 and 10 April, and the other longitudes keep the line in the channels the sources name. Changes from the notes: the start is the middle of the Grande Rade (the notes' 43°06′ N 5°56′ E lies in the inner road on the chart's coast); row 5 lies in the open sea south of the Gulf of Lion and row 6 south-east, not SSE, of Barcelona; the Strait is passed at about 11 a.m., not noon; the 4 p.m. point is just past Cape Trafalgar, off Conil; an estimated point in the offing keeps the line about 3 nm off Cádiz's rocky shore; the anchorage is in open water about 3 nm west of Las Puercas, not the outer-bay berth of August to October; and three estimated points (east of Cabo de la Nao, off Garrucha, south of Málaga) are dropped, as the line keeps at least 3½ nm off those coasts without them. High for the shape, medium for the line, low for the 4–8 April longitudes.
- **The crossing.** Table B, the Atlas's 32 noon positions by reckoning (Desbrière 1907, p. 26 n.), drawn without day marks. Dropped as zigzags: 8 May (16°00′ N, 55 nm south of the line between 7 and 9 May) and 11 May (an impossible run of 5½°, about 330 nm, in a day). The last reckoning point lies about 1°50′ east of the truth, so the line runs on to the landfall, through the St Lucia Channel and up the lee coast to Fort-de-France. Medium.
- **Villeneuve's way home.** Table C (the places named, the track between them estimated), then table D: Corbett's chart after Desbrière, read to about 0.3°, with the Sally's position. The chart's daily marks stop on 24 June, and the line runs straight to Corvo (2 July). Left out: both 19 June positions of the Curieux, James's 30 June point NE of Corvo (it would double back), the doubtful noon of 23 July and the 40 leagues WSW of Finisterre of the 27 July letter. The Z of 9–20 July west of Portugal is kept because it is real, not reckoning noise; dropping 12 and 16 July gives a plain line. Medium; low from 25 July.
- **Vigo, A Coruña, and Ferrol to Cádiz.** Estimated courses round Finisterre and into the two bays; then table E, one British fix (the Iris, 14 August) and Reille's noon of the 15th, the rest estimated. The stand towards Cape Prior and the morning chase of 20 August are left out as hooks. The line enters Cádiz bay by the channel between Las Puercas and the El Diamante shoal, 0.2–0.4 nm from the buoys, to the outer-bay berth of `research/scene-02/a-h2c.md`, H2-36d. Low.
- **Nelson.** Tables F–J, as corrected in `research/scene-02/a-h1b.md`. In the Mediterranean the line starts at Pula (the Palmas–Pula–Toro moves would double back along Sardinia's south coast), loops to Palermo and back with two control points north of Capo San Vito (without them the curve crossed the cape), and is estimated on the beat west, when no positions were recorded. Across the Atlantic and back it follows the daily marks of Faden's chart of 1807, "copied from his Lordship's original MS." and redrawn by Corbett, which agree with seven log fixes within about 0.3°; north of the Azores the 8 July log fix replaces the chart's mark. Dropped as zigzags: 24 June (a doubling back, 70 nm west of the line), 4 and 6 July (wobbles of 14–28 nm in the calms), and 2, 4, 5 and 6 August (tacks against the northerlies, up to 30 nm either side of the line between the diary's positions). The Tetuan call of 22–25 July is left out. Medium.
