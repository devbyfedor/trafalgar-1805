# The invasion flotilla at Boulogne, summer 1805: the craft and how they lay

Research note for scene 2, beat 1 ("Boulogne from the sea"): an evening view from a boat anchored in Boulogne's roads in August 1805, looking at the shore. This note covers the flotilla's craft (what each type looked like, its size, rig, guns and colours) and how they lay in the harbour and the roads. It builds on `../history-scene-02-merged.md` ("Boulogne and the invasion army"), `../h2-boulogne-cadiz.md` (H2-04, H2-05) and `../a-h2a.md` (R14).

Confidence uses the scale at the top of `docs/history-notes.md`. Each fact says what it rests on: **period** (documents, memoirs, dictionaries, plans and images of 1803–1825), **scholarly** (later works built on the archives: Desbrière 1902, James, Clowes), **modern** (modern secondary works and web pages, used as leads), or **inference** (ours).

Units: French measures are converted at 1 pied du roi = 0.3248 m, 1 pouce = 2.7 cm, 1 toise = 1.95 m, 1 encablure (cable) = 120 brasses = 195 m, 1 lieue marine = 5.56 km. James's feet are French feet where he says so.

Status: complete (13 November 2026). Not yet independently checked.

## 1. For the painter

- **What lies in the roads: rigged gun craft, not barges.** Two-masted brigs (chaloupes canonnières, about 25 m) and two- or three-masted luggers (bateaux canonniers and péniches, 19.5 m), with now and then a three-masted ship-rigged prame (36 m). The 1805 lines had brigs and luggers in about equal numbers: 27 and 27 in July. High for the types; Medium for the mix.
- **A chaloupe canonnière, close up (100–300 m).** A low, flat, black hull 25 m long and 5.5 m wide, its side 1–1.5 m above the water; brig rig with yards crossed and sails furled, mastheads about 16–24 m up (estimate); heavy guns on slides at bow and stern and a mortar or howitzer besides, sweeps stowed; a tricolour at the stern and a long narrow pennant (about 11 m) at the masthead. High for hull and guns; Low for mast height.
- **A bateau canonnier.** 19.5 × 4.5 m, side under a metre plus an 18-inch (0.5 m) washboard pierced for 28 oars; a 24-pounder on a slide in the bow and a field gun on its wheels at the stern; two or three lug masts about 10–14 m high (estimate), yards lowered at anchor. High for the hull and guns; Medium for the rig; Low for heights.
- **A péniche.** A long, low open rowing boat, 19.5 × 3.25 m, gunwale about half a metre above the water, up to 40 oars, two or three lug masts about 9 m high, a howitzer or small gun; they lay at the ends of the line and between it and the beach, and at night as pickets a cable (200 m) out to sea. High.
- **The prame.** Like a small, very long and low corvette: flush deck, probably a row of six gun ports a side (inference), three masts with square yards, mastheads about 28–36 m up (estimate), the tallest thing on the water; five went out on 12 August 1805, and a line would carry them in pairs at its ends or one every twenty brigs. Medium for the look; Low for heights.
- **The line.** One line (a double one in strength) parallel to the beach, about 1–2 km out beyond the 450–800 m strand that dries at low tide, running from off the harbour mouth north-east toward Wimereux; craft evenly spaced, roughly every 45–55 m, all lying the same way to the tidal stream, so seen broadside from the sea. About 50 craft in 1805 (2.5–3 km of line); up to 150 over 6–8 km in 1804. Medium for the order; Low for distance and spacing.
- **August 1805 itself.** No craft were in the roads on 3–6 August (bad weather); on 12 August 5 prames, 30 chaloupes and 40 péniches went out and chased the British cruisers; other August days are not documented. A moored line on an August evening is a reconstruction of the line of July 1805. Medium for the events; Low for any given evening.
- **The harbour behind.** Over the jetties, a dense comb of bare masts and yards: about 1,100 craft at Boulogne in July 1805, moored side by side in tiers along the Liane and round the semicircular basin; from the roads a thicket of thin vertical lines in front of the town. High.
- **Colours.** Hulls black or very dark tarred brown, matt and weathered, no bright bands evidenced; sails off-white weathered to buff and grey, with some brown tanned sails on fishing craft; masts and yards brown wood. Bright paint only on show craft (green sides, white bottoms, gilding). Low-Medium.
- **Flags.** Every craft wears the tricolour (three equal vertical bands, blue at the staff) at the stern, up to the length of its beam (about 5.5 m on a brig, 8 m on a prame, less on small craft), and a long pennant at the masthead twice the beam or more; French flags were linen and hang heavily in a light air; with the sun still up they fly (colours came down at sunset). High for the flag; Medium-Low for sizes.
- **At anchor.** Sails furled on the yards, yards squared aloft on brigs and prames, lug yards lowered; boats alongside or rowing guard along the line from sunset; no riding lights are recorded, so at dusk the line goes dark but for a rare lantern. Medium for sails; Low for lights.
- **Motion in a gentle swell.** Flat, stiff, shallow craft ride the swell like corks: they rise and fall with it and tilt with the passing slope by a degree or two, the small péniches jerking more, the prames steadiest; mastheads sweep a few decimetres. Low (inference).
- **Apparent sizes.** At 1 km a prame's hull spans about 2° and its masts rise about 1.7°; a brig 1.45° and 1°; a lugger 1.1° and 0.6°; at 3 km a third of that, at 300 m about three times (table in 2.9). Computed.
- **Leave out.** Barges or rafts; Dutch schuyts and other Batavian craft (they lay at Ambleteuse until after 30 August); transports in the roads (they stayed in port); a single line of 2,000 craft from Étaples to Gris-Nez (Napoleon's flourish of 26 July). Medium-High.

## 2. Findings

### 2.1 Names, spellings and the classes at Boulogne

- The "espèce" names came into use at the end of 1803 (Desbrière III p. 546, which speaks of canonnières "or vessels of the 1st kind, as they were beginning to be called"). A period table of loaded draughts, reproduced by Desbrière facing p. 90, pairs the names exactly as above ("Une Prame ou Bateau de grande espèce", "Une Canonnière ou Bateau de 1re espèce", "Un Bateau canonnier ou de 2e espèce", "Une Péniche ou Bateau de 3e espèce"). Period. High.
- British usage: James calls the prame "prame, or corvette", the chaloupe canonnière "gun-vessel, or canonniere" or a brig, and the péniche "peniche" or "a heavily-armed launch, or peniche" (James III pp. 216–217, 233). The Naval Chronicle's translation of Lacrosse's report of 1 October 1805 renders the craft of the line as "Gun-boats" and the British boats as "Pinnaces" (Naval Chronicle XIV p. 339). Scholarly and period. High.
- **Forfait.** Pierre-Alexandre-Laurent Forfait, naval engineer and former minister of marine, was made inspector general of the "flottille nationale" on 24 May 1803, in charge of its building, purchases, classing and movements (arrêté of 4 prairial an XI, Desbrière III p. 82). The caïques were built to his plan (Willaumez p. 123), and the 60-pied bateau canonnier "modèle an XII" is attributed to him (ANCRE monograph). No drawing of his for the prame or the chaloupe canonnière was found. Period and modern. High for his role.
- Spelling: prame is feminine in French ("la prame"); péniche takes an acute accent; "chaloupe canonnière" is written without a hyphen in Desbrière's documents; plural "bateaux canonniers". The modern French "péniche" (a river barge) is a later, different sense. High.
- How many of each at Boulogne. On 30 March 1804 the port held 2 prames, 61 craft of the 1st kind, 158 of the 2nd kind, 118 péniches (plus 3 for the First Consul, 1 for the admiral and 1 for the general-in-chief), 9 packet boats, caïques (one for the First Consul) and 2 bomb galliots, besides 474 transports (Desbrière III p. 558, a return in the Archives nationales). On 20 July 1805 Boulogne held 578 prames and gun craft and 526 transports (James III p. 325). The decree of 30 August 1805 kept 6 prames, 72 chaloupes canonnières and 72 péniches armed in Boulogne (Corr. XI No. 9164). Period and scholarly. High for each date; the mix in August 1805 is not given type by type.

### 2.2 Prame

- **Design.** 110 × 25 × 8 pieds (35.7 × 8.1 × 2.6 m), "gréement de corvette" (ship rig), three keels; 12 × 24-pounders; 38 seamen and 120 soldiers (Desbrière III p. 90, from the construction records of the Ministry of Marine). James agrees: strongly built, about 110 French feet in extreme length and 25 in breadth, drawing 7–8 feet, ship-rigged, 12 long 24-pounders, 38 sailors and upwards of 100 soldiers; the first 20 were ordered with stalls for 50 horses (James III pp. 216–217). The period draught table gives a loaded prame 8½ pieds (2.8 m) (plate facing Desbrière III p. 90, my reading of its scale to ±¼ foot). Period and scholarly. High for the design.
- **Origin.** On 5 July 1803 the First Consul ordered 50 boats on the model of the one then at Dunkirk under Lieutenant Jornin, the Foudroyant, each to carry 12 × 24-pounders and 60 horses with their riders, and fitted with a leeboard on each side (Decrès to Forfait, Desbrière III pp. 87–89). By 1804 there were 18 prames, 16 of them new (p. 90). Period. High.
- **Hull.** Flat-floored ("à plates varangues", Willaumez's definition of a prame) with three keels instead of one (design table), so it could sit on the mud of the harbour at low water. No plan of the 1803 prame was found. The only prame model checked, the "Foudroyante" in the Musée national de la Marine (17 MG 18, Trianon collection, made about 1813–1818), is catalogued by the museum as a late-18th-century 20-gun artillery prame, not the 1803 design; it shows a long, low, flush-decked ship hull with one row of ports in a high bulwark, a plain stem and a square stern. It can serve only as a type reference. Period definition; model is a lead. Low for the details of the 1803 hull.
- **Guns.** Twelve heavy guns on a single deck. Their placement is not documented in what was read; a battery of six a side is the natural reading of a 12-gun ship-rigged hull and of the British prints of 1811 ("praams carrying each 12 guns, 24 p."). Inference. Medium-Low.
- **Under way.** Slow: in July 1805 "the slow sailing of the prames" held back Ver Huell's division (James III p. 319). James judged the British 20-gun ship Ariadne "not above a third larger" than a prame (same page). Scholarly. Medium.
- **Names met in the sources.** Ville-de-Mayence (Lacrosse's flagship, at the centre of the line on 1–2 October 1804; James III pp. 232–233), Ville-de-Lille (at Dunkirk in 1803; Desbrière III p. 223), Ville-d'Aix, Ville-d'Anvers and Ville-de-Genève (with Ver Huell's division, July 1805; James III pp. 318–319), Ville-de-Lyon (taken off Boulogne in 1811). High.
- **At Boulogne in 1805.** Five prames that could not enter on 21 February came in at the end of March (Desbrière IV p. 395). Five went out with the flotilla on 12 August 1805 (Dumas XII p. 76; see 2.10). The decree of 30 August kept six armed in the port and placed them in the semicircular basin, "le bassin circulaire" (Corr. XI No. 9164). Period. High for the decree; medium for the 12 August sortie (a French account only).

### 2.3 Chaloupe canonnière (gun-brig)

- **Design.** 76–80 × 17 × 5–6 pieds (24.7–26.0 × 5.5 × 1.6–1.9 m), brig rig; 3 × 24-pounders and a howitzer or mortar of 6–8 inches; 22 seamen and 130 soldiers (Desbrière III p. 90). The period draught table distinguishes the canonnière "of new construction", drawing 5½ pieds (1.8 m) loaded, from the canonnière "of old construction", drawing 8 pieds (2.6 m) (plate facing p. 90). The old ones were the deeper gun-brigs of the 1801 flotilla, which James describes as vessels of 200–250 tons with four to eight heavy long guns (James III p. 67). Period. High for the design.
- **Rig.** Two masts with square sails, a brig. James: the first-class gun-vessels were "rigged as brigs" (III p. 217). The 1804 British watercolour of the French line (E. D. Lewis, RMG PAI6989) and Hue's painting of 1806 show two-masted square-rigged craft with a gaff sail on the mainmast. Willaumez's dictionary illustrates "une canonnière de première espèce gréée en brigantin" (*Dictionnaire de marine*, 1825, p. 123, pl. B fig. 10); his own definition of the brigantin is a lighter brig, with shorter yards on the mainmast than on the foremast, no royals, and a gaff mainsail that is always lowered instead of a square main course (s.v. Brigantin, p. 106), which matches the craft in the 1804 watercolour. In July 1803 the First Consul asked that the chaloupes still to be built have a simpler rig, avoiding "the complication of a square-rigged masting" (Desbrière III p. 88). So brig is the norm, with some simplified or brigantine rigs. Period. Medium-High.
- **Guns and their places.** The three 24-pounders and the howitzer or mortar (design); "three long 24-pounders and an 8-inch mortar" (James III p. 217; and the four canonnières of Hamelin's division in June 1805, Desbrière IV p. 402). The small chaloupes already built in 1803 were to carry four 36-pounder carronades on the sides and three 18- or 12-pounders fore and aft (Desbrière III p. 88). Napoleon reckoned that four chaloupes could fire only eight guns at an enemy (Corr. No. 8925, 20 June 1805, in Desbrière IV p. 401), which fits heavy guns on slides firing over the bow and stern rather than a broadside. Period. Medium for the placement (inference from the figures).
- **Oars and leeboards.** Leeboards were ordered for all chaloupes in July 1803 (Desbrière III p. 88). French Wikipedia gives 12 pairs of oars, citing modern works not checked here. Willaumez says Swedish and Russian gun-sloops, "superior to those of France in every respect", rowed 24 oars (p. 123). The British watched "Gun-boats, which carry a number of oars" (Naval Chronicle XIV p. 482). Period; the number of oars is modern and unchecked. Medium for oars being used, Low for their number.
- **Crew in the roads.** When the craft lay in the roads in April 1804, the garrisons were raised to 50 men (officers included) in each chaloupe and 40 in each bateau canonnier (Soult, 15 April 1804, Desbrière III p. 559). Napoleon put six canonnières at no more than 450 men (Desbrière IV p. 401). Period. High.

### 2.4 Bateau canonnier (gunboat)

- **Design.** 60 × 14 × 4½ pieds (19.5 × 4.5 × 1.5 m), lugger rig; one 24-pounder and a howitzer or a field gun; 6 seamen, 100 soldiers and 2 horses (Desbrière III p. 90). Loaded draught on the period table: 3½ pieds (1.1 m), the same as a péniche (plate facing p. 90). Period. High for the design.
- **Guns.** A 24-pounder forward and a field piece aft: "one 24-pounder forward, and a field-piece abaft" (James III p. 217). The plan approved on 23 fructidor an XI (10 September 1803) put the field guns at the stern on their own field carriages, where they would serve to defend the boat; 40 boats were to carry a bronze 12-pounder field gun and 100 a light bronze 24 instead of the navy's iron 24 (Desbrière III pp. 100–101). A modern monograph on Forfait's 60-foot model of an XII (ANCRE, by S. Muffat, P. Grandvilliers and D. Désormière, drawn from plans in the Service historique de la Défense, the Boulogne library and the Crucy yard archives) gives the 24-pounder on a slide in the bow and a Gribeauval field gun at the stern, to be landed with the troops. Period and modern. High.
- **Rig.** Lug sails ("gréement de lougre", Desbrière III p. 90). James: the second class were "some rigged as schooners, and some as luggers" (III p. 217). The number of masts is not in the documents read; period views (Hue 1806, Baugean about 1804–1805) show many two- and three-masted luggers among the flotilla. The modern monograph calls the boat under-canvassed, a poor sailer that made much leeway. Period and modern. Medium for lug rig on two or three masts.
- **Hull and freeboard.** A flat-bottomed boat ("bateau plat"). In July 1803 the First Consul had the side raised 18 inches (49 cm) by a removable washboard pierced for the oars, on the 100 boats already built at Dunkirk and on all new ones, and leeboards fitted (Desbrière III p. 88). The monograph confirms both changes. With 3½–4½ feet of draught the deck lies low, a little under a metre above the water at the side, with the washboard above it (inference from the draught and the 18-inch washboard). Period, with inference. Medium.
- **Oars.** Napoleon's own pencil note on a minute of 1803: a bateau canonnier has 28 oars, needing 56 men (Desbrière III p. 101). French Wikipedia gives 14 pairs. Period. High.

### 2.5 Péniche

- **Design.** 60 × 10 × 3½ pieds (19.5 × 3.25 × 1.1 m), lugger rig; a 6-inch howitzer and a 4-pounder, or a Prussian howitzer, or an 8-inch mortar; 5 seamen and 66 soldiers (Desbrière III p. 90). Period. High.
- **Willaumez (1825), a naval officer of the period.** The Boulogne péniches were heavy and carvel-built ("à franc bord"). Those of the first kind were 60 pieds from stem to sternpost (19.5 m), 53 pieds 8 pouces on the keel, 10 pieds in beam and 5 in depth, and carried forty oars and forty swivel guns or espingoles; those of the second kind were 42 × 8½ pieds and 3 deep (13.6 × 2.8 × 1.0 m), with 28 oars; others built as coastguards were 50 × 10 × 5 pieds, with a 12-pounder and 12 espingoles, lug-rigged and double-banked (*Dictionnaire de marine*, p. 447). Period. High.
- **Shape and rig, from the period drawings.** The plate facing Desbrière III p. 90 ("Bateaux de 3e espèce", from the Archives de la Guerre) shows péniches as long, low, open boats with an almost straight sheer, a raked stem and a near-vertical stern, under lug sails on two masts or on three (with the mizzen sheeted to an outrigger) and a jib on a bowsprit; its detail drawing shows an open hull with about fifteen thwarts and a round-bilged, fairly flat-floored midship section. In the drawing the mainmast head stands about half the boat's length above the water. Period. Medium-High.
- **Oars.** Napoleon's 1803 note gives a péniche 64 men at the oars (Desbrière III p. 101); Willaumez gives 40 oars. Period. High that they were primarily rowing boats.
- **Role in the roads.** Péniches lay at the ends of the line and between it and the shore, and at night some were posted a cable ahead as pickets; péniches "à obusiers prussiens" (with long-range Prussian howitzers) supported the line (ordre du jour of 16 April 1804, Desbrière III p. 560; Desbrière IV p. 397). Period. High.

### 2.6 Other craft: caïques, bomb vessels, corvettes, transports, the Batavian flotilla

- **Caïques.** Launches like those of ships of the line, each with a 24-pounder (Desbrière III p. 90). Willaumez: caïques were oak-built gun-launches on Forfait's plan, 43 pieds 3 pouces long, 10 pieds 6 pouces in beam, 4 pieds deep and drawing 2 pieds 11 pouces (14.0 × 3.4 × 1.3 × 0.95 m), with a 36-pounder gun aft and a 36-pounder carronade forward, both on slides, and a short deck of about 5 pieds at each end (p. 123). The Desbrière plate shows one with a sloop-like rig (a gaff mainsail, square yards and a jib) and one with two lug sails. 41 were built (p. 90). Period. High.
- **Bomb craft.** The line of 23 March 1804 had 6 "bombardes" and 2 "galiotes à bombes"; on 15 April 1804 the 120 craft in the roads included 9 bombardes (Desbrière III pp. 556, 559). In 1805 the sorties included 3 bombardes and a sloop-bombarde (9 March) and 3 "bateaux bombardes" (15 June) (Desbrière IV pp. 395, 397). The bomb craft kept a short distance behind (inshore of) the line (Desbrière III p. 560). Their rigs are not described in what was read. Period. High for presence, no data on looks.
- **Corvettes canonnières.** Foudre and Audacieuse, each with 10 guns (four or six long 18-pounders and brass 36-pounder carronades) and over 80 men, called brigs by the British; they reached Boulogne with Hamelin's division at the end of July 1805 (James III pp. 316, 324–325; Desbrière IV pp. 401–404). Scholarly. High.
- **Transports.** Fishing and merchant craft bought or requisitioned: in 1803 the plan was for 1,000 chasse-marées and fishing boats (Desbrière III p. 89). The period draught table lists, for the transport flotilla, a Blankenberge boat (2¾ pieds loaded), a chasse-marée (6½), a schooner (7), a smack and a sloop fitted as horse stables (8 and 8½), a brig of 80 tons (9) and a Dieppe boat (9½). At Boulogne in March 1804 the 474 transports included 190 for cavalry, 45 for artillery, 76 for infantry and 125 Newfoundland fishing ships. Saint-Haouen's plan of 3 August 1805 sent the transports into the roads only on the eve of a general sailing, so on an ordinary day they lay in the harbour. Period. High for the types; medium that none lay in the roads.
- **The Batavian flotilla.** Ver Huell's craft (Dutch schooners and armed schuyts of about 75 tons with three guns each, chiefly long 24-pounders, plus French prames) were based at Ambleteuse in the summer of 1805, with part at Calais (James III pp. 315–316, 318–321, 325–326; Desbrière IV pp. 397–398). They were brought into Boulogne only by the decree of 30 August 1805, after the army had left. In Boulogne's roads in August 1805 they would not normally be seen. Period and scholarly. Medium-High.
- **Show craft.** On 26 August 1804 a British cutter fought "a heavy gun-brig and two lugger-rigged yachts, painted with white bottoms and green sides, and richly gilt" (James III p. 230). The return of March 1804 lists péniches for the First Consul, the admiral and the general-in-chief and a caïque for the First Consul (Desbrière III p. 558). Such painted and gilt craft were the exception. Scholarly. Medium.

### 2.7 Colours: hulls, sails, spars

- **No document found.** None of the period documents read (Desbrière's two volumes of orders and returns, Napoleon's Correspondance for 1804–1805, James, Dumas, Willaumez, the Naval Chronicle for 1805) says how the flotilla's craft were painted. Searches for French and English descriptions ("peints en noir", "goudronnés", "painted black") found nothing. Everything below rests on period images and inference.
- **What the period images show.**
  - E. D. Lewis's watercolour of 1804, drawn from the British bomb vessel Tartarus (RMG PAI6989): the French line as a row of two-masted craft with dark grey to black hulls and no visible bands, each with a tricolour at the stern and a long red pennant at the masthead. A naive but first-hand drawing. Period.
  - Hue's oil painting of Napoleon visiting the camp in July 1804 (Salon of 1806, Versailles): the flotilla's hulls dark brown to black; the lug sails cream to off-white, one lugger's sails darker and browner; masts and yards brown wood. Period, by a marine painter, but painted in the studio.
  - Pocock's watercolour, after an officer's sketch of July 1804: the harbour craft as a dense comb of brown-grey bare masts; the hulls hidden behind the jetty. Period.
  - Baugean's engraving of the flotilla going out to its lines (about 1804–1805) is monochrome. Period.
  - The Trianon model of the Foudroyante (2.2) is built in ebony and boxwood, so its black and pale bands are the woods of a fine model, not evidence of paint.
- **Inference for the painter.** Hulls black or very dark tarred brown above the water, matt and weathered after one or two years in a tidal harbour; no bright bands are evidenced. A pale or ochre strake along the gun ports of the prames, as on contemporary French warships, is possible but unsupported. Below the waterline the craft lay on the mud twice a day, so whatever showed when they rolled would be dark and fouled. The imperial show craft (green sides, white bottoms, gilding) were exceptions. Low for the details; Medium that the general effect was dark hulls.
- **Sails.** Navy canvas of hemp, one to two years old by August 1805: off-white weathered to buff and grey, darker where wetted and patched. Requisitioned fishing craft from the Channel ports may have had tanned, red-brown sails, which would fit the darker sail in Hue's picture. Inference from period images. Low-Medium.
- **Flags.** Willaumez notes that French ensigns and pennants were made of linen ("toile"), where foreign navies used bunting (p. 444): a slightly heavier, duller cloth that hangs more limply in a light air. Period (1825). Medium-Low.

### 2.8 Flags, pennants and numbers

- **Ensign.** The French naval ensign in 1805 was the tricolour of three equal vertical bands, blue at the staff, white, red (fixed by the decree of 27 pluviôse an II, 15 February 1794). On the craft at anchor in the 1804 watercolour it flies from a staff at the stern; under sail it would go to the peak of the gaff (Willaumez: the large stern ensign is hoisted at the gaff of every vessel that carries a fore-and-aft "brigantine" sail, p. 443). High for the flag; Medium for where it flew on luggers.
- **Ensign size.** Willaumez's rule: the large stern ensign is as long (fly) as the vessel's beam and two-thirds of the beam deep (hoist); the medium ensign is two-thirds of that (pp. 443–444). That gives up to about 8 × 5.4 m for a prame, 5.5 × 3.7 m for a chaloupe canonnière, 3.3 × 2.2 m for a péniche, and two-thirds of these for medium ensigns. Small craft probably wore the smaller sizes (inference). Period rule (1825). Medium-Low.
- **Masthead pennant.** A warship flies at the head of the mainmast a long, narrow pennant in the colours of its stern ensign, ending in a point (Willaumez p. 291); pennants are at least twice the beam in length and one metre wide at the hoist (p. 444): about 16 m for a prame, 11 m for a chaloupe canonnière, 6–7 m for a péniche. The 1804 watercolour shows such long pennants on every craft of the French line. The order of the three colours along the 1805 pennant was not found in the sources read. Period. Medium for the pennant, Low for its colour arrangement.
- **Command marks.** Division commanders flew a split pennant, the "guidon", half the beam long and split to half its length (Willaumez pp. 444, and s.v. Guidon); the plate facing Desbrière III p. 90 shows two flotilla flags labelled "Amiral" (a broad swallow-tailed flag) and "Ordinaire" (a long pennant). Rear-Admiral Lacrosse flew his flag in the prame Ville-de-Mayence in the centre of the line in October 1804 (James III p. 232). Period. Medium.
- **Signals.** When the general signalled to dress the line, the craft at its head, centre and rear hoisted a distinguishing flag at the foremast; captains were forbidden private signals and used only the flotilla's signal book (ordre du jour of 16 April 1804, Desbrière III p. 560). Period. High.
- **Numbers.** The craft were numbered: bateau of the 1st kind No. 1 (March 1804; Desbrière III p. 557), péniche No. 267 and craft Nos. 142 and 149 of the second line (October 1804; James III p. 233), chaloupe No. 275 (May 1805; Desbrière IV p. 396), gun-boats Nos. 34, 61, 62, 138, 280, 291, 305 and 306 in the line of September 1805 (Lacrosse's report, Naval Chronicle XIV p. 339). Where the numbers were painted (bow, stern or sail) was not found. Period. High for the numbers, unknown for their display.

How the mast heights were estimated (all Low; inference):

- **Prame.** Rule of thumb of the period for square-rigged ships (Steel's, close to French practice): mainmast about half the sum of length and beam, here (35.7 + 8.1) / 2 ≈ 22 m; topmast about 0.6 of the mainmast (13 m); topgallant about half the topmast (6.5 m). Taking off the part of the mainmast below deck (about 3 m) and the overlaps at the mastheads (about 2.4 m and 1.6 m), and adding about 1 m from waterline to deck, the main truck comes to about 36 m. Prames were notoriously slow and may well have been under-rigged, so the range is set at 28–36 m. Checks: in Hue's painting the ship-rigged craft inside the jetty carries its mastheads about as high above the water as its hull is long (about 36 m); the late-18th-century prame model in the Musée de la Marine shows the same proportion; James judged the 20-gun Ariadne "not above a third larger" than a prame.
- **Chaloupe canonnière.** The same rule gives a mainmast of about 15 m and a main truck of about 24–25 m with a topgallant; without one, or with the "simplified" rig asked for in 1803, about 16–20 m. Range 16–24 m.
- **Péniche.** Measured on the Desbrière plate: the mainmast head of the three-masted péniche stands about 0.47 of the hull's length above the water, so about 9 m for a 19.5 m boat; the lug yards peak a little higher.
- **Bateau canonnier.** Between the péniche and the chaloupe: a beamier, heavier 19.5 m boat, "under-canvassed" according to the modern monograph; 10–14 m.

### 2.10 How they lay: harbour, basin and roads

**In the harbour.**

- Boulogne's harbour was the estuary of the Liane, nearly dry at low water; for the flotilla it was given quays on both banks, a large dug basin and a dam ("barrage") that held the water in to keep the craft afloat (James III p. 217). Before the works even craft as shallow as the flotilla's lay aground almost all the time in most of the port, and the entrance could be used only for a short time around high water (Desbrière III p. 141). Period and scholarly. High.
- A plan dated Thermidor an XIII (July–August 1805), "Dispositions d'appareillage de la flottille impériale du port de Boulogne", shows how the craft lay for a general sailing: moored side by side in tiers of five to ten, bows to the quay, by escadrille (each escadrille coloured on the plan); péniches in rows round the semicircular basin, canonnières along both banks of the channel, prames and bomb craft by the basin entrance, transports and horse transports farther up the river. It accompanied Saint-Haouen's report of 3 August 1805 (printed by Desbrière IV pp. 398–400, who reproduces the plan as a folding plate there; a colour reproduction is on Wikimedia Commons). Period. High for the arrangement in port.
- Seen from outside, this mass of craft appeared as a "forest of masts" in the harbour (the British correspondent of July 1804, Naval Chronicle XIV p. 481). Pocock's watercolour, Hue's painting and Baugean's engraving all show it as a dense comb of bare masts above the jetties and the waterfront. Period. High.
- After the army left, the decree of 30 August 1805 kept 6 prames, 72 chaloupes canonnières and 72 péniches armed in the port, ready to go out whenever the weather allowed, placed the rest afloat above the dam and the prames in the semicircular basin (Corr. XI No. 9164; also No. 9137 of 26 August). Period. High.

**In the roads: the "ligne d'embossage".**

- **What it was.** A line of gun craft moored in the open roads outside the pier, under the guns of the shore batteries and of the forts built on the sands, as an advance guard and a screen against bombardment (James III pp. 217, 229; Desbrière III pp. 140–141). Divisions took turns in it and relieved one another (Dumas XI p. 49). Because the port emptied and filled only around high water, never more craft could safely be kept in the roads than could be brought back on one tide (Desbrière III p. 545); by 1805 about 100 per tide, falling as the channel silted (Desbrière IV p. 398). Period and scholarly. High.
- **How many, 1804.** 65 on 23 March (15 craft of the 1st kind, 42 of the 2nd, 6 bomb craft, 2 bomb galliots); 120 on 15 April (2 prames, 30 chaloupes, 9 bomb craft, the rest bateaux, caïques and péniches); more than 150 before Boulogne and Wimereux on 16 April (Desbrière III pp. 556, 559–560). On 25 August 1804, 146: 62 of the 1st kind, 42 of the 2nd, 6 bomb boats and 36 péniches (Dumas XI p. 45; James III p. 229 has 146, 62 of them brigs, the rest chiefly luggers). At the start of October about 150 in a double line outside the pier, the admiral's prame in the centre (James III pp. 232–233). Period and scholarly. High.
- **How many, 1805.** Much smaller (Desbrière IV pp. 395–398, from the daily reports of Lafond, the flotilla's chief of staff): 64 craft on 9–15 March; 25 on 26–30 March; 55 on 9–14 April; no line from mid-April to 21 May, only péniches going out for a few hours to practise rowing and landing; 54 on 21–23 May and again for fourteen days on 26 May–10 June; a strong line on 13–15 June (27 chaloupes, 21 bateaux, a brig, 3 bomb boats, two divisions of péniches with Prussian howitzers, 2 caïques and armed boats); 47 bateaux and chaloupes on 21–25 June, reinforced at times by a division of péniches; 54 on 1–2 July; 54 again on 10–21 July (27 canonnières and 27 bateaux), which on 17 July moved up between Ambleteuse and Audresselles with 36 péniches and armed boats to cover the Batavian flotilla. Period. High.
- **August 1805.** When Napoleon arrived on 3 August the weather had turned bad and there was not one craft in the roads; this is certain until 6 August; after that the flotilla's reports have gaps, and neither they nor the generals' reports mention a line (Desbrière IV p. 398). On 12 August, however, Lacrosse sent out at about noon a division of 5 prames, 30 chaloupes canonnières and 40 péniches with long-range howitzers under Captain Moras, which stood out more than two leagues at the British (a ship of the line, two frigates and some thirty corvettes and brigs) and chased them nearly three leagues; by evening more than sixty British warships were in sight (Dumas XII pp. 76–77). Napoleon wrote on 13 August that he had had the flotilla attack the British cruisers and chase them three and a half leagues out (Corr. XI Nos. 9069, 9074), and to Villeneuve that "we harass them every day with our prames and flotillas" (No. 9073). His letter to Villeneuve of 26 July had claimed 2,000 craft forming "a single ligne d'embossage" in all the roads from Étaples to Cap Gris-Nez (No. 9022), which Desbrière's figures show to be a flourish. Period. High that the flotilla was out on 12 August; Low for a moored line on any other August evening.
- **Later in 1805.** At the end of September a line of 27 gun-boats under Captain Dordelin lay in the roads, its head to the east (gun-boat No. 62), facing 23 British vessels; British fire-ships attacked it at night without effect (Lacrosse's report of 1 October, Naval Chronicle XIV p. 339). James mentions attempts on "the line of gun-vessels at anchor in the road" in September and November (III p. 333). On 13 September Napoleon still spoke of having to "re-establish the ligne d'embossage" one day (Corr. XI No. 9209). Period. High.
- **Where.** The roads (the "rade de Saint-Jean") have 5–15 m of water over marl and mud of good holding, lie completely open (the Bassure de Baas bank offshore gives no shelter from the swell), and are fronted by a wide foreshore that dries at low water: 700–800 m wide at the head of the bay and still 450 m at La Crèche, which kept the line well away from the land and from the batteries' best protection (Desbrière III p. 141, from his reconstruction of the port and roads). Saint-Haouen describes the bottom as excellent, rising gently toward the land (Desbrière IV p. 399). In 1801 a division lay 500 "fathoms" (the translator's word, probably toises: about 0.9–1 km) off the harbour entrance (French report, Naval Chronicle XIV pp. 400–401). In July 1804 the line of gun-brigs and luggers stretched "four or five miles" under the forts, and the British anchored about three miles from it (Naval Chronicle XIV p. 481); Dumas puts the British a league and a half off, on a parallel line (XI p. 45). In 1805, Dumas says, the lines spread and pushed out beyond the reach of the batteries (XII p. 39). Period and scholarly. Medium for these figures; the exact distance from the shore is not stated anywhere read.
- **Inference for the scene.** A single line, or a double line in strength, parallel to the shore, about 1–2 km off the beach and beyond the low-water line, running from off the harbour mouth north-eastward toward Wimereux. Its head was the north-east end (gun-boat No. 62, "head of the Line to the East", 1805) and its "left" the south-west end (Dumas XI p. 49 has the British taking station to the south-west, "to windward of the last vessel on the left of the line"); for a camera looking east at the shore, the head is on the left. Medium-Low.
- **Spacing and order.** The admiral required equal distances along the line, so that no craft could foul another, and kept the line dressed on bearings, with the head, centre and rear craft as guides (ordre du jour of 16 April 1804, Desbrière III p. 560). Saint-Haouen's plan put prames and chaloupes canonnières in the front (seaward) line because they rode out a gale best, two prames side by side at each end and the others one every twenty canonnières, the bateaux canonniers in a third line with the péniches between, each escadrille's transports anchoring behind its canonnières; prames and canonnières each had four cables, moored with two anchors (Desbrière IV pp. 399–400). Over four or five miles (6–8 km) a line of about 150 craft (1804) gives one craft every 45–55 m, about two lengths of a chaloupe; the 1804 watercolour shows one to one and a half hull-lengths of open water between hulls (inference). Period, with inference. Medium for the order, Low for the spacing.
- **Pickets and boats.** With the line formed, the bomb craft kept a short distance behind it, péniches as pickets ("péniches de vedette") one cable (195 m) ahead, the other péniches between the land and the ends of the line, and from sunset to sunrise the moored craft's boats rowed continually along the line (ordre du jour of 16 April 1804, Desbrière III p. 560). After the fire-ship attack of October 1804 booms ("barrages", "une chaîne de barrage") were laid in front of the line (Dumas XI p. 49; James III p. 234). Armed boats went out by day and came back before night, "as was their habit" (June 1805, Desbrière IV p. 397). Period. High.
- **In and out with the tide.** Sorties were made on the morning or evening high water ("à la marée du matin", "à la marée du soir"); a line once formed stayed out for one day to two weeks and came in when a westerly to north-easterly blow threatened, since the flat craft could not ride out a gale in the open roads (Desbrière III pp. 557, 559–560; IV pp. 396–399). In winter 1803–1804 the roads could be held one day in eighteen (Desbrière III p. 565). Period. High.

### 2.11 At anchor: sails, yards, colours, lights

- **Sails and yards.** In the 1804 watercolour the craft of the French line ride with yards crossed and sails furled on them; in Hue's painting the craft in the harbour show bare masts and yards. The line was a fighting position from which divisions weighed and fought within minutes (25–26 August 1804, Dumas XI pp. 45–48), so sails were bent and furled, not unbent, and yards were left aloft and squared (inference from the images and from the practice). On luggers the lug yards were usually lowered at anchor (general practice; inference). Sails loosed to dry after rain would be furled again by evening. Period images and inference. Medium.
- **Colours.** The ensign flew by day. Hauling it down at sunset was the long-standing custom of both navies, but no French regulation of 1805 saying so was found; in the scene, with the sun still up, the ensigns fly. The masthead pennant marked a commissioned warship and stayed up. Inference. Medium-Low for the sunset rule.
- **Lights.** No riding lights are mentioned in any source read. Facing a British squadron at night, the line relied on rowing guard boats and pickets, and the French reports describe night attacks seen only by gunfire and explosions (Naval Chronicle XIV p. 339; James III pp. 233–234). Lanterns were used for night signals. For the dusk part: the line almost dark, with at most a lantern or two on the commanders' craft and occasional signal lights; the brighter lights would be in the town and the camps. Inference. Low.

### 2.12 Riding at anchor in a gentle swell

- **Heading.** The tidal stream along this coast is strong: Desbrière gives the flood setting north-north-east toward England at up to 4 knots (III p. 141). Craft at anchor in such a stream lie tide-rode, bows into it, parallel to the coast; at slack water, or when the breeze is stronger than the stream, they swing toward the wind (general seamanship; inference). Moored with two anchors, the prames and canonnières swung little. So from seaward the craft of the line would most often be seen broadside, all heading the same way: north-north-east on the ebb and south-south-west on the flood (each bow points into the stream). Inference. Medium.
- **Sea state.** The French report of 25 August 1804 gives exactly the conditions of a good day in the roads: "the weather was fine and the sea slightly swelling" ("la mer peu houleuse", Dumas XI p. 45). Period. High as a condition the line kept.
- **How they moved.** These were shallow, flat-floored, beamy craft, very stiff for their size; their natural roll period is short, roughly 3 s for a bateau canonnier or péniche, 4 s for a chaloupe and 5 s for a prame (estimate from T ≈ 2π·k / √(g·GM), with a radius of gyration of about 0.38 of the beam and an assumed metacentric height of 1–1.5 m). A gentle swell of 6–10 s is much longer than that, so they do not resonate: they ride up and down with the swell and tilt with the slope of the passing wave, a degree or two each way for a swell half a metre high, and their mastheads sweep slow arcs of a few decimetres; the small péniches jerk more, the prames least. Period sources only speak of the bad side: the flat craft "cannot defend themselves from the sea" (Saint-Haouen, Desbrière IV p. 399), and the British saw them suffer "more from the sea ... than from our ships" when the wind came onshore (Naval Chronicle XIV p. 481). Inference for the motion. Low.

## 3. Doubts and disagreements

1. **The roads in August 1805.** This is the biggest doubt. Desbrière, reading the flotilla's daily reports, finds no craft in the roads from 3 to 6 August and no mention of a line afterwards (IV p. 398). Dumas describes a sortie of 75 craft on 12 August (XII pp. 76–77), and Napoleon's letters of 13 August speak of attacking the British cruisers and of harassing them "every day" (Corr. XI Nos. 9069, 9073, 9074). Neither James nor the Naval Chronicle for 1805 mentions the 12 August action, and Dumas's "more than sixty" British sail is a French count. A moored line on a given August evening is therefore a reconstruction, best modelled on the documented line of 10–21 July 1805 (54 craft). Napoleon's claim of 26 July that 2,000 craft formed one line from Étaples to Cap Gris-Nez (No. 9022) is contradicted by every return and should not be painted.
2. **Rig of the chaloupes canonnières.** Brig (design table, James, the 1804 and 1806 images) or brigantine (Willaumez's plate); in July 1803 the First Consul asked for a simpler rig on those still to be built (Desbrière III p. 88). Which craft at Boulogne in 1805 had which rig is not known.
3. **Rig of the bateaux canonniers and péniches.** Lugger in the design table; James has some second-class gun-vessels as schooners and the péniches "rigged chiefly as schuyts", which may reflect the Batavian schuyts he saw rather than the French péniches. The number of masts on a bateau canonnier is not documented; two and three appear in the period views.
4. **Dimensions and crews.** Péniche: 3½ pieds is the draught (Desbrière's table) and 5 pieds the depth (Willaumez); the second kind was 42 pieds long. Oars: 40 (Willaumez), 64 rowers (Napoleon's note of 1803), 13 pairs (French Wikipedia, unchecked). Prame crew: 38 seamen and 120 soldiers (Desbrière), 38 and "upwards of 100" (James), 31 and 44 with 200 passengers or 60 horses (French Wikipedia, unchecked). Bateau canonnier draught: 4½ pieds in the design, 3½ loaded in the period table. Chaloupe draught: 5–6 pieds designed, 5½ loaded for the new type, 8 for the old type of 1801. Each figure is right for its source; the table in 2.9 uses the design and the loaded table.
5. **Colours.** Not documented. Dark hulls rest on three period pictures; ochre or natural-wood bands, painted numbers and sail colours are guesses.
6. **Mast heights.** Estimates by proportion (2.9), not measurements. A sail plan of a Boulogne craft would replace them: the ANCRE monograph on the bateau canonnier (with masting and sail plates), Potel's École polytechnique plans in the Bibliothèque numérique de la Défense (which refused connection), and the archive plans offered by The Model Shipwright (references GM11PL0555 and 0556) were not seen.
7. **Distance and spacing of the line.** No source read gives the line's distance from the shore or the spacing in metres. The figures in 2.10 rest on the width of the foreshore, the 1801 precedent of 500 toises, the length of the 1804 line and the 1804 watercolour. "Lieue" in the French texts may be the sea league (5.56 km) or a land league (about 4 km).
8. **The Pocock view.** Wikipedia captions it "as recorded from HMS Euryalus in 1805" and Commons dates the episode to October 1804; the Naval Chronicle prints the correspondent's letter dated off Boulogne, 31 July 1804, and the plate list credits the sketch to an officer of the Euryalus; Pocock signed the watercolour in 1805. The view is of the summer of 1804.
9. **The Foudroyante model.** French Wikipedia captions the Musée de la Marine's "Foudroyante" (17 MG 18) as the prame "Ville de Lille (ex-La Foudroyante)"; the museum's own entry, as quoted in a list of the Trianon models, calls it a 20-gun artillery prame of the late 18th century. It is not the 1803 prame and is used here only as a type reference.
10. **Flags.** The arrangement of the colours on the 1805 masthead pennant, and the size of ensign actually worn by small craft, were not found. Willaumez's rules date from 1825, under the white Bourbon flag; only the shapes and sizes are carried over.
11. **Lights and the sunset rule.** No French regulation of 1805 on hauling down colours or on riding lights at anchor was found; 2.11 rests on general practice.
